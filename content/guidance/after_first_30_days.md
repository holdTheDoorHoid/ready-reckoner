Recovery takes weeks. These steps come in rough order.

**Going home.** Go back only when local officials say it is safe. Do not go inside if you smell gas or the building looks damaged.[^ready_gov_recovering] Run a generator only outside, more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents.[^cdc_co_basics]

**Insurance and records.** Before you clean up, take photos and videos and make a list of the damage.[^ready_gov_recovering] Keep every receipt.[^fema_home_inspections] Call your insurer as soon as you can, and an adjuster will come to inspect. Check the adjuster's estimate, and talk to them before you sign a repair contract.[^floodsmart_start_claim]

**Help from FEMA.** If a federal disaster declaration covers your county, apply at DisasterAssistance.gov or call 1-800-621-3362.[^disasterassistance_gov] FEMA help does not replace insurance.[^fema_ihp]

**Scams.** FEMA never charges to apply or to inspect.[^fema_disaster_fraud] Its inspectors carry an official photo ID and never ask for your bank details; a FEMA shirt is not an ID.[^fema_home_inspections] Never sign your insurance check over to a contractor, and walk away from anyone who wants cash up front.[^ftc_scams_after_disasters]

**Medicine.** In a declared disaster, Medicare drug plans can help you find a pharmacy and replace lost medicine.[^medicare_drugs_disaster] Many states let a pharmacist give an emergency supply when your prescriber cannot be reached, and the rules differ by state.[^healthcare_ready_refill_laws] Healthcare Ready's Rx Open map shows which pharmacies are open in a disaster area.[^healthcare_ready_rx_open]

**Papers.** To replace lost IDs, start with your birth certificate, from the vital records office of the state where you were born.[^usa_gov_replace_ids]
