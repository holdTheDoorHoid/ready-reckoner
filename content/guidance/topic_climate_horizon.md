The climate setting shows your risks as they are today, or as projected for around 2050, meaning the years 2036 to 2065.[^rr_research_risk_model] The projections come from county climate data published by federal agencies.[^cmra_2025] They are projections, not predictions, so the plan shows them as ranges.

**What changes.** The contiguous U.S. has warmed about 60% faster than the planet as a whole since 1970, and temperatures are projected to keep rising.[^nca5_climate_trends] Days with the heaviest rain have become more common, by about 60% in the Northeast and 45% in the Midwest.[^nca5_climate_trends] So the 2050 setting raises the chance of heat and heavy-rain flooding where the data supports it, and can lower the chance of cold snaps and icy days.[^rr_research_risk_model] It changes how often hazards happen, not how long repairs take.[^rr_research_risk_model]

**What stays the same.** Earthquakes, tsunamis and volcanoes are not adjusted, and neither are personal risks like job loss or a house fire. Hurricane frequency is not changed county by county, because the science does not support that.[^rr_research_risk_model]

**What to avoid.** Do not read a 2050 number as a forecast for next year. Plan your supplies for today, and use the 2050 view for long-lived choices, such as where to live or how to build.
