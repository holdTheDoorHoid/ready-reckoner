{frequency} Heat and cold become dangerous indoors when the power fails or the heating or air conditioning stops. Some people need extra care: babies and young children, people over 65, pregnant women, and people with heart disease or asthma.[^cdc_heat_health] In the 1995 Chicago heat wave, people who lived alone or did not leave home each day were more likely to die. Working air conditioning and social contact protected people.[^semenza_1996_heat_deaths]

**What helps.** Agree who checks on whom, especially older neighbours and anyone who lives alone.[^cdc_heat_health][^cdc_winter_safety] In heat, go where there is air conditioning. Dial 2-1-1 to find one.[^cdc_heat_health] Cover windows with drapes or shades.[^ready_gov_heat] In cold, close off rooms you don't need, stuff towels under doors, and cover windows with blankets at night. Have extra blankets, sleeping bags and warm coats.[^cdc_winter_safety] Babies under one year old should never sleep in a cold room.[^cdc_winter_safety] If you cannot keep your home warm, go to a warming center. Dial 2-1-1 to find one.[^ready_gov_stay_safe_warm] If you heat with wood, have the stove and chimney professionally inspected and cleaned every year, because creosote from the smoke builds up and can start a fire.[^epa_burnwise_faq]

**What to avoid.** Fans move air but do not lower body temperature.[^ready_gov_heat] Above 90°F indoors, a fan can make you hotter.[^cdc_heat_health] Never heat your home with a gas oven, and never use a grill or camp stove indoors.[^cdc_co_basics] Headache, dizziness, nausea or confusion can mean carbon monoxide poisoning: get outside, then call 911.[^cpsc_co_information_center] Keep space heaters 3 feet from anything that can burn.[^usfa_heating_fires] Never leave people or pets in a closed car on a warm day.[^ready_gov_heat]
