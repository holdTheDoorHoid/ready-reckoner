These pointers are for the rare event that lasts for months. They are places to start, not a manual.

**Rain.** Each state sets its own rules on collecting rain, and the rules vary widely, so check yours.[^pnnl_2015_rainwater] Rainwater is not safe to drink until germs and chemicals are removed, but it can water plants you do not eat.[^cdc_rainwater_collection]

**Wells.** A well pump stops when the power goes out. Have a licensed electrician connect any generator.[^vdh_storm_wells] A hand pump fitted to the well can still draw water in a long outage; test and treat that water before drinking it.[^wsc_wellcare_help_2025]

**Fuel.** Stored fuel does not keep forever, so use it and replace it. Following the International Fire Code, one Utah fire department limits gasoline at home to 25 gallons in approved containers, with no more than 10 in an attached garage and none in a basement. Ask your local fire department for your rules.[^lehi_fuel_storage]

**Toilets.** After a strong earthquake, toilets may not flush for weeks or months. A twin-bucket toilet keeps pee and poo apart, and handling poo safely stops diseases like cholera.[^rdpo_emergency_toilet]

**Food.** Badly home-canned food can cause botulism. Vegetables, meat and fish need a pressure canner.[^cdc_botulism_home_canning] Use tested recipes, such as those from the National Center for Home Food Preservation.[^nchfp_home] Your state or county Extension service can help with growing and preserving food.[^cdc_botulism_home_canning]

**Spirits.** Keep regular sleep and wake times, take breaks from the news, and talk with people you trust.[^cdc_managing_stress] Keep books, games and puzzles for children.[^ready_gov_kit]
