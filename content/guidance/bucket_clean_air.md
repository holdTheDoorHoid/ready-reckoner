{frequency} Wildfire smoke, blowing dust and chemical spills can make the air unhealthy, and smoke gets into homes too.[^epa_wildfire_indoor_air] The answer is the same kind of room each time: one you can close off from the air outside.[^epa_clean_room][^ready_gov_chemical]

**What helps.** Pick a clean room big enough for everyone, such as a bedroom with its own bathroom. Close its windows and doors, and run a portable air cleaner sized for the room.[^epa_clean_room] If you cannot get one, a box fan with a MERV 13 filter attached is a low-cost stand-in.[^epa_diy_air_cleaners][^epa_clean_room] If your heating and cooling system pulls in outside air, close the intake or set it to recirculate.[^epa_wildfire_indoor_air] Keep N95 or P100 respirators with two straps for anyone who has to go outside. They work only when they seal over the nose and under the chin.[^epa_protect_lungs_2026] Respirators do not come in sizes for young children, so for them the clean room is the protection.[^epa_protect_lungs_2026][^epa_children_wildfire_smoke] Check the air quality on AirNow or your local news.[^epa_wildfire_indoor_air]

**What to avoid.** Do not use an air cleaner that makes ozone.[^epa_clean_room] While the air is bad, avoid frying, candles, smoking and vacuuming without a HEPA filter, and skip hard exercise.[^epa_wildfire_indoor_air] Masks with ear loops or one strap do not seal, so they will not protect your lungs.[^epa_protect_lungs_2026] If you have heart or lung disease or are pregnant, ask your doctor before wearing a respirator.[^epa_protect_lungs_2026]
