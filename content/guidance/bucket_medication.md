{frequency} In a disaster, pharmacies and roads can close, and pharmacy computer systems can go down.[^rr_research_risk_model] Anyone who takes a daily medicine needs a way to keep taking it.

**What helps.** Refill early, while you still have a week left. The Red Cross suggests keeping a 7-day supply, and Florida advises at least two weeks.[^redcross_survival_kit][^florida_dem_medication] Ask your doctor, pharmacist and drug plan how to build an emergency supply, such as a 90-day fill.[^ready_gov_disability][^medicare_drugs_disaster] Keep a written list of each person's medicines, doses and allergies.[^ready_gov_disability] Many states let pharmacists give an emergency refill, but the rules differ.[^healthcare_ready_refill_laws] After a declared disaster, a federal program can help people without insurance replace prescriptions.[^hhs_aspr_epap] People with diabetes should pack supplies for at least 1 to 2 weeks: syringes or pen needles, a glucose meter with test strips and spare batteries, and glucose tablets.[^cdc_diabetes_emergencies][^aace_diabetes_emergency] Insulin in its original vial or pen, opened or not, keeps working for up to 28 days at 59°F to 86°F. In an outage, keep it below 86°F in the shade or a cooler, never frozen.[^fda_insulin_emergency][^cdc_insulin_emergency] For other medicines that need a fridge, ask your pharmacist now how long each keeps out of it, and write the answer on your medicine list.[^ready_gov_power_outages]

**What to avoid.** Do not throw insulin away after a day without power. If it gets hotter than 86°F and you have nothing else, use it, and replace it as soon as you can.[^fda_insulin_emergency] Never use insulin that has frozen or looks unusual, with particles, clumps, crystals or a changed color.[^fda_insulin_emergency][^ada_insulin_storage] Do not use expired medicines. Weak antibiotics can fail to treat an infection.[^fda_expired_medicines] Do not share antibiotics or save them for later.[^cdc_antibiotic_use]
