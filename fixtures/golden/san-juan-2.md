### Leaving home

Warning can be as short as no warning at all (an earthquake) or as long as 3 days. For chemical spills and releases, plan for as little as a few minutes of warning; for tsunamis, 15 minutes. Plan to be away for about 5 days. Most evacuations last a few days, but after a home fire you could be away for 2 months or more.[7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15]

### Staying in touch

**Four ways to reach each other.** Emergency planners call this a PACE plan: a primary way, an alternate, a contingency and an emergency way. Each one takes over when the one before it fails.[16] For a household it can be:

1. Primary: call.
2. Alternate: text. A text may get through when a call does not, and it saves battery.[17]
3. Contingency: everyone texts one out-of-state contact, who passes the news along.[17]
4. Emergency: go to the place you agreed to meet if you get separated.[17]

## Your risks

### What to know and do

#### 1. Medical emergency

Nearly every household like yours will have someone need emergency care in the next ten years.[18]

**Includes:** far from emergency care; falls.

**What helps.** Take a first-aid and bleeding-control class. You may be able to save a life by acting right after an injury, before help arrives.[19] If a teen or adult collapses and their heart stops, call 911 and push hard and fast in the center of the chest. This hands-only CPR needs no training. Note where the nearest heart defibrillator (AED) is; it talks you through each step.[20] Keep a stocked first-aid kit. Check it regularly and replace anything used or out of date.[21] Keep a list of each person's medicines, doses and allergies with your emergency supplies.[22] Know how to reach 911. Call if you can, and text where that service works.[23]

**What to avoid.** Do not start antibiotics on your own or take someone else's. The wrong medicine can delay the right treatment and cause serious side effects.[24] Do not use expired medicines.[25]

**How bad:** Serious (based on data).

#### 2. Hurricane

Of 100 households like yours, about 95 (88–98) will lose power or have damage from a hurricane or tropical storm in the next ten years.[10, 11, 12, 13, 14]

**Includes:** inland flooding from a tropical storm.

**What helps.** Know your evacuation zone, your routes and where you would stay. If you live in a mandatory evacuation zone and officials tell you to leave, go right away.[4] If you are not told to leave, shelter from the wind in a small inside room, closet or hallway away from windows, on the lowest floor that will not flood.[4, 26] Clear drains and gutters, bring in outdoor furniture, and think about storm shutters. Keep phones charged and have backup chargers.[4] A flood policy typically takes up to 30 days to start, so buy it well before a storm.[27] If you would leave in an electric car, keep it fully charged all hurricane season.[28]

**What to avoid.** Never walk or drive through floodwater. One foot of moving water can sweep a car away.[27] Do not drive or charge an electric car that has been in floodwater. Salt water can start a battery fire.[28] Never run a generator inside your home or garage.[29]

**How bad:** Serious (mostly data).

#### 3. Burst pipe or water leak

Of 100 households like yours, about 14 will have a burst pipe or leak flood part of the home in the next ten years.[30]

**Includes:** sewer backup.

**What helps.** Find your main water shut-off valve now, and show everyone how to turn it off. A burst pipe can flood a room quickly.[31, 32] Once a year, check the hoses to the water heater, washing machine, dishwasher and fridge, and replace any that crack or leak. A leak alarm can warn you early.[31] In freezing weather, let a faucet drip at night, open the cabinet doors under sinks on outside walls, and keep the heat at 55°F or higher.[32] Home and renters policies usually cover burst pipes. Many do not cover a sewer backup unless you add that cover, and floods need their own policy.[31]

**What to avoid.** Never thaw a frozen pipe with an open flame; use a hair dryer. Watch for electric shock around standing water.[32] Do not leave home while the washing machine or dishwasher is running.[31]

**How bad:** Severe (mostly data).

#### 4. Earthquake

Of 100 households like yours, about 9 will feel an earthquake strong enough to knock things off shelves in the next ten years.[33]

**Includes:** liquefaction; fire after an earthquake.

**What helps.** Practice Drop, Cover and Hold On: drop to your hands and knees, cover your head and neck, and hold on until the shaking stops.[17] Secure bookcases, water heaters, TVs and other heavy things, and keep heavy objects on low shelves. A standard homeowner's policy does not cover earthquake damage.[17] Store water in case the taps stop. Near a volcano, leave early if you are told to. Ash can travel hundreds of miles, so keep N95 masks.[34]

**What to avoid.** Do not run outside during shaking, and stay away from doorways.[17] Avoid driving in heavy ash.[34]

**How bad:** Severe (mostly data).

#### 5. House fire

Of 100 households like yours, about 5 (3–8) will have a house fire, in their home or next door in the next ten years.[14, 15]

**Includes:** carbon monoxide poisoning; lithium battery fire.

**What helps.** Put smoke alarms inside and outside every bedroom and on every level, and test them each month.[35] Practice your escape plan twice a year, including finding your way out in the dark.[36] Sleep with bedroom doors closed. Teach children not to hide from firefighters.[36] If there is smoke, crawl low under it to your exit.[36] If you cannot get out, close the door, cover vents and cracks with cloth or tape, call 911, and signal from the window with a light-colored cloth or a flashlight.[36] Insurance and copies of your documents help you recover afterward.[37]

**What to avoid.** Before you open a door, feel the door and the knob. If either is hot, or smoke is coming around it, keep it closed and use your second way out.[36] If a person or pet is still inside, get out and tell firefighters right away.[36] If your clothes catch fire, stop, drop and roll.[36]

**How bad:** Severe (mostly data).

Of 100 households like yours, about 5 (2–11) will have ash or mudflows from a volcano reach them in the next ten years.[10, 14]

**What helps.** The steps under "Earthquake" above apply here too.

#### 7. Flooding from rivers or heavy rain

Of 100 households like yours, about 2 (1–7) will be cut off or lose building services in a flood in the next ten years.[9, 10, 14]

**Includes:** flash flood; basement or street flooding in heavy rain.

**What helps.** Look up your address on FEMA's flood maps, and sign up for your community's warnings.[27] Homeowner's insurance does not cover flooding, and a flood policy typically takes up to 30 days to start.[27] Renters can buy flood coverage for their belongings.[38] Know how you would get to higher ground or a higher floor.[27] A wildfire makes landslides more likely on a burned slope for several years. Near a burn scar, watch the forecast and listen for rushing water or a rumbling that grows louder.[39]

**What to avoid.** Six inches of moving water can knock you down, and one foot can sweep your car away.[27] Never drive around barriers on a flooded road. The road may have collapsed under the water.[40] Stay off bridges over fast-moving water.[27] Leave if you are told to, or if you feel unsafe near a slope.[39]

**How bad:** Severe (mostly data).

### No grid power at home: about 3 months (3 weeks to 6 months)

**What helps.** A light for each person, a radio and phone power are in your plan; keeping food cold and charging up are on the forecast list; registries are under Local help.

**What to avoid.** Never run a generator inside your home or garage, even with the doors and windows open. Keep it outside, more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents.[29] Put a carbon monoxide alarm on every level of your home.[82] Headache, dizziness, nausea or confusion can mean carbon monoxide poisoning. Get everyone outside to fresh air, then call 911.[83] Never heat your home with a gas stove or oven. Unplug electronics, because the power can come back with a surge that damages them.[82] Treat every downed power line as live: stay at least 30 feet from it and anything it touches, and call 911.[84] If one falls on your car, stay inside unless the car is on fire.[85]

The worst power cut in the territory's records was Hurricane Maria (2017); being ready for 3 months would have left some homes that lost power still waiting (about 80 in 100 of all customers there were still out).[10, 11, 13, 14, 86, 87, 88]

### Tap water must be treated: about 1½ months (1–3)

**What helps.** Use bottled water, or bring clear water to a rolling boil for one minute. Boil for three minutes above 5,000 feet, then let it cool.[89] If you cannot boil water, add plain unscented bleach: 8 drops of 6% bleach or 6 drops of 8.25% bleach per gallon. Stir, and wait 30 minutes.[89] Use boiled or bottled water for drinking, cooking, brushing teeth, baby formula and ice. If the power is out too, a camp stove works, but only outdoors.[29]

**What to avoid.** Do not use water or ice from the fridge dispenser while a notice is on. Boil tap water even if it went through a home filter or a filter pitcher.[90] Boiling does not help if the notice warns of chemicals. That is a do-not-drink notice, and you need stored or bottled water instead.[90, 91]

### No tap water at all: about 6 months (1–6)

**What helps.** Store at least one gallon per person per day, for drinking and keeping clean.[1] Fill clean bottles or jugs with tap water and replace it every six months.[92] You can pick a level in the plan. About 3 liters a day covers drinking to survive, one gallon is the basic default, and about 4 gallons (15 liters) is the humanitarian minimum for drinking, cooking and washing.[93] A tank water heater holds 20 to 80 gallons of clean water.[94, 95] For long outages, a filter and a source of raw water go further than more jugs.[81] Filters do not remove viruses, so add a disinfectant such as chlorine dioxide after filtering.[91] A two-bucket toilet keeps waste safe when toilets cannot flush.[96]

**What to avoid.** Do not store water in containers that held chemicals.[92] Milk jugs seal poorly and turn brittle.[97] Never drink pool or spa water. Use it only for washing and cleaning.[95] After a flood, do not drink from a private well until it has been disinfected and tested. Your health department can tell you how.[98]

Water-system failures are counted about 9 times as often as the national average here (an expert estimate), because nearly all public-water customers in your county are served by a system with a health-based violation in the last five years (EPA) and you said your water system has frequent notices or outages. This rounds up to 6 months: before rounding it is about 3 months, just past 3 months. The worst loss of tap water on record in your state was in Puerto Rico after Hurricane Maria (2017): 2 months for most, up to 5 months. Being ready for 6 months would have covered that.[7, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 99, 100, 101]

### Can't get to a store: about 2 weeks (10 days to 1 month)

**What helps.** The food is on your checklists, counted in calories, not servings: pick foods you already eat, and use the oldest first.

**What to avoid.** Do not buy everything at once. It strains your budget and the store. Do not buy or eat from cans that bulge, leak, are rusted or are deeply dented.[102] Do not trust a kit's "days" label. Check the calories on the package instead.

### Dangerous heat or cold indoors: about 2 weeks (7 days to 3 months)

**What helps.** Agree who checks on whom, especially older neighbours and anyone who lives alone.[103, 104] In heat, go where there is air conditioning. Dial 2-1-1 to find one.[103] Cover windows with drapes or shades.[105] In cold, close off rooms you don't need, stuff towels under doors, and cover windows with blankets at night. Have extra blankets, sleeping bags and warm coats.[104] Babies under one year old should never sleep in a cold room.[104] If you cannot keep your home warm, go to a warming center. Dial 2-1-1 to find one.[106] If you heat with wood, have the stove and chimney professionally inspected and cleaned every year, because creosote from the smoke builds up and can start a fire.[107]

**What to avoid.** Fans move air but do not lower body temperature.[105] Above 90°F indoors, a fan can make you hotter.[103] Never heat your home with a gas oven, and never use a grill or camp stove indoors.[29] Headache, dizziness, nausea or confusion can mean carbon monoxide poisoning: get outside, then call 911.[83] Keep space heaters 3 feet from anything that can burn.[108] Never leave people or pets in a closed car on a warm day.[105]

### Safety rules to learn now

- **Fire:** Know two ways out of every room. Once you are out, stay out, and call 911.[36, 125]
- **Gas:** If your gas was shut off, only the gas company or a professional should turn it back on.[126]
- **Water heater:** Turn off its power or gas before you drain it for water.[126]
- **Food:** In a power cut, throw out food that has been at 40°F or warmer for 2 hours.[82]
- **Generator:** Run it outside, 20 feet from windows and doors. Never plug it into a wall outlet or the house wiring: that can electrocute utility workers and neighbors.[82, 127]

## Your shelter plan

Pick a spot for each danger below, at home and at work, and practice going there.[6]

**Strong wind.** In a high wind or severe thunderstorm warning, go inside a sturdy building, to an inside room.[128] Stay away from windows while damaging wind is near.[129]

**Hurricane.** If officials tell your evacuation zone to leave, go right away. If you stay, use an inside room away from windows.[4]

**Earthquake.** Drop where you are, cover your head and neck, and hold on. Crawl under a sturdy table, or next to an inside wall away from windows. Do not run outside.[17]

**Chemical release.** If told to stay inside, go to an above-ground room with few openings. Close the windows, doors and vents, turn off fans and air conditioning, and seal the room with plastic sheeting and duct tape.[130]

**Radiation.** If officials warn of fallout, get inside, stay inside, stay tuned. Go to a basement or the middle of a brick or concrete building.[78]

## When a storm, freeze or heat wave is forecast

Hurricanes, winter storms and heat waves usually come with warning. Keep your phone charged when one is forecast, and use the time the same way each time.[4]

**Before any storm**

- Charge phones, power banks and other batteries.[131] 
- Fill the car's tank and get some cash, since card readers and cash machines need power.[131] Charge the power station.[131] If you would leave in an electric car, charge it fully.[28]
- Refill prescriptions if you can.[131]
- Fill the bathtub and other large containers with water for flushing and cleaning.[131]
- Turn the fridge to its coldest setting and keep the doors shut.[131] A full freezer keeps food cold for about 48 hours, a fridge for about four.[82]
- Bring in or tie down outdoor furniture and grills.[4]
- Check your evacuation zone and your plan to leave.[4]
- Check with neighbors, older adults and anyone who may need help.[4]

**Before a heat wave.** Pick a place to cool down, such as a library, or ask your local health department about cooling options. Plan to check on family, older adults and neighbors. Fans do not lower body temperature.[105]

## After a disaster: the first 30 days

**Your county.** San Juan County, Puerto Rico had one federal major-disaster declaration in the last five full years.[155]

Recovery takes weeks. These steps come in rough order.

**Going home.** Go back only when local officials say it is safe. Do not go inside if you smell gas or the building looks damaged.[156] Run a generator only outside, more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents.[29]

**Insurance and records.** Before you clean up, take photos and videos and make a list of the damage.[156] Keep every receipt.[157] Call your insurer as soon as you can, and an adjuster will come to inspect. Check the adjuster's estimate, and talk to them before you sign a repair contract.[158]

**Help from FEMA.** If a federal disaster declaration covers your county, apply at DisasterAssistance.gov or call 1-800-621-3362.[159] FEMA help does not replace insurance.[160]

**Scams.** FEMA never charges to apply or to inspect.[161] Its inspectors carry an official photo ID and never ask for your bank details; a FEMA shirt is not an ID.[157] Never sign your insurance check over to a contractor, and walk away from anyone who wants cash up front.[162]

**Medicine.** In a declared disaster, Medicare drug plans can help you find a pharmacy and replace lost medicine.[147] Many states let a pharmacist give an emergency supply when your prescriber cannot be reached, and the rules differ by state.[146] Healthcare Ready's Rx Open map shows which pharmacies are open in a disaster area.[163]

**Papers.** To replace lost IDs, start with your birth certificate, from the vital records office of the state where you were born.[164]

**Rain.** Each state sets its own rules on collecting rain, and the rules vary widely, so check yours.[165] Rainwater is not safe to drink until germs and chemicals are removed, but it can water plants you do not eat.[166]

**Food.** Badly home-canned food can cause botulism. Vegetables, meat and fish need a pressure canner.[167] Use tested recipes, such as those from the National Center for Home Food Preservation.[168] Your state or county Extension service can help with growing and preserving food.[167]

#### Stress, mental health and the 988 line

**What helps.** Save 988 in every phone. Retrieved from https://fema.maps.arcgis.com/home/item.html?id=39485e8035d446a5bff03259508ae355 on 2026-09-26 (UTC). This product uses the Federal Emergency Management Agency’s National Risk Index dataset API or downloadable datasets but is not endorsed by FEMA. The Federal Government or FEMA cannot vouch for the data or analyses derived from these data after the data have been retrieved from the Agency's website(s).

> **CDC/ATSDR SVI, version 2022.** Centers for Disease Control and Prevention / Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry / Geospatial Research, Analysis, and Services Program. CDC/ATSDR Social Vulnerability Index 2022 Database US. https://svi.cdc.gov/Documents/Data/2022/csv/states_counties/SVI_2022_US_county.csv

> **CMRA, version CMRA 2025.** Climate projections: Climate Mapping for Resilience and Adaptation (CMRA), NOAA and the U.S. Climate Resilience Toolkit. https://www.arcgis.com/home/item.html?id=54f4e2343500422bbddf1f5dafb30bbd

> **EPA ECHO SDWA.** Drinking-water system violations from the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's Enforcement and Compliance History Online (ECHO), Safe Drinking Water Act data downloads. https://echo.epa.gov/tools/data-downloads/sdwa-download-summary

> **EPA TRI, USACE NID, FEMA nuclear sites.** Facility counts from EPA Toxics Release Inventory (2024), USACE National Inventory of Dams (2026-9-23) and FEMA's Operating Nuclear Power Plant Sites layer. https://nid.sec.usace.army.mil/

> **IGRF-14 and NERC TPL-007, version IGRF-14 epoch 2025.0.** Geomagnetic latitude from the International Geomagnetic Reference Field, 14th generation (IAGA, distributed by NOAA NCEI); scaling factor from NERC's Benchmark Geomagnetic Disturbance Event Description (TPL-007). https://www.ngdc.noaa.gov/IAGA/vmod/coeffs/igrf14coeffs.txt

> **NCA5 Atlas and LOCA2, version NCA5 (2023).** Climate projections: U.S. Global Change Research Program, Fifth National Climate Assessment Interactive Atlas and LOCA2 county summaries (CC BY 4.0). Ratios derived by Ready Reckoner. https://www.arcgis.com/home/item.html?id=2f7a8715927b4ba083be450c85f7f761

> **NCA5 Atlas regions, version NCA5 (2023).** Region boundaries: U.S. Global Change Research Program, Fifth National Climate Assessment Interactive Atlas (CC0 1.0). https://www.arcgis.com/home/item.html?id=d6614156fe694956be25f4bb9f52b378

> **NOAA HMS and EPA AQS, version 2016-2023.** Smoke days from NOAA NESDIS Hazard Mapping System smoke analysis polygons and U.S. EPA Air Quality System daily PM2.5 data. https://www.ospo.noaa.gov/products/land/hms.html

> **NOAA nClimGrid-Daily, version v1.0.0.** Daily temperature from NOAA's NClimGrid-Daily county averages (Durre et al., NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information). https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/land-based-station/nclimgrid-daily

> **ORNL EAGLE-I, version figshare version 4 published 2026-02-25T18:52:23Z (10.6084/m9.figshare.24237376.v4).** Power outage statistics derived by Ready Reckoner from: Brelsford, C., Tennille, S., Myers, A., et al., The Environment for Analysis of Geo-Located Energy Information's Recorded Electricity Outages 2014-2025, Oak Ridge National Laboratory, figshare, doi:10.6084/m9.figshare.24237376 (CC BY 4.0). https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.24237376

> **OpenFEMA (NFIP), version Penetration v1, Claims v3.** Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA), OpenFEMA Datasets: NFIP Residential Penetration Rates - v1 and FIMA NFIP Redacted Claims - v3. Retrieved from https://www.fema.gov/openfema-data-page/nfip-residential-penetration-rates-v1 and https://www.fema.gov/openfema-data-page/nfip-redacted-claims-v3 on 2026-09-26 (UTC). This product uses the Federal Emergency Management Agency’s OpenFEMA API, but is not endorsed by FEMA. The Federal Government or FEMA cannot vouch for the data or analyses derived from these data after the data have been retrieved from the Agency's website(s).

> **OpenFEMA declarations and housing assistance, version v2.** Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA), OpenFEMA Datasets: Disaster Declarations Summaries - v2, Housing Assistance Program Data - Owners - v2 and Renters - v2. Retrieved from https://www.fema.gov/openfema-data-page/disaster-declarations-summaries-v2, https://www.fema.gov/openfema-data-page/housing-assistance-program-data-owners-v2 and https://www.fema.gov/openfema-data-page/housing-assistance-program-data-renters-v2. This product uses the Federal Emergency Management Agency’s OpenFEMA API, but is not endorsed by FEMA. The Federal Government or FEMA cannot vouch for the data or analyses derived from these data after the data have been retrieved from the Agency's website(s).

> **PNNL Event-correlated Outage Dataset, version release 1 (Outage_Dataset_R1.zip).** Grid disturbance counts derived by Ready Reckoner from the Event-correlated Outage Dataset in America, Pacific Northwest National Laboratory, OpenEI submission 6458 (CC BY 4.0), which links U.S. Department of Energy Form OE-417 electric emergency and disturbance reports to ORNL EAGLE-I outages. https://data.openei.org/submissions/6458

> **Storm-surge proxy.** County storm-surge proxy built by Ready Reckoner from the FEMA National Risk Index (coastal flooding exposure), NOAA NHC HURDAT2 storm tracks and state and local hurricane evacuation-zone tools. https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/nationalsurge/

> **Strategic sites, version reviewed 2026-09-26.** Strategic-site classes compiled by Ready Reckoner from public sources: DoD MIRTA installation points, the Sentinel Final EIS (Department of the Air Force), NNSA, the Missile Defense Agency, Census metropolitan areas and centres of population, EIA refinery capacity, BTS port statistics and FEMA's FY2026 UASI allocations. A higher class does not mean an attack is likely. https://github.com/holdTheDoorHoid/ready-reckoner/blob/main/crates/rr-etl/data/strategic_sites.toml

> **USACE National Levee Database.** People behind levees and levee risk ratings from the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers National Levee Database. https://levees.sec.usace.army.mil/

> **USGS National Seismic Hazard Model, version NSHM 2023 grids, Hawaii 2021.R2 grid, PRVI 2025 service.** Earthquake shaking probabilities from the U.S. Geological Survey National Seismic Hazard Model (2023 50-state model data release; Hawaii 2021.R2; Puerto Rico and U.S. Virgin Islands 2025). https://doi.org/10.5066/P9GNPCOD

> **USGS karst and landslide maps.** Karst: Weary, D.J., and Doctor, D.H., 2014, Karst in the United States: A digital map compilation and database, USGS Open-File Report 2014-1156. Landslides: USGS, Slope-Relief Threshold Landslide Susceptibility Models for the United States and Puerto Rico, 2024, doi:10.5066/P13KAGU3 (CC0). https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/2014/1156/

The other data sets were accessed September 26, 2026 too, unless they say otherwise.
