### Leaving home

Warning can be as short as a few minutes (a home fire) or as long as 3 days. For chemical spills and releases, plan for as little as a few minutes of warning; for wildfires, 15 minutes. ### Staying in touch

**Four ways to reach each other.** Emergency planners call this a PACE plan: a primary way, an alternate, a contingency and an emergency way. Each one takes over when the one before it fails.[16] For a household it can be:

1. Primary: call.
2. Alternate: text. A text may get through when a call does not, and it saves battery.[17]
3. Contingency: everyone texts one out-of-state contact, who passes the news along.[17]
4. Emergency: go to the place you agreed to meet if you get separated.[17]

### What to know and do

#### 1. Heat wave

Nearly every household like yours will go through a heat wave in a ten-year stretch around 2050.[6, 18]

**Includes:** a blackout during a heat wave.

**What helps.** Know where you can go to cool off. Dial 2-1-1 to find air conditioning near you.[19] Cover windows with drapes or shades, and weather-strip doors and windows.[20] Take cool showers, wear loose, light-colored clothing, and drink plenty of fluids. Check on family members, older adults and neighbours.[20] Heat can cause power outages, so plan for refrigerated medicines and powered medical devices.[19] In a drought, follow local limits on water use, and make saving water a daily habit.[21]

**What to avoid.** Do not rely on a fan when it is over 90°F indoors. It can make you hotter.[19] Never leave people or pets in a closed car on a warm day.[20] Heat stroke is an emergency. Signs include a body temperature of 103°F or more, hot, red skin that may be dry or damp, a fast, strong pulse, confusion or passing out. Call 911, move the person somewhere cooler, cool them with cool cloths or a cool bath, and give them nothing to drink.[22]

**How bad:** Serious (based on data).

#### 2. Medical emergency

Of 100 households like yours, about 99 will have someone need emergency care in the next ten years.[23]

**Includes:** far from emergency care; falls.

**What helps.** Take a first-aid and bleeding-control class. You may be able to save a life by acting right after an injury, before help arrives.[24] If a teen or adult collapses and their heart stops, call 911 and push hard and fast in the center of the chest. This hands-only CPR needs no training. Note where the nearest heart defibrillator (AED) is; it talks you through each step.[25] Keep a stocked first-aid kit. Check it regularly and replace anything used or out of date.[26] Keep a list of each person's medicines, doses and allergies with your emergency supplies.[27] Know how to reach 911. Call if you can, and text where that service works.[28]

**What to avoid.** Do not start antibiotics on your own or take someone else's. The wrong medicine can delay the right treatment and cause serious side effects.[29] Do not use expired medicines.[30]

**How bad:** Serious (based on data).

#### 3. Cold wave

Of 100 households like yours, about 93 will go through a spell of dangerous cold in the next ten years.[18]

**Includes:** rolling blackouts in extreme cold.

**What helps.** Keep extra blankets, sleeping bags and warm coats, and pick one room to keep warm.[31] If you cannot keep your home warm, go to a warming center. Dial 2-1-1 to find one.[32] Learn how to keep pipes from freezing, and test smoke and carbon monoxide alarms before winter.[33] Keep a car kit with jumper cables, sand, a flashlight, warm clothes, blankets, water and snacks, and keep the gas tank full.[33] Check on older neighbours often.[31] Know the signs of hypothermia, such as shivering, confusion and slurred speech. A body temperature below 95°F is an emergency. Warm the center of the body first.[33]

**What to avoid.** Never heat your home with a gas stove or oven. Use generators and grills only outdoors, away from windows.[33] Choose electric space heaters with automatic shut-off, and keep them 3 feet from anything that can burn.[31, 34] Do not overdo it when you shovel snow.[33]

**How bad:** Serious (based on data).

#### 4. Hurricane

Of 100 households like yours, about 82 (58–95) will lose power or have damage from a hurricane or tropical storm in a ten-year stretch around 2050.[10, 11, 12, 14]

**Includes:** inland flooding from a tropical storm.

**What helps.** Know your evacuation zone, your routes and where you would stay. If you live in a mandatory evacuation zone and officials tell you to leave, go right away.[4] Winds are stronger higher up. If you are not told to leave, take shelter on or below the 10th floor, in a small inside room or hallway away from windows and above any flooding. Elevators can stop when the power goes out.[35, 36, 37] Clear drains and gutters, bring in outdoor furniture, and think about storm shutters. Keep phones charged and have backup chargers.[4] A flood policy typically takes up to 30 days to start, so buy it well before a storm.[38] If you would leave in an electric car, keep it fully charged all hurricane season.[39]

**What to avoid.** Never walk or drive through floodwater. One foot of moving water can sweep a car away.[38] Do not drive or charge an electric car that has been in floodwater. Salt water can start a battery fire.[39] Never run a generator inside your home or garage.[40]

**How bad:** Serious (mostly data).

#### 5. Burst pipe or water leak

Of 100 households like yours, about 14 will have a burst pipe or leak flood part of the home in the next ten years.[41]

**Includes:** sewer backup.

**What helps.** Find your main water shut-off valve now, and show everyone how to turn it off. A burst pipe can flood a room quickly.[42, 43] Once a year, check the hoses to the water heater, washing machine, dishwasher and fridge, and replace any that crack or leak. A leak alarm can warn you early.[42] In freezing weather, let a faucet drip at night, open the cabinet doors under sinks on outside walls, and keep the heat at 55°F or higher.[43] Home and renters policies usually cover burst pipes. Many do not cover a sewer backup unless you add that cover, and floods need their own policy.[42]

**What to avoid.** Never thaw a frozen pipe with an open flame; use a hair dryer. Watch for electric shock around standing water.[43] Do not leave home while the washing machine or dishwasher is running.[42]

**How bad:** Severe (mostly data).

#### 6. House fire

Of 100 households like yours, about 4 (2–6) will have a fire in their home or building in the next ten years.[14, 15]

**Includes:** carbon monoxide poisoning; lithium battery fire.

**What helps.** Put smoke alarms inside and outside every bedroom and on every level, and test them each month.[44] Practice your escape plan twice a year, including finding your way out in the dark.[45] Sleep with bedroom doors closed. Teach children not to hide from firefighters.[45] If there is smoke, crawl low under it to your exit.[45] If you cannot get out, close the door, cover vents and cracks with cloth or tape, call 911, and signal from the window with a light-colored cloth or a flashlight.[45] Insurance and copies of your documents help you recover afterward.[46]

**What to avoid.** Before you open a door, feel the door and the knob. If either is hot, or smoke is coming around it, keep it closed and use your second way out.[45] If a person or pet is still inside, get out and tell firefighters right away.[45] If your clothes catch fire, stop, drop and roll.[45]

**How bad:** Severe (mostly data).

#### 7. Flooding from rivers or heavy rain

Of 100 households like yours, about 3 (1–6) will be cut off or lose building services in a flood in the next ten years.[13, 14]

**Includes:** flash flood; basement or street flooding in heavy rain.

**What helps.** Look up your address on FEMA's flood maps, and sign up for your community's warnings.[38] Homeowner's insurance does not cover flooding, and a flood policy typically takes up to 30 days to start.[38] Renters can buy flood coverage for their belongings.[47] Know how you would get to higher ground or a higher floor.[38]

**What to avoid.** Six inches of moving water can knock you down, and one foot can sweep your car away.[38] Never drive around barriers on a flooded road. The road may have collapsed under the water.[48] Stay off bridges over fast-moving water.[38] Leave if you are told to.[49]

**How bad:** Severe (mostly data).

#### 8. Coastal flooding

Of 100 households like yours, about 3 (1–8) will be cut off or lose building services in coastal flooding in the next ten years.[9, 10, 14]

**What helps.** The steps under "Flooding from rivers or heavy rain" above apply here too.

**How bad:** Serious (rough data).

### No grid power at home: about 2 weeks (10 days to 3 weeks)

**What helps.** A light for each person, a radio and phone power are in your plan; keeping food cold and charging up are on the forecast list; registries are under Local help.

**What to avoid.** Never run a generator inside your home or garage, even with the doors and windows open. Keep it outside, more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents.[40] Put a carbon monoxide alarm on every level of your home.[94] Headache, dizziness, nausea or confusion can mean carbon monoxide poisoning. Get everyone outside to fresh air, then call 911.[95] Never heat your home with a gas stove or oven. Unplug electronics, because the power can come back with a surge that damages them.[94] Treat every downed power line as live: stay at least 30 feet from it and anything it touches, and call 911.[96] If one falls on your car, stay inside unless the car is on fire.[97]

This rounds up to 2 weeks: before rounding it is about 2 weeks, just past 10 days. The worst power cut in your region's records (where it was worst, about 129 miles (207 km) away) was Hurricane Ian (2022); being ready for 2 weeks would have outlasted it for at least 9 in 10 of the homes that lost power.[10, 11, 12, 14, 89, 98, 99]

### Tap water must be treated: about 2 weeks (5 days to 1 month)

**What helps.** Use bottled water, or bring clear water to a rolling boil for one minute. Boil for three minutes above 5,000 feet, then let it cool.[100] If you cannot boil water, add plain unscented bleach: 8 drops of 6% bleach or 6 drops of 8.25% bleach per gallon. Stir, and wait 30 minutes.[100] Use boiled or bottled water for drinking, cooking, brushing teeth, baby formula and ice. If the power is out too, a camp stove works, but only outdoors.[40]

**What to avoid.** Do not use water or ice from the fridge dispenser while a notice is on. Boil tap water even if it went through a home filter or a filter pitcher.[101] Boiling does not help if the notice warns of chemicals. That is a do-not-drink notice, and you need stored or bottled water instead.[101, 102]

This rounds up to 2 weeks: before rounding it is about 2 weeks, just past 10 days.[7, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 18, 103, 104, 105, 106]

### No tap water at all: about 2 weeks (10 days to 1 month)

**What helps.** Store at least one gallon per person per day, for drinking and keeping clean.[1] Fill clean bottles or jugs with tap water and replace it every six months.[107] You can pick a level in the plan. About 3 liters a day covers drinking to survive, one gallon is the basic default, and about 4 gallons (15 liters) is the humanitarian minimum for drinking, cooking and washing.[108] A tank water heater holds 20 to 80 gallons of clean water.[109, 110] For long outages, a filter and a source of raw water go further than more jugs.[92] Filters do not remove viruses, so add a disinfectant such as chlorine dioxide after filtering.[102] A two-bucket toilet keeps waste safe when toilets cannot flush.[111]

**What to avoid.** Do not store water in containers that held chemicals.[107] Milk jugs seal poorly and turn brittle.[112] Never drink pool or spa water. Use it only for washing and cleaning.[110] After a flood, do not drink from a private well until it has been disinfected and tested. Your health department can tell you how.[113]

Water-system failures are counted about half as often as the national average here (an expert estimate), because fewer than 1 of 100 public-water customers in your county are served by a system with a health-based violation in the last five years (EPA).[7, 8, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 18, 98, 99, 103, 104]

### Safety rules to learn now

- **Fire:** Know two ways out of every room. Once you are out, stay out, and call 911.[45, 129]
- **Gas:** If your gas was shut off, only the gas company or a professional should turn it back on.[130]

- **Generator:** Run it outside, 20 feet from windows and doors. Never plug it into a wall outlet or the house wiring: that can electrocute utility workers and neighbors.[94, 131]
- **CPR:** Take a first-aid and CPR class.[121]

### Things to watch

- **Worth acting on: No way to keep refrigerated medicine cool through a power cut by month 3.** Insulin keeps working out of the fridge for a while if it stays cool, but a home without power in hot weather can get too warm, and other medicines must stay in the fridge. A cooler bag with cold packs helps only for a short time; a battery power station can keep a small cooler or the fridge running. Ask your pharmacist how long yours can stay out of the fridge.

## Your shelter plan

Pick a spot for each danger below, at home and at work, and practice going there.[5]

**Strong wind.** In a high wind or severe thunderstorm warning, go inside a sturdy building, to an inside room.[132] Stay away from windows while damaging wind is near.[133]

**Hurricane.** If officials tell your evacuation zone to leave, go right away. If you stay, use an inside room away from windows.[4] In a tall building, take shelter on or below the 10th floor when hurricane winds come, and do not count on the elevators.[35, 37]

**Chemical release.** If told to stay inside, go to an above-ground room with few openings. Close the windows, doors and vents, turn off fans and air conditioning, and seal the room with plastic sheeting and duct tape.[134]

**Radiation.** If officials warn of fallout, get inside, stay inside, stay tuned. Go to a basement or the middle of a brick or concrete building.[81]

## When a storm, freeze or heat wave is forecast

Hurricanes, winter storms and heat waves usually come with warning. Keep your phone charged when one is forecast, and use the time the same way each time.[4]

**Before any storm**

- Charge phones, power banks and other batteries.[135] 
- Fill the car's tank and get some cash, since card readers and cash machines need power.[135] Charge the power station.[135] If you would leave in an electric car, charge it fully.[39]
- Refill prescriptions if you can.[135]
- Fill the bathtub and other large containers with water for flushing and cleaning.[135]
- Turn the fridge to its coldest setting and keep the doors shut.[135] A full freezer keeps food cold for about 48 hours, a fridge for about four.[94]
- Bring in or tie down outdoor furniture and grills.[4]
- Check your evacuation zone and your plan to leave.[4]
- Check with neighbors, older adults and anyone who may need help.[4]

**Before a hard freeze.** Let hot and cold water drip from a faucet at night, open the cabinet doors under sinks on outside walls, and keep the heat at 55°F or higher. Make sure everyone knows how to shut off the water in case a pipe bursts.[43]

**Before a heat wave.** Pick a place to cool down, such as a library, or ask your local health department about cooling options. Plan to check on family, older adults and neighbors. Fans do not lower body temperature.[20]

## After a disaster: the first 30 days

**Your county.** Miami-Dade County, Florida had 3 federal major-disaster declarations in the last five full years.[147]

Recovery takes weeks. These steps come in rough order.

**Going home.** Go back only when local officials say it is safe. Do not go inside if you smell gas or the building looks damaged.[148] Run a generator only outside, more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents.[40]

**Insurance and records.** Before you clean up, take photos and videos and make a list of the damage.[148] Keep every receipt.[149] Call your insurer as soon as you can, and an adjuster will come to inspect. Check the adjuster's estimate, and talk to them before you sign a repair contract.[150]

**Help from FEMA.** If a federal disaster declaration covers your county, apply at DisasterAssistance.gov or call 1-800-621-3362.[151] FEMA help does not replace insurance.[152]

**Scams.** FEMA never charges to apply or to inspect.[153] Its inspectors carry an official photo ID and never ask for your bank details; a FEMA shirt is not an ID.[149] Never sign your insurance check over to a contractor, and walk away from anyone who wants cash up front.[154]

**Medicine.** In a declared disaster, Medicare drug plans can help you find a pharmacy and replace lost medicine.[155] Many states let a pharmacist give an emergency supply when your prescriber cannot be reached, and the rules differ by state.[140] Healthcare Ready's Rx Open map shows which pharmacies are open in a disaster area.[156]

**Papers.** To replace lost IDs, start with your birth certificate, from the vital records office of the state where you were born.[157]

## If it lasts for months

- [ ] Going further: food for months (month 1)

**How likely here.** From your own power curve, power out for two months or more: between 1 in 1,800 and 1 in 290 households like yours in the next 10 years; for three months or more: between 1 in 7,900 and 1 in 1,200.[10, 11, 12, 14, 89, 98, 99]

These pointers are for the rare event that lasts for months. They are places to start, not a manual.

**Rain.** Each state sets its own rules on collecting rain, and the rules vary widely, so check yours.[158] Rainwater is not safe to drink until germs and chemicals are removed, but it can water plants you do not eat.[159]

**Toilets.** After a strong earthquake, toilets may not flush for weeks or months. A twin-bucket toilet keeps pee and poo apart, and handling poo safely stops diseases like cholera.[111]

**Food.** Badly home-canned food can cause botulism. Vegetables, meat and fish need a pressure canner.[160] Use tested recipes, such as those from the National Center for Home Food Preservation.[161] Your state or county Extension service can help with growing and preserving food.[160]

**Spirits.** Keep regular sleep and wake times, take breaks from the news, and talk with people you trust.[162] Keep books, games and puzzles for children.[163]

### Stress, mental health and the 988 line

**What helps.** Save 988 in every phone. It offers free, judgment-free support 24/7 for mental health, substance use and more, by call, text or chat.[176] After a disaster, the Disaster Distress Helpline offers crisis counseling 24/7 to anyone in emotional distress. Call or text 1-800-985-5990.[177] Write a short calm plan: what you might feel, what helps you, and who you will call.[178] Oregon's 2 Weeks Ready program even includes practicing mindfulness as a step in getting prepared.[179]

**What to avoid.** Do not keep the news on all day. No one, adult or child, benefits from graphic details or disturbing images.[180] Reach out before things feel unbearable. The 988 line supports people in emotional distress as well as in crisis.[176]
