### Staying in touch

**Four ways to reach each other.** Emergency planners call this a PACE plan: a primary way, an alternate, a contingency and an emergency way. Each one takes over when the one before it fails.[12] For a household it can be:

1. Primary: call.
2. Alternate: text. A text may get through when a call does not, and it saves battery.[13]
3. Contingency: everyone texts one out-of-state contact, who passes the news along.[13]
4. Emergency: go to the place you agreed to meet if you get separated.[13]

## Your risks

What could reach a household like yours in Maricopa County, Arizona over the next 10 years: the likeliest events, and the rarer ones that can kill, most likely first.

### What to know and do

#### 1. Heat wave

Nearly every household like yours will go through a heat wave in a ten-year stretch around 2050 (about 14 times a year).[3, 14, 15, 16]

**Includes:** a blackout during a heat wave.

**What helps.** Know where you can go to cool off. Dial 2-1-1 to find air conditioning near you.[17] Cover windows with drapes or shades, and weather-strip doors and windows.[18] Take cool showers, wear loose, light-colored clothing, and drink plenty of fluids. Check on family members, older adults and neighbours.[18] Heat can cause power outages, so plan for refrigerated medicines and powered medical devices.[17] In a drought, follow local limits on water use, and make saving water a daily habit.[19]

**What to avoid.** Do not rely on a fan when it is over 90°F indoors. It can make you hotter.[17] Never leave people or pets in a closed car on a warm day.[18] Heat stroke is an emergency. Signs include a body temperature of 103°F or more, hot, red skin that may be dry or damp, a fast, strong pulse, confusion or passing out. Call 911, move the person somewhere cooler, cool them with cool cloths or a cool bath, and give them nothing to drink.[20]

**How bad:** Serious (based on data).

#### 2. Dust storm

Nearly every household like yours will be caught in a dust storm that closes roads or fouls the air at home in the next ten years.[10, 14]

**What helps.** If dense dust is blowing across the road ahead, avoid driving into it. If it reaches you, pull off the pavement as far as you can, stop, turn off all your lights, set the parking brake and take your foot off the brake pedal.[21] If you cannot pull off, slow down, turn on your lights, sound the horn now and then, and follow the painted center line.[21]

**What to avoid.** Never stop in a lane of traffic. Do not leave your lights on once you are parked off the road: drivers behind may follow them and hit you.[21]

**How bad:** Serious (rough data).

#### 3. Medical emergency

Of 100 households like yours, about 99 will have someone need emergency care in the next ten years.[22]

**Includes:** far from emergency care; falls; going into labour during a disaster.

**What helps.** Take a first-aid and bleeding-control class. You may be able to save a life by acting right after an injury, before help arrives.[23] If a teen or adult collapses and their heart stops, call 911 and push hard and fast in the center of the chest. This hands-only CPR needs no training. Note where the nearest heart defibrillator (AED) is; it talks you through each step.[24] Keep a stocked first-aid kit. Check it regularly and replace anything used or out of date.[25] Keep a list of each person's medicines, doses and allergies with your emergency supplies.[26] Know how to reach 911. Call if you can, and text where that service works.[27]

**What to avoid.** Do not start antibiotics on your own or take someone else's. The wrong medicine can delay the right treatment and cause serious side effects.[28] Do not use expired medicines.[29]

**How bad:** Serious (based on data).

#### 4. Burst pipe or water leak

Of 100 households like yours, about 11 (7–16) will have a burst pipe or leak flood part of the home in the next ten years.[10, 30]

**Includes:** sewer backup.

**What helps.** Find your main water shut-off valve now, and show everyone how to turn it off. A burst pipe can flood a room quickly.[31, 32] Once a year, check the hoses to the water heater, washing machine, dishwasher and fridge, and replace any that crack or leak. A leak alarm can warn you early.[31] In freezing weather, let a faucet drip at night, open the cabinet doors under sinks on outside walls, and keep the heat at 55°F or higher.[32] Home and renters policies usually cover burst pipes. Many do not cover a sewer backup unless you add that cover, and floods need their own policy.[31]

**What to avoid.** Never thaw a frozen pipe with an open flame; use a hair dryer. Watch for electric shock around standing water.[32] Do not leave home while the washing machine or dishwasher is running.[31]

**How bad:** Severe (mostly data).

#### 5. House fire

Of 100 households like yours, about 5 (3–8) will have a house fire, in their home or next door in the next ten years.[10, 11]

**Includes:** carbon monoxide poisoning; lithium battery fire.

**What helps.** Put smoke alarms inside and outside every bedroom and on every level, and test them each month.[33] Practice your escape plan twice a year, including finding your way out in the dark.[34] Sleep with bedroom doors closed. Teach children not to hide from firefighters.[34] If there is smoke, crawl low under it to your exit.[34] If you cannot get out, close the door, cover vents and cracks with cloth or tape, call 911, and signal from the window with a light-colored cloth or a flashlight.[34] Insurance and copies of your documents help you recover afterward.[35]

**What to avoid.** Before you open a door, feel the door and the knob. If either is hot, or smoke is coming around it, keep it closed and use your second way out.[34] If a person or pet is still inside, get out and tell firefighters right away.[34] If your clothes catch fire, stop, drop and roll.[34]

**How bad:** Severe (mostly data).

#### 6. Wildfire

Of 100 households like yours, about 3 (1–17) will have to leave home or lose power because of a wildfire in a ten-year stretch around 2050.[3, 6, 10]

**What helps.** Keep a zone at least 30 feet around your home clear of leaves, debris and anything that burns.[36] Know your evacuation zone and routes, and practice with your pets.[36] Store N95 masks for smoke. Pick a room you can close off from outside air, and run a portable air cleaner there.[36] N95 masks are not made to fit children, so for them the clean room is the protection.[37] A box fan with a MERV 13 filter makes a low-cost air cleaner.[38] If your heating and cooling system brings in fresh air, set it to recirculate.[36] Keep phones charged and have backup chargers.[36]

**What to avoid.** Leave as soon as authorities tell you to, even if you cannot see flames.[36] Do not use an air cleaner that makes ozone.[39] Avoid hard exercise when it is smoky, so you breathe in less smoke.[39]

**How bad:** Moderate (rough data).

#### 7. Flooding from rivers or heavy rain

Of 100 households like yours, about 2 (fewer than 1 to 5) will be cut off or lose building services in a flood in the next ten years.[9, 10]

**Includes:** flash flood; basement or street flooding in heavy rain.

**What helps.** Look up your address on FEMA's flood maps, and sign up for your community's warnings.[40] Homeowner's insurance does not cover flooding, and a flood policy typically takes up to 30 days to start.[40] Renters can buy flood coverage for their belongings.[41] Know how you would get to higher ground or a higher floor.[40]

**What to avoid.** Six inches of moving water can knock you down, and one foot can sweep your car away.[40] Never drive around barriers on a flooded road. The road may have collapsed under the water.[42] Stay off bridges over fast-moving water.[40] Leave if you are told to.[43]

**How bad:** Very severe (rough data).

#### 8. Hail

Of 100 households like yours, about 1 (fewer than 1 to 2) will have hail damage their home or car in the next ten years.[6, 10]

**What helps.** Pick your shelter spot now. A safe room or storm shelter is best.[44] In an apartment building, pick an inside hallway, stairwell or small room with no windows, as low in the building as you can get to quickly. Stay off the elevators.[45, 46] Sign up for your community's warnings, and learn what your sirens sound like.[44] When thunder roars, go indoors. Get into a building or a car with a roof.[47]

**What to avoid.** Stay away from windows, doors and outside walls.[44] During a thunderstorm, avoid running water and electronics plugged into the wall.[47]

**How bad:** Severe (mostly data).

### No grid power at home: about 3 days (2–7)

**What helps.** A light for each person, a radio and phone power are in your plan; keeping food cold and charging up are on the forecast list; registries are under Local help.

**What to avoid.** Never run a generator inside your home or garage, even with the doors and windows open. Keep it outside, more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents.[94] Put a carbon monoxide alarm on every level of your home.[95] Headache, dizziness, nausea or confusion can mean carbon monoxide poisoning. Get everyone outside to fresh air, then call 911.[96] Never heat your home with a gas stove or oven. Unplug electronics, because the power can come back with a surge that damages them.[95] Treat every downed power line as live: stay at least 30 feet from it and anything it touches, and call 911.[97] If one falls on your car, stay inside unless the car is on fire.[98]

The worst power cut in your region's records (where it was worst, about 102 miles (164 km) away) was Winter storm, January 2023; being ready for 3 days would have outlasted it for at least 9 in 10 of the homes that lost power.[3, 6, 7, 8, 10, 14, 15, 16, 99, 100]

### Tap water must be treated: about 5 days (1 day to 2 weeks)

**What helps.** Use bottled water, or bring clear water to a rolling boil for one minute. Boil for three minutes above 5,000 feet, then let it cool.[101] If you cannot boil water, add plain unscented bleach: 8 drops of 6% bleach or 6 drops of 8.25% bleach per gallon. Stir, and wait 30 minutes.[101] Use boiled or bottled water for drinking, cooking, brushing teeth, baby formula and ice. If the power is out too, a camp stove works, but only outdoors.[94]

**What to avoid.** Do not use water or ice from the fridge dispenser while a notice is on. Boil tap water even if it went through a home filter or a filter pitcher.[102] Boiling does not help if the notice warns of chemicals. That is a do-not-drink notice, and you need stored or bottled water instead.[102, 103]

### No tap water at all: about 5 days (2–10)

**What helps.** Store at least one gallon per person per day, for drinking and keeping clean.[1] Fill clean bottles or jugs with tap water and replace it every six months.[104] You can pick a level in the plan. About 3 liters a day covers drinking to survive, one gallon is the basic default, and about 4 gallons (15 liters) is the humanitarian minimum for drinking, cooking and washing.[105] A tank water heater holds 20 to 80 gallons of clean water.[106, 107] For long outages, a filter and a source of raw water go further than more jugs.[93] Filters do not remove viruses, so add a disinfectant such as chlorine dioxide after filtering.[103] A two-bucket toilet keeps waste safe when toilets cannot flush.[108]

**What to avoid.** Do not store water in containers that held chemicals.[104] Milk jugs seal poorly and turn brittle.[109] Never drink pool or spa water. Use it only for washing and cleaning.[107] After a flood, do not drink from a private well until it has been disinfected and tested. Your health department can tell you how.[110]

Water-system failures are counted about half as often as the national average here (an expert estimate), because about 5 of 100 public-water customers in your county are served by a system with a health-based violation in the last five years (EPA). This rounds up to 5 days: before rounding it is about 3.3 days, just past 3 days.[3, 4, 5, 9, 10, 14, 16, 30, 31, 111, 112]

### Things to watch

- **Worth acting on: No backup power for a medical device by month 3.** A breathing machine or other powered device stops when the power goes out. Ask the device supplier which battery or backup works with it, and ask your power company about its medical-needs list.
- **Worth acting on: No stored water beyond refilled bottles by month 3.** Refilled drink bottles are a good start, but they hold only a little. Water is the one supply you cannot go long without: a few gallons of bottled water or a water jug is the next step, and costs little.
- **Worth acting on: At this budget the full plan would take more than three years, so it starts with the bare minimum.** The smallest kit that covers three days of water, light, warmth and medicine comes first and is complete by month 4; everything else follows in the usual order, and the whole plan takes until month 42. ## Your shelter plan

In FEMA's 2024 survey, where to shelter safely was the information people most wanted.[132] Pick a spot for each danger below, at home and at work, and practice going there.[2]

**Hurricane.** If you live in an evacuation zone and officials tell you to leave, go right away. If you stay, use an inside room away from windows.[133]

**Chemical release.** Pick an above-ground room with as few openings as possible. If you are told to stay inside, close the windows, doors and vents, turn off air conditioning and fans, and seal the room with plastic sheeting and duct tape.[134]

**Radiation.** If officials warn of fallout, get inside, stay inside, stay tuned. Go to a basement or the middle of a brick or concrete building.[83]

## When a storm, freeze or heat wave is forecast

Hurricanes, winter storms and heat waves usually come with warning. Keep your phone charged when one is forecast, and use the time the same way each time.[133]

**Before any storm**

- Charge phones, power banks and other batteries.[135] 
- Fill the car's tank and get some cash, since card readers and cash machines need power.[135] Charge the power station.[135] If you would leave in an electric car, charge it fully.[136]
- Refill prescriptions if you can.[135]
- Fill the bathtub and other large containers with water for flushing and cleaning.[135]
- Turn the fridge to its coldest setting and keep the doors shut.[135] A full freezer keeps food cold for about 48 hours, a fridge for about four.[95]
- Bring in or tie down outdoor furniture and grills.[133]
- Check your evacuation zone and your plan to leave.[133]
- Check with neighbors, older adults and anyone who may need help.[133]

**Before a hard freeze.** Let hot and cold water drip from a faucet at night, open the cabinet doors under sinks on outside walls, and keep the heat at 55°F or higher. Make sure everyone knows how to shut off the water in case a pipe bursts.[32]

**Before a heat wave.** Pick a place to cool down, such as a library, or ask your local health department about cooling options. Plan to check on family, older adults and neighbors. Fans do not lower body temperature.[18]

### Decisions

- [ ] **Decide: ID for every person (passport book or card, or a state ID).** A passport book or card for each person, children included, proves who you are and your citizenship if other papers are lost, and the card shows no home address; get them before you need them. The federal fee schedule: a first adult book $165 and card $65 ($130 and $30 plus a $35 acceptance fee), for children under 16 a book $135 and card $50 (22 CFR 22.1).[35, 147, 148]
- [ ] **Decide: flood insurance, even outside a flood zone.** Home and renters policies usually don't cover floods. Decide on flood insurance before you need it: Most homes in your county are outside the mapped high-risk flood zone, and that is where many uninsured floods happen: about 3 in 10 flood insurance claims come from outside high-risk flood areas. A quote costs nothing, and a new policy usually starts 30 days after you buy it. It pays up to $250,000 for the building and $100,000 for belongings, and renters can buy cover for belongings only. (FEMA, FloodSmart, Ready.gov)[9, 41, 149, 150, 151]
- [ ] **Decide: disability and life insurance for the people who earn.** You earn your household's money: check whether your job offers disability insurance. About 1 in 4 workers who start at 20 become disabled before retirement age. NAIC's buyer's guide helps you work out how much cover is enough. (SSA, NAIC)[59, 152]

## After a disaster: the first 30 days

**Your county.** Maricopa County, Arizona had no federal major-disaster declarations in the last five full years.[155]

Recovery takes weeks. These steps come in rough order.

**Going home.** Go back only when local officials say it is safe. Do not go inside if you smell gas or the building looks damaged.[156] Run a generator only outside, more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents.[94]

**Insurance and records.** Before you clean up, take photos and videos and make a list of the damage.[156] Keep every receipt.[157] Call your insurer as soon as you can, and an adjuster will come to inspect. Check the adjuster's estimate, and talk to them before you sign a repair contract.[158]

**Help from FEMA.** If a federal disaster declaration covers your county, apply at DisasterAssistance.gov or call 1-800-621-3362.[159] FEMA help does not replace insurance.[160]

**Scams.** FEMA never charges to apply or to inspect.[161] Its inspectors carry an official photo ID and never ask for your bank details; a FEMA shirt is not an ID.[157] Never sign your insurance check over to a contractor, and walk away from anyone who wants cash up front.[162]

**Medicine.** In a declared disaster, Medicare drug plans can help you find a pharmacy and replace lost medicine.[163] Many states let a pharmacist give an emergency supply when your prescriber cannot be reached, and the rules differ by state.[146] Healthcare Ready's Rx Open map shows which pharmacies are open in a disaster area.[164]

**Papers.** To replace lost IDs, start with your birth certificate, from the vital records office of the state where you were born.[165]

- No antibiotics are included in this plan. If you have a specific, foreseeable need, such as remote travel, talk to your own clinician about a standby prescription with written instructions. ### Powered medical devices

- Backup power for medical equipment: a CPAP uses about 170 Wh a night with the humidifier on (about 96 with it off). That is about 170 Wh a day × 3 days = 510 Wh. Keep spare batteries charged, and ask your utility about its list for customers who need power for medical equipment. (SIL, Ready.gov)[3, 6, 7, 8, 10, 14, 15, 16, 26, 95, 99, 100, 176]
- 1 spare battery for each powered medical device: ask the maker which battery fits and how long it runs, and keep it charged. (Ready.gov, SIL)[26, 176]
- 1 battery power station (about 1,070 Wh each) to run the medical equipment through the outage. Your essential load (medical equipment and phones) is about 185 Wh a day, 560 Wh for 3 days; one station gives about 910 Wh after losses, enough for about 4.9 days of that load. A full-size fridge alone would drain one in about 0.9 days. (SIL, retail prices, ENERGY STAR; some amounts are estimates)[3, 6, 7, 8, 10, 14, 15, 16, 99, 100, 137, 144, 176, 177]

- **Power plan for a medical device.** Ask your doctor or supplier how to keep the device running in an outage and how long its battery lasts, and ask your power company to put you on its list for priority restoration (Ready.gov). Keep batteries charged and know where you would go if the power stays off.[26, 94, 95]

### Stress and mental health

#### Stress, mental health and the 988 line

**What helps.** Save 988 in every phone. It offers free, judgment-free support 24/7 for mental health, substance use and more, by call, text or chat.[178] After a disaster, the Disaster Distress Helpline offers crisis counseling 24/7 to anyone in emotional distress. Call or text 1-800-985-5990.[179] Write a short calm plan: what you might feel, what helps you, and who you will call.[180] Oregon's 2 Weeks Ready program even includes practicing mindfulness as a step in getting prepared.[171]

**What to avoid.** Do not keep the news on all day. No one, adult or child, benefits from graphic details or disturbing images.[181] Reach out before things feel unbearable. The 988 line supports people in emotional distress as well as in crisis.[178]

> **Strategic sites, version reviewed 2026-09-26.** Strategic-site classes compiled by Ready Reckoner from public sources: DoD MIRTA installation points, the Sentinel Final EIS (Department of the Air Force), NNSA, the Missile Defense Agency, Census metropolitan areas and centres of population, EIA refinery capacity, BTS port statistics and FEMA's FY2026 UASI allocations. A higher class does not mean an attack is likely. https://github.com/holdTheDoorHoid/ready-reckoner/blob/main/crates/rr-etl/data/strategic_sites.toml

> **USACE National Levee Database.** People behind levees and levee risk ratings from the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers National Levee Database. https://levees.sec.usace.army.mil/

> **USGS National Seismic Hazard Model, version NSHM 2023 grids, Hawaii 2021.R2 grid, PRVI 2025 service.** Earthquake shaking probabilities from the U.S. Geological Survey National Seismic Hazard Model (2023 50-state model data release; Hawaii 2021.R2; Puerto Rico and U.S. Virgin Islands 2025). https://doi.org/10.5066/P9GNPCOD

> **USGS karst and landslide maps.** Karst: Weary, D.J., and Doctor, D.H., 2014, Karst in the United States: A digital map compilation and database, USGS Open-File Report 2014-1156. Landslides: USGS, Slope-Relief Threshold Landslide Susceptibility Models for the United States and Puerto Rico, 2024, doi:10.5066/P13KAGU3 (CC0). https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/2014/1156/

The other data sets were accessed September 26, 2026 too, unless they say otherwise.
