## Your risks

These are the events most likely to reach a household like yours in Maricopa County, Arizona, over the next 10 years. Common events come first: a lost job, a house fire or a week-long outage is far more likely than a disaster from the movies, and the plan is ordered the same way.

### The ones most likely to reach you

#### 1. Heat wave

Nearly every household like yours will go through a heat wave in a ten-year stretch around 2050 (about 17 times a year).[2, 3, 4] Heat is most dangerous for older adults, babies and young children, pregnant women, outdoor workers, and people with heart disease or asthma.[5] Heat and a power cut together are far worse than either alone. One study estimated that a five-day blackout during a heat wave would send about half of Phoenix's residents to emergency care.[6]

**What helps.** Know where you can go to cool off. Dial 2-1-1 to find air conditioning near you.[5] Cover windows with drapes or shades, and weather-strip doors and windows.[7] Take cool showers, wear loose, light-colored clothing, and drink plenty of fluids. Check on family members, older adults and neighbours.[7] Heat can cause power outages, so plan for refrigerated medicines and powered medical devices.[5] In a drought, follow local limits on water use, and make saving water a daily habit.[8]

**What to avoid.** Do not rely on a fan when it is over 90°F indoors. It can make you hotter.[5] Never leave people or pets in a closed car on a warm day. Heat stroke is an emergency. Signs include a body temperature above 103°F, hot, red, dry skin, a fast pulse and confusion. Call 911.[7]

- **What it can do to you:** no power and dangerous heat or cold at home.
- **How bad one can be:** Minor. **How sure we are:** Mostly data.

#### 2. Medical emergency

Of 100 households like yours, about 99 will have someone need emergency care in the next ten years.[9] Injuries and sudden illness are common. In 2024, accidental injuries caused about 58 deaths for every 100,000 people.[10] Emergency departments see about 47 visits for every 100 people each year.[9] In rural areas, ambulances take longer to arrive.[11]

**What helps.** Take a first-aid and bleeding-control class. You may be able to save a life by acting right after an injury, before help arrives.[12] Keep a stocked first-aid kit. Check it regularly and replace anything used or out of date.[13] Keep a list of each person's medicines, doses and allergies with your emergency supplies.[14] Know how to reach 911. Call if you can, and text where that service works.[15] Set up telehealth with your own clinician before you need it.[16]

**What to avoid.** Do not start antibiotics on your own or take someone else's. The wrong medicine can delay the right treatment and cause serious side effects.[17] Do not use expired medicines.[18]

- **What it can do to you:** a medical emergency.
- **How bad one can be:** Serious. **How sure we are:** Based on data.

#### 3. Supply chain disruption

Of 100 households like yours, about 86 (63–98) will find store shelves empty of things they need in the next ten years.[3] Sometimes you cannot get to a store, or the shelves are empty. Winter storms, runs on stores before a storm, curfews and disease outbreaks can all do this. In spring 2020, statewide stay-at-home orders lasted about six weeks in the middle state.[19, 20]

**What helps.** Keep extra food that needs no refrigeration, cooking or water, and choose foods your family will eat.[21] Use the oldest cans first and replace them as you go.[22] Buy a little at a time. Ready.gov asks people to buy supplies slowly, so everyone can get what they need.[23] Keep a manual can opener.[21] The plan counts food in calories, not servings. Many "30-day" meal kits hold only about 1,300 to 1,700 calories a day.[24] That is less than most adults need.[25] Store food somewhere cool and dry. Below 85°F is best.[22]

**What to avoid.** Do not buy everything at once. It strains your budget and the store. Do not buy or eat from cans that bulge, leak, are rusted or are deeply dented.[22] Do not trust a kit's "days" label. Check the calories on the package instead.

- **What it can do to you:** no way to get to a store and gaps in medicine.
- **How bad one can be:** Minor. **How sure we are:** Expert estimate.

#### 4. Local water or gas outage

Of 100 households like yours, about 78 (50–95) will have a water main break or boil-water notice in the next ten years.[3, 26] Not every outage comes from the weather. Equipment failures and cyberattacks can take down the power grid, card payments, or pharmacy and insurance systems for days.[20] Closer to home, a broken water main can bring a boil-water notice. Most notices come from main breaks and repairs.[26]

**What helps.** The same basic supplies cover all of these: water, food, lights, a radio and a backup phone battery.[27] Keep some cash in small bills, because ATMs and credit cards may not work.[28] Write important phone numbers on paper, not only in your phone.[29] Keep a written list of each person's medicines and doses.[14] Protect your accounts with a password manager and two-step login, and back up your files regularly.[30] Install software updates without delay.[31]

**What to avoid.** Do not click links in texts or emails from people you don't know. Scammers can make fake websites.[30] Learn to spot phishing: messages that try to get you to open an attachment or share personal information.[31] During a boil-water notice, do not use water or ice from the fridge dispenser.[32]

- **What it can do to you:** boil-water notices and no tap water.
- **How bad one can be:** Minor. **How sure we are:** Mostly data.

#### 5. Stranded in a vehicle

Of 100 households like yours, about 78 (39–98) will be stranded away from home by a crash, breakdown or shutdown in the next ten years.[3, 33] Police reported about 6.1 million crashes in 2023.[33] Breakdowns, closed roads and storms can also leave you stuck for hours.

**What helps.** Keep a car kit with jumper cables, sand, a flashlight, warm clothes, blankets, water and snacks.[34] Keep the gas tank at least half full.[35] Check the pressure in all your tires, including the spare, at least once a month.[36] Before a winter trip, tell someone where you are going and when you expect to arrive, and ask them to call for help if you are late.[37] If you drive an electric car, know its range and where you can charge along your route.[38, 39]

**What to avoid.** If you are stuck in a snowstorm, staying in the car is often safest. Tie a bright cloth to the antenna. Run the engine about 10 minutes an hour with a window open a little, and keep the exhaust pipe clear of snow.[37] Never drive into a flooded road or around the barriers.[40]

- **What it can do to you:** being stranded away from home.
- **How bad one can be:** Minor. **How sure we are:** Mostly data.

### Rare but severe

These are shown apart, with how likely and how bad as separate columns. A tiny chance times a huge loss would otherwise crowd out everything else, and the plan never lets them take over the budget.

| What | How likely | How bad |

| Nuclear attack | Experts' estimates of a worldwide nuclear catastrophe range from about 1 in 2,000 to about 1 in 400 a year. No reliable estimate exists for effects where you live. The first 24 hours of sheltering inside are covered by your basic supplies.[52, 53] | Very severe |
| Terrorist attack | An attack that disrupts daily life where you live is rare: expert estimates range from about 1 in 10,000 to about 1 in 1,000 a year.[3] | Very severe |

The first advice for a nuclear emergency is to get inside, stay inside and stay tuned.[53] Your three-day supplies already cover that first stretch of sheltering.

These events are very rare. In a 2024 survey, experts and forecasters put the chance of a nuclear catastrophe anywhere in the world before 2045 at 1 to 5 in 100.[52] The right response is simple, and your three-day supplies already cover it.

**What helps.** Get inside, stay inside, stay tuned.[53] Go to a basement or the middle of a large brick or concrete building. Stay there for 24 hours unless there is a fire or another immediate danger, or officials say it is safe to leave. Radiation levels drop fast during the first 24 hours.[53] Turn off fans and air conditioning that pull in outside air.[54] If you were outside, take off your outer layer of clothing. That can remove up to 90% of radioactive material. Then wash with soap and water.[54] If you live near a nuclear power plant, learn whether you are in its planning zone.[51]

**What to avoid.** Do not take potassium iodide unless public health or emergency officials tell you to. It protects only the thyroid.[55] Near a plant, states can give out tablets to people within 10 miles, on official instruction.[56] Do not spend on special gear before your basic supplies are in place.[20]

#### Today or 2050: the climate setting

The climate setting shows your risks as they are today, or as projected for around 2050, meaning the years 2036 to 2065.[20] The projections come from county climate data published by federal agencies.[4] They are projections, not predictions, so the plan shows them as ranges.

**What changes.** The contiguous U.S. has warmed about 60% faster than the planet as a whole since 1970, and temperatures are projected to keep rising.[48] Days with the heaviest rain have become more common, by about 60% in the Northeast and 45% in the Midwest.[48] So the 2050 setting raises the chance of heat and heavy-rain flooding where the data supports it, and can lower the chance of cold snaps and icy days.[20] It changes how often hazards happen, not how long repairs take.[20]

**What stays the same.** Earthquakes, tsunamis and volcanoes are not adjusted, and neither are personal risks like job loss or a house fire. Hurricane frequency is not changed county by county, because the science does not support that.[20]

**What to avoid.** Do not read a 2050 number as a forecast for next year. Plan your supplies for today, and use the 2050 view for long-lived choices, such as where to live or how to build.

### No grid power at home: about 3 days (2–7)

Of 100 households like yours, about 35 (25–70) will lose grid power for a day or more in the next 10 years.[2, 3, 4, 6, 47, 48, 57, 58, 59, 60, 61] Most outages last a few hours. In 2024 the average home lost power for about 11 hours in total, mostly because of hurricanes.[62] After recent hurricanes, getting power back to 95 of every 100 customers who lost it took from about 3 days to nearly 3 weeks.[61]

**What helps.** Have a flashlight for each person.[63] Keep a battery or hand-crank radio, and a backup battery for each phone.[27] Keep the fridge and freezer closed. A closed fridge keeps food cold for about four hours, and a full freezer for about two days.[63] If someone relies on a powered medical device or cold medicine, make a plan with their medical provider now.[63] You can also ask your power company to put you on its list for priority restoration.[14] A battery power station burns no fuel, so it makes no carbon monoxide.[64]

**What to avoid.** Never run a generator inside your home or garage, even with the doors and windows open. Keep it outside, more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents.[64] Put a carbon monoxide alarm on every level of your home.[63] Never heat your home with a gas stove or oven. Unplug electronics, because the power can come back with a surge that damages them.[63]

**Your numbers.** Being ready for 3 days leaves about 9 (6–25) of 100 households like yours facing a longer power cut in the next 10 years. This number mostly depends on how long power cuts from heat waves straining the grid last (expert estimate) and how often heat waves happen where you live (expert estimate).[2, 3, 4, 6, 47, 48, 57, 58, 59, 60, 61]

**What counts toward it:**

- Backup power for medical equipment: a CPAP uses about 170 Wh a night with the humidifier on (about 96 with it off). That is about 170 Wh a day × 3 days = 510 Wh. Keep spare batteries charged, and ask your utility about its list for customers who need power for medical equipment. (SIL, Ready.gov)[2, 3, 4, 6, 14, 47, 48, 57, 58, 59, 60, 61, 63, 65]
- 1 spare battery for each powered medical device: ask the maker which battery fits and how long it runs, and keep it charged. (Ready.gov, SIL)[14, 65]
- 1 battery light: a headlamp or lantern for each person aged 4 and over. Use lights, not candles. (Ready.gov; some amounts are estimates)[27, 63, 66]
- 1 pack of AA or AAA batteries for the lights and the radio: one pack lasts a household about 7 days. Check which sizes your lights use. (Ready.gov; some amounts are estimates)[2, 3, 4, 6, 27, 47, 48, 57, 58, 59, 60, 61, 66]
- 1 battery power station (about 1,070 Wh each) to run the medical equipment through the outage. Your essential load (medical equipment and phones) is about 185 Wh a day, 560 Wh for 3 days; one station gives about 910 Wh after losses, enough for about 4.9 days of that load. A full-size fridge alone would drain one in about 0.9 days. (SIL, retail prices, ENERGY STAR; some amounts are estimates)[2, 3, 4, 6, 47, 48, 57, 58, 59, 60, 61, 65, 66, 67, 68]

**Also worth knowing:**

- Optional: keeping a fridge running takes about 995 Wh a day (a chest freezer about 597) × 3 days = 2,990 Wh. A closed fridge keeps food cold about 4 hours and a full freezer about 48; shelf-stable food avoids the need. (ENERGY STAR, Ready.gov)[2, 3, 4, 6, 21, 47, 48, 57, 58, 59, 60, 61, 63, 68]

### Tap water must be treated: about 5 days (2–10)

Of 100 households like yours, about 35 (15–60) will have to boil or treat their tap water for a day or more in the next 10 years.[2, 3, 26, 47, 48, 69, 70] A boil-water notice means the taps still run, but germs may be in the water. Most notices come from broken water mains and repairs.[26] In Texas the most common notice lasted three to four days, and a few lasted more than a month.[69]

**What helps.** Use bottled water, or bring clear water to a rolling boil for one minute. Boil for three minutes above 5,000 feet, then let it cool.[71] If you cannot boil water, add plain unscented bleach: 8 drops of 6% bleach or 6 drops of 8.25% bleach per gallon. Stir, and wait 30 minutes.[71] Use boiled or bottled water for drinking, cooking, brushing teeth, baby formula and ice. If the power is out too, a camp stove works, but only outdoors.[64]

**What to avoid.** Do not use water or ice from the fridge dispenser while a notice is on. Boil tap water even if it went through a home filter or a filter pitcher.[32] Boiling does not help if the notice warns of chemicals. That is a do-not-drink notice, and you need stored or bottled water instead.[32, 72]

**Your numbers.** Being ready for 5 days leaves about 8 (2–25) of 100 households like yours facing a longer boil-water notice in the next 10 years. This number mostly depends on how long boil-water notices from local water problems last (expert estimate) and how often local water and gas problems happen where you live (expert estimate).[2, 3, 26, 47, 48, 69, 70]

**What counts toward it:**

- For a boil-water notice of up to 5 days: make about 1.5 gallons of drinking water safe each day, about 7.5 gallons in all. To make it safe, boil it for 1 minute (3 minutes above 5,000 feet), or add 8 drops of 6 % unscented bleach per gallon (6 drops of 8.25 %) and wait 30 minutes; double the bleach for cloudy or very cold water. A gas stove can boil water while the gas flows; never use a camp stove or grill indoors. (Ready.gov, CDC, NCA5 Atlas, EPA; some amounts are estimates)[1, 2, 3, 26, 47, 48, 64, 66, 69, 70, 71, 72, 73, 74]
- 1 bottle of plain unscented bleach (5 to 9 % sodium hypochlorite, not the splashless kind), for disinfecting water and cleaning: one bottle lasts a household about 14 days. Bleach weakens over time, so buy fresh when you rotate your water. (CDC, Ready Reckoner research, EPA; some amounts are estimates)[2, 3, 24, 26, 47, 48, 66, 69, 70, 71, 73, 75]

**Also worth knowing:**

- If you boil on a camp stove outdoors, about 1 lb of propane brings 28 L to a boil (about 25 to 30 L a pound). Never use a camp stove indoors, and keep no more than 2 one-pound cylinders inside. (EIA, local fire code, CDC; some amounts are estimates)[64, 66, 76, 77]

### No tap water at all: about 5 days (2–10)

Of 100 households like yours, about 25 (15–50) will have no usable tap water for a day or more in the next 10 years.[2, 3, 26, 43, 58, 78] Sometimes no water comes out of the tap, or officials say not to use it at all. Earthquakes, hard freezes, hurricanes and chemical spills can do this. After Hurricane Helene in 2024, Asheville, North Carolina, waited about seven weeks for safe tap water.[78] If your well has an electric pump, every power cut is also a water cut.[20]

**What helps.** Store at least one gallon per person per day, for drinking and keeping clean.[1] Fill clean bottles or jugs with tap water and replace it every six months.[73] You can pick a level in the plan. About 3 liters a day covers drinking to survive, one gallon is the basic default, and about 4 gallons (15 liters) is the humanitarian minimum for drinking, cooking and washing.[79] A tank water heater holds 20 to 80 gallons of clean water.[80, 81] For long outages, a filter and a source of raw water go further than more jugs.[20] Filters do not remove viruses, so add a disinfectant such as chlorine dioxide after filtering.[72] A two-bucket toilet keeps waste safe when toilets cannot flush.[82]

**What to avoid.** Do not store water in containers that held chemicals.[73] Milk jugs seal poorly and turn brittle.[83] Never drink pool or spa water. Use it only for washing and cleaning.[81]

**Your numbers.** Being ready for 5 days leaves about 7 (3–15) of 100 households like yours facing a longer water outage in the next 10 years. This number mostly depends on how often chemical spills and releases happen where you live (expert estimate) and how long water outages from chemical releases last (expert estimate).[2, 3, 26, 43, 58, 78]

**What counts toward it:**

- 1 person × 1.75 gallons a day × 5 days = 8.8 gallons. Your county has at least 30 days a year at 95 °F or more, and drinking water can double in heat, so each person gets 1.75 gallons a day. Replace water you store yourself every 6 months. (Ready.gov, CDC, NCA5 Atlas; some amounts are estimates)[1, 2, 3, 26, 43, 58, 66, 73, 74, 78]
- A two-bucket toilet for when toilets can't flush: 2 sturdy buckets with a seat, one for pee and one for poo. (RDPO, Oregon OEM)[82, 84]
- Toilet bags: about 0.45 heavy garbage bags a person a day (0.3 to 0.6), counting both bags of a double-bagged load: 1 person × 5 days = 3 bags. Fill halfway, tie, double-bag, and store away from food, water, children and pets. Don't put them in curbside trash or bury them unless officials say so. (RDPO, Oregon OEM; some amounts are estimates)[2, 3, 26, 43, 58, 66, 78, 82, 84]
- Cover material for the poo bucket (sawdust, shredded paper, dry leaves or pet bedding): about 1 cup a person a day (0.5 to 1.5) × 1 person × 5 days = 5 cups, about 0.3 gallons. (RDPO; some amounts are estimates)[2, 3, 26, 43, 58, 66, 78, 82]

**Also worth knowing:**

- Free first step: fill clean drink bottles you already have with tap water, about 5.3 gallons (3 days of your water, up to the 6 gallons a household can usually gather). Not milk jugs: they leak. Label them and replace the water every 6 months. (Ready.gov, CDC, NCA5 Atlas, Ready Reckoner research; some amounts are estimates)[1, 20, 66, 73, 74, 83]

### Dangerous heat or cold indoors: about 3 days (2–5)

Of 100 households like yours, about 35 (20–65) will face dangerous heat or cold at home with no working cooling or heating for a day or more in the next 10 years.[2, 3, 4, 6, 47, 48, 57, 58, 59, 60, 61] Heat and cold become dangerous indoors when the power fails or the heating or air conditioning stops. **What helps.** Agree who checks on whom, especially older neighbours and anyone who lives alone.[5, 37] In heat, go where there is air conditioning. **What to avoid.** Fans move air but do not lower body temperature.[7] Above 90°F indoors, a fan can make you hotter.[5] Never heat your home with a gas oven, and never use a grill or camp stove indoors.[64] Keep space heaters 3 feet from anything that can burn.[91] Never leave people or pets in a closed car on a warm day.[7]

**Your numbers.** Being ready for 3 days leaves about 7 (4–20) of 100 households like yours facing a longer spell of dangerous heat or cold in the next 10 years. A power cut during a heat wave also stops your air conditioning.[2, 3, 4, 6, 47, 48, 57, 58, 59, 60, 61]

**What counts toward it:**

- 1 battery fan: 1 for the home. Fans help only while it is below 90 °F indoors; above that, go to a cooling centre. (CDC; some amounts are estimates)[5, 66]

- A heat plan: find your nearest cooling centre (dial 2-1-1), pick the coolest room, cover sunny windows, and agree who checks on whom. Never leave people or pets in a closed car. (CDC, Ready.gov)[5, 7]

### Medication and medical-supply continuity: about 2 weeks (10 days to 1 month)

Of 100 households like yours, about 35 (25–65) will have trouble getting medicine for 3 days or more in the next 10 years.[2, 3, 26, 44, 45, 47, 48, 49] In a disaster, pharmacies and roads can close, and pharmacy computer systems can go down.[20] Anyone who takes a daily medicine needs a way to keep taking it.

**Your numbers.** Being ready for 2 weeks leaves about 8 (5–15) of 100 households like yours facing a longer gap in medicine in the next 10 years. - No antibiotics are included in this plan. If you have a specific, foreseeable need, such as remote travel, talk to your own clinician about a standby prescription with written instructions. Never use fish or pet antibiotics, never share prescriptions, and don't use expired ones. (CDC, FDA)[17, 18, 99, 100]

### Must leave home quickly

Of 100 households like yours, about 15 (10–25) will have to leave home quickly at least once in the next 10 years.[2, 3, 4, 26, 43, 47, 48, 49, 50] Wildfires, floods, hurricanes, chemical spills and house fires can force you to leave. Sometimes you get days of warning, sometimes only minutes. In the year before December 2022, about 16 of every 1,000 U.S. households were forced from home by a disaster.[107]

**What helps.** Plan where you would go and more than one way to get there.[35] Keep the gas tank at least half full, and full when an evacuation seems likely.[35] Pack a go-bag for each person. CDC's short list is phone, chargers, medicines, ID, cash and the car emergency kit.[108] Plan for pets, because public shelters may take only service animals.[35] Agree on an if-then rule ahead of time, such as "If our zone gets a warning, we leave within 30 minutes." Plans written this way help people follow through.[109] Leave a note saying when you left and where you are going.[35]

**What to avoid.** Many people wait to check with others before they act on a warning.[110] Always follow the instructions of local officials, and leave early enough to avoid being trapped by bad weather.[35] Never walk or drive through floodwater. Six inches of moving water can knock you down.[111]

**Your numbers.** Warning can be as short as a few minutes or as long as 3 days. Plan to be away for about 2 days.[2, 3, 4, 26, 43, 47, 48, 49, 50]

**What counts toward it:**

- 1 go-bag, one for each person: water, food, medicines, copies of documents, a phone charger, cash, a flashlight, a whistle, a change of clothes and sturdy shoes. Plan where you would stay for about 2 days. (Washington EMD, Ready.gov, CDC; some amounts are estimates)[2, 3, 4, 26, 27, 43, 47, 48, 49, 50, 66, 108, 112]
- Water for the go-bags, the Red Cross three-day supply for leaving home: about 1.8 gallons a day for the 1 person × 2 days = 3.5 gallons. If you leave on foot, carry what you can (about 2.5 L each) and a filter; the rest goes in the car. - Food for the go-bags: about 2,262 kcal a day × 2 days = 4,500 kcal of food that needs no cooking or refrigeration. (Red Cross, Washington EMD, Dietary Guidelines)[2, 3, 4, 25, 26, 43, 47, 48, 49, 50, 92, 112]

### Stranded away from home

Of 100 households like yours, about 80 (45–100) will have someone stranded away from home at least once in the next 10 years.[3, 33] An earthquake, a storm or a transit shutdown can leave you far from home with roads or trains closed. You may need to stay where you are, or walk home.

**What helps.** Ready.gov has advised being ready to stay at work for at least 24 hours, with food, water, medicines and comfortable walking shoes.[113] Keep a small get-home bag at work or in the car: water, snacks, a flashlight, a phone battery, a whistle, a dust mask and a paper map.[27] Plan your route home and walk part of it once. Road engineers plan for people walking about 2 to 2.4 miles an hour, so 10 miles can take 4 to 5 hours.[114] In heat, drink about 1 cup of water every 15 to 20 minutes while you walk.[115] Include your children's school in your family plan, and agree who will pick them up.[105]

**What to avoid.** Do not force down water when you are not thirsty. Too much water can be harmful.[116] In heat, do not drink more than 6 cups an hour.[115] If you are stuck in a car in a snowstorm, staying in the car is often safest. Run the engine about 10 minutes an hour with a window cracked, and keep the exhaust pipe clear of snow.[37]

**Your numbers.** The 9-mile (14 km) trip is about 3 hours on foot; carry about 1 litres of water for it in hot weather. The get-home bag is sized by the walk home at about 3 miles an hour, with about half a litre of water per hour in hot weather.[3, 33]

**What counts toward it:**

- Water for the walk home: 2.9 hours × about 0.71 L an hour in heat = 2.1 L. (FHWA, NIOSH; some amounts are estimates)[66, 114, 115]

### Medical emergency when help is slow

Of 100 households like yours, about 100 (95–100) will have a medical emergency at least once in the next 10 years.[3, 9] Emergency departments see about 47 visits for every 100 people each year.[9] Ambulances take longer to reach rural homes.[11] After a disaster, emergency medical services are in high demand and help may be delayed.[117]

**What helps.** Learn how to stop serious bleeding. Hospitals, fire departments and health departments often offer free or low-cost classes.[12] Keep a first-aid kit at home and in the car. **What to avoid.** Do not save antibiotics for later or take someone else's. The wrong medicine can delay the right treatment.[17] Do not wait for a disaster to learn first aid. People with emergency training before the World Trade Center attack were less likely to develop PTSD afterward.[120]

**What counts toward it:**

- 1 oral thermometer (non-mercury, non-glass; it is on the Red Cross kit list). (Red Cross)[13]

### House fire

Of 100 households like yours, about 5 (3–8) will have a home fire at least once in the next 10 years.[3, 49] There were about 345,000 home fires in the United States in 2023.[49] Cooking is by far the leading cause of home fires and home fire injuries.[123]

**What helps.** Put smoke alarms inside and outside sleeping areas and on every level of your home. Test them every month.[124] Make an escape plan with two ways out of each room, and practice it twice a year.[125] Cooking left alone is the top cause of kitchen fires that spread beyond the pan, so stay with your cooking. Keep a pan lid or baking sheet nearby to cover a pan that catches fire.[123] Keep anything that can burn at least 3 feet from heaters, fireplaces and candles.[91] A multipurpose extinguisher labeled "A-B-C" works on most home fires.[126]

**What to avoid.** If a person or pet is still inside, get out, call 911, and tell firefighters where they are.[125] Never heat your home with an oven.[91] Turn off space heaters when you leave the room or go to bed.[91] Replace 9-volt alarm batteries at least once a year.[124]

**What counts toward it:**

- A fire escape plan: two ways out of every room, a meeting spot outside, and a practice run with everyone. In a building, know both stairways and never use the elevator in a fire. Test your smoke alarms every month. (Ready.gov)[125]
- No carbon monoxide alarm yet: put one on every level where people sleep (1 for a home like yours), with battery backup. Replace them every 5 years or as the maker says. (CDC, Ready.gov; some amounts are estimates)[63, 64, 66]
- No fire extinguisher yet: 1 extinguisher, one on each level including the kitchen's. Learn how to use one before you need it. (USFA; some amounts are estimates)[66, 126]
- 1 escape ladder for an upstairs bedroom window, in case the stairs are blocked by fire. Keep it by the window and practise with it. (Ready.gov; some amounts are estimates)[66, 125]

### Free steps

Free actions come first in every plan, because they cost nothing and cover a lot. In FEMA's 2024 survey, cost was the most common reason people gave for not preparing, and "I don't know what else to do" came second.[141] This tier answers both.

**What is in it.** Turn on emergency alerts on every phone.[102] Write each person a contact card, and pick an out-of-state contact.[29, 101] Fill clean bottles with tap water.[73] Test smoke alarms every month.[124] Refill medicines early, and keep a written list of them.[14] Photograph your belongings and copy key documents.[106] Swap phone numbers with two neighbours. About 7 in 10 people expect help from friends and family, but fewer than 2 in 10 had heard how to help their neighbours.[141] Washington's guide also starts with a free step: talk with your family and make a plan.[112]

### Three days

### Extras for the hazards you face

These help with one hazard rather than a whole need, so they sit outside the budget. Consider them once the steps above are done.

- **Plastic sheeting and duct tape to shelter in place** (usually $43–70 per kit): Thick plastic sheeting and duct tape to seal a room's windows, doors and vents if officials tell you to shelter in place from a chemical release (Ready.gov). Cut the sheets several inches wider than each opening ahead of time and label them.[27, 67, 147, 148]
- **Personal radiation dosimeter card** (usually $25 per dosimeter): A card that changes color to show how much radiation it has absorbed. It is optional: in a radiation emergency, official instructions on when to shelter and when to leave matter far more than your own reading.[54, 67]
- **Shielded (Faraday) bag for spare electronics** (usually $38–98 per bag): A metal-lined bag for a spare radio, phone or power bank. The effects of an electromagnetic pulse on home electronics are highly uncertain, so this is optional and never a priority; a battery radio and general supplies are the main protection.[20, 52, 67]
- **Indoor room thermometer** (usually $2–15 per thermometer): A simple room thermometer, so you know when it is too hot for a fan to help (90°F) or too cold for an infant to sleep, and when to leave for a cooling center or warming shelter (CDC).[5, 37, 67]

### Contacts and meeting places

- **Make a family emergency plan.** Agree how you will reach each other, where you will meet if you are separated, and who picks up the children (Ready.gov). Include each person's medical needs, pets and school or work plans, then practice it.[105, 149]
- **Choose an out-of-area contact everyone can text.** Pick one relative or friend in another state whom everyone in the household will text to say they are safe (Ready.gov). Texts often get through when calls do not, and they use less battery.[101, 105]
- **Written contact card for each person.** Write key phone numbers on a card for each person's wallet or bag: family, the out-of-area contact, work, school, doctor and 988. Ready.gov advises storing important numbers somewhere besides your phone, and offers a fillable card.[29, 106, 149]
- **Turn on emergency alerts on every phone.** Check that Wireless Emergency Alerts are switched on in each phone's settings. They are free and need no sign-up (Ready.gov), but a phone only gets them when it is on, not in airplane mode, and served by a carrier that takes part (FCC).[102, 150]
- **Sign up for your county's emergency alerts.** Sign up for your county or city's emergency text and email alerts, which carry local warnings such as evacuation orders and boil-water notices. Ready.gov's planning questions start with how you will receive alerts and warnings.[102, 105]
- **Know how to reach 911 by call or text.** Always call 911 when you can, because a voice call gives the call center more information; text-to-911 works only in some places (FCC). Find out whether your county accepts texts, which helps when speaking is unsafe or impossible.[15]

### Leaving home: triggers and routes

About 15 of 100 households like yours have to leave home quickly at least once in 10 years. Warning can be 1 minute to 3 days ahead. Plan to be away for about 2 days.[2, 3, 4, 26, 43, 47, 48, 49, 50]

- **Know your evacuation zone, routes and where you would go.** Many states set evacuation zones and routes; look up yours on your state and county emergency management websites (Ready.gov). Pick where you would go: a friend's or relative's home out of the area, a pet-friendly hotel, or a public shelter.[35, 151, 152, 153]
- **Agree if-then triggers for leaving.** Write short promises such as 'If the county issues an evacuation warning for our zone, we leave within 30 minutes; the bag is by the door; Sam gets the kids.' Plans in if-then form make people much more likely to follow through.[109, 154]
- **Write a go-or-stay card for each likely hazard.** For each hazard your plan names, write one card that says when you leave, when you stay and shelter, and what you do first. People who get complete warnings spend less time checking with others before acting, and a card decided in advance works the same way.[110, 154]
- **Run ten-minute drills twice a year.** Practice a fire escape, grabbing the go-bag against a timer, or Drop, Cover and Hold On, for ten minutes at a time. Ready.gov advises practicing your home fire escape plan twice a year, and people who have drilled act faster and cope better in real emergencies.[120, 125, 155]
- **Keep the gas tank at least half full.** Ready.gov advises keeping at least half a tank of gas at all times in case you have to leave suddenly, and a full tank when an evacuation looks likely. Gas stations close or run dry when the power is out.[34, 35]
- **Never walk or drive through floodwater.** Just six inches of moving water can knock you down and one foot can sweep a vehicle away (Ready.gov). Turn around and find another way, and move to higher ground or a higher floor if water rises.[40, 111]
- **Pick your tornado shelter spot.** Choose where you will go when a tornado warning sounds: a safe room or storm shelter if you have one, otherwise a small, interior, windowless room or basement on the lowest floor of a sturdy building (Ready.gov). If you live in a mobile home, plan which sturdy building you would go to instead.[156]
- **Know Drop, Cover and Hold On.** When shaking starts, drop to your hands and knees, cover your head and neck, and hold on until it stops (Ready.gov). Stay inside and away from doorways; after the shaking, expect aftershocks and check for gas leaks and damage.[101, 155]

#### Drills and if-then plans

Supplies help most when you already know what to do with them. Practice and simple rules made in advance turn a plan into action.

**If-then plans.** An if-then plan links a trigger to an action: "If the county issues an evacuation warning for our zone, we leave within 30 minutes." Plans written this way help people follow through.[109] When a warning comes, people often wait to confirm it with others before they act.[110] A trigger you agreed on earlier leaves less to decide in the moment. Write one for each likely hazard, and add who picks up the children and where you will meet.[105]

**Drills.** In New Zealand, people who took part in earthquake drills knew the right actions better, used them more during real shaking, and did more to prepare.[155] Among people who got out of the World Trade Center on 9/11, those with earlier emergency training were less likely to develop PTSD.[120] Practice your fire escape plan twice a year.[125] Other ten-minute drills: Drop, Cover and Hold On, a night without power, grabbing the go-bag against a timer, and sealing a room to shelter in place.[154]

**What to avoid.** Do not skip drills because they feel silly. Each one you finish counts toward your plan.

### School, work and getting home

- **Plan and walk part of your route home.** Map two walking routes from work to home and walk part of one. Plan on about 2 to 2.4 miles an hour with a loaded bag, and about 24 to 32 ounces of water an hour in heat, so a 12-mile walk takes five to six hours.[24, 114, 115]
- **Know your workplace's emergency plan.** Ask how your workplace would shelter people if roads closed; Ready.gov's work-kit advice is to be ready to shelter at work for at least 24 hours. Keep your medicines, walking shoes and a phone charger there.[113]
- **Check the spare tire and jack.** Find the spare tire, jack and wrench, and check the spare's pressure when you check the others. A flat tire is a common way to be stranded, and it is easier to learn the steps on a dry day.[20, 36]

### Neighbors

- **Get to know two neighbours and swap phone numbers.** Introduce yourself to at least two neighbours and save each other's numbers. After the 2011 Japan tsunami, places with stronger social ties had fewer deaths, especially among older and low-income people. About 7 in 10 people expect help from friends and family first (FEMA's 2024 survey).[141, 157, 158]
- **Agree who checks on whom.** Agree with neighbours or relatives who checks on whom during heat waves, storms and outages, especially older people who live alone. In the 1995 Chicago heat wave, living alone and not leaving home each day made death far more likely, and regular social contact was protective.[5, 90, 159]
- **Make a simple street contact list.** With willing neighbours, list names, phone numbers and anything useful in an emergency: who needs help to evacuate, who has a generator or medical skills, and who has pets. State programs such as Listos California offer templates for exactly this.[159, 160]
- **Join or start a block group, or take CERT training.** Join a neighbourhood preparedness group or take free Community Emergency Response Team (CERT) training, which teaches fire safety, light search and rescue, team organization and disaster first aid (Ready.gov). Trained neighbours can help until responders arrive.[118, 120, 160]

#### Neighbours and mutual aid

Neighbours are a supply, and the plan counts them. After Japan's 2011 tsunami, towns with stronger community ties had lower death rates.[157] In neighbourhoods flooded by the same tsunami, strong ties mattered most for older and lower-income people.[158] In the 1995 Chicago heat wave, living alone raised the risk of dying.[90]

**The gap.** About 7 in 10 people expect help from friends and family in a disaster, but fewer than 2 in 10 had heard how to help their neighbours.[141]

**What helps.** Swap phone numbers with two neighbours. Agree who checks on whom, especially older neighbours and anyone who lives alone. Make a simple contact list for your street. Give a trusted person a spare key, and tell them where your supplies are and how to use any medical equipment.[159] Host a block party, since strong communities start with prepared neighbours.[160] Take CERT training to learn fire safety, light search and rescue, and disaster first aid.[118]

**What to avoid.** Do not wait for a disaster to meet the people next door. A short hello now makes it easier to knock on a door later.

## Documents and money

Keep paper copies in a waterproof pouch and photos you can reach from any phone. FEMA's Emergency Financial First Aid Kit groups them in four parts.[106]

- [ ] **Who you are:** photo IDs, birth certificates, Social Security cards, passports, and pet records.
- [ ] **Money and legal papers:** insurance policies, the lease or deed, bank and card contact numbers (not PINs), and recent tax returns.
- [ ] **Medical papers:** insurance cards, the written medicine list, prescriptions, and vaccination records.
- [ ] **Contacts:** family, doctors, the insurance agent, the landlord or lender, and employers.

- **Copy and protect your key documents.** Use FEMA's Emergency Financial First Aid Kit to gather identification, financial and legal papers, insurance, medical information and contacts. Keep phone photos, paper copies in a waterproof bag, and a copy with someone you trust or in secure online storage.[27, 28, 106]
- **Photograph or video your home and belongings.** Walk through your home recording each room, the contents of closets and drawers, and serial numbers of costly items, as the first step of FEMA's financial kit. It makes insurance and aid claims faster after a fire or flood.[106]
- **Fire-resistant, waterproof document pouch.** A fire-resistant, water-resistant pouch or small case for paper copies of key documents, a USB drive and some cash, easy to grab when you leave (FEMA's financial kit suggests fire- and waterproof storage).[67, 106]

### Insurance questions

- Home and renters policies usually don't cover floods. Decide on flood insurance before you need it: a new policy usually starts 30 days after you buy it. It pays up to $250,000 for the building and $100,000 for belongings, and renters can buy cover for belongings only. (FEMA, FloodSmart, Ready.gov)[28, 142, 143]
- A standard home policy does not cover earthquake damage. Decide whether earthquake insurance is worth its deductible where you live. (Ready.gov)[101]

- **Review your insurance: home or renters, flood, earthquake.** Check what your policy covers and decide on the gaps: standard home and renters policies do not cover flood or earthquake damage (Ready.gov), and a new federal flood policy usually takes 30 days to start (FEMA). Renters can buy contents-only flood cover.[28, 101, 111, 142, 143]
- **Renters: get a renters-insurance quote.** Renters are about half as likely as owners to have insurance (FEMA's 2024 survey), yet a fire or flood can take everything they own and force a move. Get a quote for renters insurance with additional living expenses, then decide.[28, 106, 141]

#### Preparing when you rent

Renters face disasters with less cushion. In FEMA's 2024 survey, 43 of every 100 renters had insurance, compared with 87 of every 100 owners. Renters were also less likely to have money set aside.[141] After a disaster, renters are more likely to be out of their homes for a long time. Nearly 1 in 4 displaced renter households never went back, compared with 1 in 10 owners.[107]

**What helps.** Get renters insurance if you do not have it, and keep it up to date.[28, 106] Standard renters policies usually do not cover floods, but you can buy flood coverage for your belongings.[143] Photograph your belongings and keep copies of your lease and key documents away from home.[106] Most preparation needs no drilling or permission: water in bottles, a go-bag, a contact card and neighbours' numbers. For power, a battery power station works indoors because it burns no fuel.[64]

**What to avoid.** Do not run a generator on a balcony or near windows. It must be outdoors, more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents.[64] Do not assume the building has a plan. Ask where the exits, shut-offs and meeting points are.

### Cash

- Cash in small bills, kept with your documents, because ATMs and cards may not work in an outage: about $100 to start, or enough for about 3 days of basics (food, fuel, medicine) at your own daily spending. No agency gives a dollar amount. (Ready.gov, FEMA; some amounts are estimates)[2, 3, 28, 47, 48, 66, 106]

### Savings

Of 100 households like yours, about 20 (10–40) will have an income gap of more than 3 months in the next 10 years. The goal is about 7 months of expenses (about $18,200); you have 2 months saved. Your supplies plan is done by month 49. After that, your $40 a month for supplies could go here, reaching the goal in about 27 years. This is a savings goal, kept separate from the supplies budget.

- **Start an emergency fund, even a small one.** Set up an automatic transfer, however small, into a savings account you keep for emergencies. The CFPB says the right amount depends on your situation; planners often suggest working toward three to six months of expenses (FINRA), and the plan shows a target for your household.[135, 136, 137, 139]
- **Know how to file for unemployment in your state.** Find your state's unemployment insurance website and what it needs from you, so you can file on the first day if a job ends (Department of Labor). Keep pay stubs and employer details with your documents.[41, 138]
- **Write a bare-bones budget you could switch to.** List the spending you could cut first if income stopped, and what your essential costs are each month. Knowing that number tells you how far your savings would stretch and sets your emergency-fund goal.[20, 136]

## Special needs

### Medicine

- 1 person takes prescription medicine every day: keep 14 days of it on hand. Keep a written list of each medicine, what it is for and the dose. After a disaster declaration many states let pharmacies give an emergency refill of up to 30 days; ask your pharmacist what yours allows. (Florida DEM, CDC, Red Cross, Healthcare Ready)[2, 3, 26, 44, 45, 47, 48, 49, 92, 93, 94, 95, 98]

- **Refill when 7 days of medicine remain.** Order each refill when a week of medicine is left, so you always hold at least 7 days on hand; CDC and the Red Cross both advise a week or more for emergencies. Ask whether your plan allows 90-day fills.[18, 92, 98]
- **Ask your prescriber and pharmacist about an emergency supply.** Ask how to build an emergency supply of your medicines and what your state allows: in a declared emergency some states let pharmacists give up to a 30-day refill. If you have no insurance, the federal Emergency Prescription Assistance Program can help when it is activated after a disaster.[14, 95, 96, 98]
- **Written medicine list for each person.** A paper list of each person's medicines with the dose their prescriber set, what each one is for, allergies, and the prescriber's and pharmacy's phone numbers. Keep one copy in the go-bag and one with your documents (Ready.gov).[14, 106]
- **Set up telehealth and pharmacy accounts now.** Create your logins for your insurer's or clinic's telehealth service and your pharmacy's app or website while it is easy. In an emergency you can then reach your own clinician and move a prescription without traveling.[16, 24]

### Antibiotics

- No antibiotics are included in this plan. If you have a specific, foreseeable need, such as remote travel, talk to your own clinician about a standby prescription with written instructions. Never use fish or pet antibiotics, never share prescriptions, and don't use expired ones. (CDC, FDA)[17, 18, 99, 100]

#### Antibiotics and emergency antibiotic kits

Some companies sell "emergency antibiotic kits" for disaster preparedness. Ready Reckoner never sets an antibiotic quantity. Here is why, and what to do instead.

**What the kits are.** By federal law, antibiotics for people need a prescription.[161] The kits are sold through an online questionnaire that a prescriber signs off, and they typically cost $300 to $400. A 2026 medical review found that this model prescribes for an illness no one has diagnosed. That invites the wrong drug, the wrong dose and the wrong length of treatment. It can also cause side effects and resistant infections.[16]

**When a standby prescription can make sense.** CDC notes that travelers often carry some prescribed medicines for specific risks where they are going.[100] The same review suggests telehealth with a clinician who knows you, and keeping prescriptions made in advance for specific high-risk cases.[16] If a specific situation worries you, ask your own clinician.

**What to avoid.** Do not save antibiotics for later, share them, or take someone else's.[17] Do not use expired antibiotics. They can fail to treat an infection.[18] Never use aquarium, bird or other animal antibiotics. The FDA calls these products unapproved and unsafe.[99]

### Powered medical devices

- Backup power for medical equipment: a CPAP uses about 170 Wh a night with the humidifier on (about 96 with it off). That is about 170 Wh a day × 3 days = 510 Wh. Keep spare batteries charged, and ask your utility about its list for customers who need power for medical equipment. (SIL, Ready.gov)[2, 3, 4, 6, 14, 47, 48, 57, 58, 59, 60, 61, 63, 65]
- 1 spare battery for each powered medical device: ask the maker which battery fits and how long it runs, and keep it charged. (Ready.gov, SIL)[14, 65]
- 1 battery power station (about 1,070 Wh each) to run the medical equipment through the outage. Your essential load (medical equipment and phones) is about 185 Wh a day, 560 Wh for 3 days; one station gives about 910 Wh after losses, enough for about 4.9 days of that load. A full-size fridge alone would drain one in about 0.9 days. (SIL, retail prices, ENERGY STAR; some amounts are estimates)[2, 3, 4, 6, 47, 48, 57, 58, 59, 60, 61, 65, 66, 67, 68]

- **Power plan for a medical device.** Ask your doctor or supplier how to keep the device running in an outage and how long its battery lasts, and ask your power company to put you on its list for priority restoration (Ready.gov). Keep batteries charged and know where you would go if the power stays off.[14, 63, 64]

### Stress and mental health

#### Stress, mental health and the 988 line

Stress before, during and after a disaster is normal. In FEMA's 2024 survey, the most common worries were health and paying bills, well ahead of disasters.[141] Preparation helps with more than supplies. Among people who got out of the World Trade Center on 9/11, those with earlier emergency training were less likely to develop PTSD.[120]

**What helps.** Save 988 in every phone. It offers free, judgment-free support 24/7 for mental health, substance use and more, by call, text or chat.[119] After a disaster, the Disaster Distress Helpline offers crisis counseling 24/7 to anyone in emotional distress. Call or text 1-800-985-5990.[162] Write a short calm plan: what you might feel, what helps you, and who you will call.[154] Oregon's 2 Weeks Ready program even includes practicing mindfulness as a step in getting prepared.[117]

**What to avoid.** Do not keep the news on all day. No one, adult or child, benefits from graphic details or disturbing images.[163] Do not wait until things feel unbearable to reach out. The 988 line supports people in emotional distress as well as in crisis.[119]

#### Rotating supplies through everyday use

The easiest supplies to keep fresh are the ones you use. Store the foods your household already eats, use the oldest first, and replace them as you shop.[22, 166]

**What to check, and when.** Replace water you bottled yourself every six months.[73] Look through your pantry every few weeks and use cans you have had for a while.[22] Dates on most foods are about quality, not safety.
