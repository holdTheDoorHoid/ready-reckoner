## Your risks

What could reach a household like yours in Cook County, Illinois over the next 10 years: the likeliest events, and the rarer ones that can kill, most likely first. A lost job or a week-long outage is far more likely than a disaster from the movies, and the plan is ordered the same way.

### What to know and do

#### 1. Cold wave

Nearly every household like yours will go through a spell of dangerous cold in the next ten years (about 3.7 times a year).[2]

**What helps.** Keep extra blankets, sleeping bags and warm coats, and pick one room to keep warm.[3] If you cannot keep your home warm, go to a warming center. Dial 2-1-1 to find one.[4] Learn how to keep pipes from freezing, and test smoke and carbon monoxide alarms before winter.[5] Keep a car kit with jumper cables, sand, a flashlight, warm clothes, blankets, water and snacks, and keep the gas tank full.[5] Check on older neighbours often.[3] Know the signs of hypothermia, such as shivering, confusion and slurred speech. A body temperature below 95°F is an emergency. Warm the center of the body first.[5]

**What to avoid.** Never heat your home with a gas stove or oven. Use generators and grills only outdoors, away from windows.[5] Choose electric space heaters with automatic shut-off, and keep them 3 feet from anything that can burn.[3, 6] Do not overdo it when you shovel snow.[5]

**How bad:** Minor. **How sure:** Based on data.

#### 2. Heat wave

Nearly every household like yours will go through a heat wave in the next ten years (about 2.1 times a year).[2]

**What helps.** Know where you can go to cool off. Dial 2-1-1 to find air conditioning near you.[7] Cover windows with drapes or shades, and weather-strip doors and windows.[8] Take cool showers, wear loose, light-colored clothing, and drink plenty of fluids. Check on family members, older adults and neighbours.[8] Heat can cause power outages, so plan for refrigerated medicines and powered medical devices.[7]

**What to avoid.** Do not rely on a fan when it is over 90°F indoors. It can make you hotter.[7] Never leave people or pets in a closed car on a warm day.[8] Heat stroke is an emergency. Signs include a body temperature of 103°F or more, hot, red skin that may be dry or damp, a fast, strong pulse, confusion or passing out. Call 911, move the person somewhere cooler, cool them with cool cloths or a cool bath, and give them nothing to drink.[9]

**How bad:** Minor. **How sure:** Based on data.

#### 3. Medical emergency

Of 100 households like yours, about 99 will have someone need emergency care in the next ten years.[10]

**What helps.** Take a first-aid and bleeding-control class. You may be able to save a life by acting right after an injury, before help arrives.[11] If a teen or adult collapses and their heart stops, call 911 and push hard and fast in the center of the chest. This hands-only CPR needs no training. Note where the nearest heart defibrillator (AED) is; it talks you through each step.[12] Keep a stocked first-aid kit. Check it regularly and replace anything used or out of date.[13] Keep a list of each person's medicines, doses and allergies with your emergency supplies.[14] Know how to reach 911. Call if you can, and text where that service works.[15]

**What to avoid.** Do not start antibiotics on your own or take someone else's. The wrong medicine can delay the right treatment and cause serious side effects.[16] Do not use expired medicines.[17]

**How bad:** Serious. **How sure:** Based on data.

#### 4. Strong wind

Of 100 households like yours, about 95 (59 to nearly all) will lose power or have damage in a windstorm in the next ten years.[2, 18]

**What helps.** Pick your shelter spot now. A safe room or storm shelter is best.[19] In an apartment building, pick an inside hallway, stairwell or small room with no windows, as low in the building as you can get to quickly. Stay off the elevators.[20, 21] Sign up for your community's warnings, and learn what your sirens sound like.[19] Know the signs of a tornado: a rotating funnel cloud, a cloud of debris, or a roar like a freight train. In your shelter, protect your head and neck with your arms, and pile blankets or cushions around you.[19] When thunder roars, go indoors. Get into a building or a car with a roof.[22]

**What to avoid.** Stay away from windows, doors and outside walls.[19] There is no safe place in a car during a tornado, only less dangerous ones. If you are caught, stay buckled in and keep your head below the windows.[19] During a thunderstorm, avoid running water and electronics plugged into the wall.[22]

**How bad:** Minor. **How sure:** Rough data.

#### 5. Supply chain disruption

Of 100 households like yours, about 86 (63–98) will find store shelves empty of things they need in the next ten years.[18]

**What helps.** Keep extra food that needs no refrigeration, cooking or water, and choose foods your family will eat.[23] Use the oldest cans first and replace them as you go.[24] Buy a little at a time. Ready.gov asks people to buy supplies slowly, so everyone can get what they need.[25] Keep a manual can opener.[23] The plan counts food in calories, not servings. Many "30-day" meal kits hold only about 1,300 to 1,700 calories a day.[26] That is less than most adults need.[27] Store food somewhere cool and dry. Below 85°F is best.[24]

**What to avoid.** Do not buy everything at once. It strains your budget and the store. Do not buy or eat from cans that bulge, leak, are rusted or are deeply dented.[24] Do not trust a kit's "days" label. Check the calories on the package instead.

**How bad:** Minor. **How sure:** Expert estimate.

#### 6. Local water or gas outage

Of 100 households like yours, about 78 (50–95) will have a water main break or boil-water notice in the next ten years.[18, 28]

**What helps.** The same basic supplies cover all of these: water, food, lights, a radio and a backup phone battery.[29] Keep some cash in small bills, because ATMs and credit cards may not work.[30] Write important phone numbers on paper, not only in your phone.[31] Keep a written list of each person's medicines and doses.[14] Protect your accounts with a password manager and two-step login, and back up your files regularly.[32] Install software updates without delay.[33]

**What to avoid.** Do not click links in texts or emails from people you don't know. Scammers can make fake websites.[32] Learn to spot phishing: messages that try to get you to open an attachment or share personal information.[33] During a boil-water notice, do not use water or ice from the fridge dispenser.[34]

**How bad:** Minor. **How sure:** Mostly data.

#### 7. House fire

Of 100 households like yours, about 5 (3–8) will have a house fire, in their home or next door in the next ten years.[18, 35]

**What helps.** Put smoke alarms inside and outside every bedroom and on every level, and test them each month.[36] Practice your escape plan twice a year, including finding your way out in the dark.[37] Sleep with bedroom doors closed. Teach children not to hide from firefighters.[37] If there is smoke, crawl low under it to your exit.[37] If you cannot get out, close the door, cover vents and cracks with cloth or tape, call 911, and signal from the window with a light-colored cloth or a flashlight.[37] Insurance and copies of your documents help you recover afterward.[38]

**What to avoid.** Before you open a door, feel the door and the knob. If either is hot, or smoke is coming around it, keep it closed and use your second way out.[37] If a person or pet is still inside, get out and tell firefighters right away.[37] If your clothes catch fire, stop, drop and roll.[37]

**How bad:** Severe. **How sure:** Mostly data.

#### 8. Tornado

Of 100 households like yours, about 2 (1–7) will have a tornado damage or cut off their neighborhood in the next ten years.[18, 39]

**What helps.** The steps under "Strong wind" above apply here too.

**How bad:** Severe. **How sure:** Rough data.

#### 9. Flooding from rivers or heavy rain

Of 100 households like yours, about 2 (1–6) will be cut off or lose building services in a flood in the next ten years.[18, 40]

**What helps.** Look up your address on FEMA's flood maps, and sign up for your community's warnings.[41] Homeowner's insurance does not cover flooding, and a flood policy typically takes up to 30 days to start.[41] Renters can buy flood coverage for their belongings.[42] Know how you would get to higher ground or a higher floor.[41]

**What to avoid.** Six inches of moving water can knock you down, and one foot can sweep your car away.[41] Never drive around barriers on a flooded road. The road may have collapsed under the water.[43] Stay off bridges over fast-moving water.[41] Leave if you are told to.[44]

**How bad:** Very severe. **How sure:** Rough data.

### No grid power at home: about 3 days (2–5)

**What helps.** Have a flashlight for each person.[55] Keep a battery or hand-crank radio, and a backup battery for each phone.[29] Keep the fridge and freezer closed. A closed fridge keeps food cold for about four hours, and a full freezer for about two days.[55] If someone relies on a powered medical device or cold medicine, make a plan with their medical provider now.[55] Join your power company's medical program for early warnings of outages, but plan as if your power will not come back first.[56] Many cities and counties keep a voluntary registry of people who may need help; ask yours.[14] A battery power station burns no fuel, so it makes no carbon monoxide.[57]

**What to avoid.** Never run a generator inside your home or garage, even with the doors and windows open. Keep it outside, more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents.[57] Put a carbon monoxide alarm on every level of your home.[55] Headache, dizziness, nausea or confusion can mean carbon monoxide poisoning. Get everyone outside to fresh air, then call 911.[58] Never heat your home with a gas stove or oven. Unplug electronics, because the power can come back with a surge that damages them.[55] Treat every downed power line as live: stay at least 30 feet from it and anything it touches, and call 911.[59] If one falls on your car, stay inside unless the car is on fire.[60]

### Tap water must be treated: about 7 days (5 days to 2 weeks)

**What helps.** Use bottled water, or bring clear water to a rolling boil for one minute. Boil for three minutes above 5,000 feet, then let it cool.[61] If you cannot boil water, add plain unscented bleach: 8 drops of 6% bleach or 6 drops of 8.25% bleach per gallon. Stir, and wait 30 minutes.[61] Use boiled or bottled water for drinking, cooking, brushing teeth, baby formula and ice. If the power is out too, a camp stove works, but only outdoors.[57]

**What to avoid.** Do not use water or ice from the fridge dispenser while a notice is on. Boil tap water even if it went through a home filter or a filter pitcher.[34] Boiling does not help if the notice warns of chemicals. That is a do-not-drink notice, and you need stored or bottled water instead.[34, 62]

### No tap water at all: about 5 days (3–10)

**What helps.** Store at least one gallon per person per day, for drinking and keeping clean.[1] Fill clean bottles or jugs with tap water and replace it every six months.[63] You can pick a level in the plan. About 3 liters a day covers drinking to survive, one gallon is the basic default, and about 4 gallons (15 liters) is the humanitarian minimum for drinking, cooking and washing.[64] A tank water heater holds 20 to 80 gallons of clean water.[65, 66] For long outages, a filter and a source of raw water go further than more jugs.[54] Filters do not remove viruses, so add a disinfectant such as chlorine dioxide after filtering.[62] A two-bucket toilet keeps waste safe when toilets cannot flush.[67]

**What to avoid.** Do not store water in containers that held chemicals.[63] Milk jugs seal poorly and turn brittle.[68] Never drink pool or spa water. Use it only for washing and cleaning.[66] After a flood, do not drink from a private well until it has been disinfected and tested. Your health department can tell you how.[69]

### Medication and medical-supply continuity: about 2 weeks (10 days to 1 month)

**What helps.** Refill early, while you still have a week left. The Red Cross suggests keeping a 7-day supply, and Florida advises at least two weeks.[70, 71] Ask your doctor, pharmacist and drug plan how to build an emergency supply, such as a 90-day fill.[14, 72] Keep a written list of each person's medicines, doses and allergies.[14] Many states let pharmacists give an emergency refill, but the rules differ.[73] After a declared disaster, a federal program can help people without insurance replace prescriptions.[74] People with diabetes should pack supplies for at least 1 to 2 weeks: syringes or pen needles, a glucose meter with test strips and spare batteries, and glucose tablets.[75, 76] Insulin in its original vial or pen, opened or not, keeps working for up to 28 days at 59°F to 86°F. In an outage, keep it below 86°F in the shade or a cooler, never frozen.[77, 78] For other medicines that need a fridge, ask your pharmacist now how long each keeps out of it, and write the answer on your medicine list.[55]

**What to avoid.** Do not throw insulin away after a day without power. If it gets hotter than 86°F and you have nothing else, use it, and replace it as soon as you can.[77] Never use insulin that has frozen or looks unusual, with particles, clumps, crystals or a changed color.[77, 79] Do not use expired medicines. Weak antibiotics can fail to treat an infection.[17] Do not share antibiotics or save them for later.[16]

### No phone, internet or card payments: about 2 days (1–5)

**What helps.** Turn on emergency alerts on every phone. They are free, and you do not need to sign up.[80] Keep a battery or hand-crank radio and a NOAA Weather Radio with a tone alert.[29] NOAA Weather Radio has more than 1,000 transmitters covering all 50 states.[81] Write important phone numbers on paper, not only in your phone.[31] Pick an out-of-state contact everyone can text, and a place to meet if you are separated.[82] Keep some cash in small bills, because ATMs and credit cards may not work.[30] License-free two-way radios (FRS) let family members talk over short distances without a phone network.[83]

**What to avoid.** Do not rely on one source of news. Do not assume you can text 911 everywhere. It is not available in every area, so call 911 if you can.[15]

### House fire

**What helps.** Put smoke alarms inside and outside sleeping areas and on every level of your home. Test them every month.[36] Some fire departments and the Red Cross install them for free.[36, 88] If you rent, ask your landlord to put them in.[89] Make an escape plan with two ways out of each room, and practice it twice a year.[37] Cooking left alone is the top cause of kitchen fires that spread beyond the pan, so stay with your cooking. Keep a pan lid or baking sheet nearby to cover a pan that catches fire.[90] Keep anything that can burn at least 3 feet from heaters, fireplaces and candles.[6] A multipurpose extinguisher labeled "A-B-C" works on most home fires.[91]

**What to avoid.** If a person or pet is still inside, get out, call 911, and tell firefighters where they are.[37] Never heat your home with an oven.[6] Turn off space heaters when you leave the room or go to bed.[6] Replace 9-volt alarm batteries at least once a year.[36] If you smell gas or hear hissing, get everyone out without touching switches or phones, then call 911 and the gas company from a safe distance.[92, 93] Only a professional should turn the gas back on.[93]

### Home and personal security

**What helps.** Burglars look for homes that are easy targets. Lock up, and light the places where someone could hide.[94] Work with your neighbours to look out for each other.[94] Protect key accounts with a password manager and two-step login.[32] Learn a simple way to handle a tense moment: notice what is happening, say hello, judge the risk, and get help if you need it.[95] If an attack happens in a public place, getting away is the top priority.[96]

**What to avoid.** After a disaster, be careful with people who show up offering fast repairs or help for a fee. Report scams at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.[97] Do not hide a spare key under the doormat or a planter. Leave it with a trusted neighbour.[94]

### Leaving home: triggers and routes

About 10 of 100 households like yours have to leave home quickly at least once in 10 years. Warning can be 1 minute to 12 hours ahead. Plan to be away for about 2 days.[18, 28, 35, 39, 40, 45, 48]

- **Plan how you would leave: zone, routes, destination and triggers.** Look up your evacuation zone and routes on your state and county emergency management websites, mark two routes on a paper map, and pick where you would go, pet-friendly if you have pets (Ready.gov). For each likely hazard, write a go-or-stay card and an if-then trigger, such as 'If our zone gets an evacuation warning, we leave within 30 minutes.' Plans written in if-then form make people more likely to follow through.[29, 84, 85, 110, 111, 112, 113, 114]
- **No car? Plan your ride out.** If your household has no vehicle, decide now how you would leave: a friend, relative or neighbour who would drive you, or the bus or train that leads out of your area (Ready.gov). Leave early, before roads fill. If someone in your home has a disability, ask your local emergency management office about its voluntary registry for people who need help (Ready.gov).[14, 85, 115]

- **Practice: ten-minute drills and the first things to do.**

#### Drills and if-then plans

**If-then plans.** An if-then plan links a trigger to an action: "If the county issues an evacuation warning for our zone, we leave within 30 minutes." Plans written this way help people follow through.[113] When a warning comes, people often wait to confirm it with others before they act.[84] A trigger you agreed on earlier leaves less to decide in the moment. Write one for each likely hazard, and add who picks up the children and where you will meet.[108]

**Drills.** In New Zealand, people who took part in earthquake drills knew the right actions better, used them more during real shaking, and did more to prepare.[116] Among people who got out of the World Trade Center on 9/11, those with earlier emergency training were less likely to develop PTSD.[117] Practice your fire escape plan twice a year.[37] Other ten-minute drills: Drop, Cover and Hold On, a night without power, grabbing the go-bag against a timer, and sealing a room to shelter in place.[114]

**What to avoid.** Do not skip drills because they feel silly. Each one you finish counts toward your plan.

### Neighbors

- **Neighbours and skills: swap numbers, check on each other, learn first aid.**

#### Neighbours and mutual aid

**What helps.** Swap phone numbers with two neighbours. Agree who checks on whom, especially older neighbours and anyone who lives alone. Make a simple contact list for your street. Give a trusted person a spare key, and tell them where your supplies are and how to use any medical equipment.[115] Host a block party, since strong communities start with prepared neighbours.[118] Take CERT training to learn fire safety, light search and rescue, and disaster first aid.[119]

**What to avoid.** Meet the people next door before you need them. A short hello now makes it easier to knock on a door later.

## Documents and money

Keep paper copies in a waterproof pouch and photos you can reach from any phone. FEMA's Emergency Financial First Aid Kit groups them in four parts.[38]

- [ ] **Who you are:** photo IDs, birth certificates, Social Security cards, passports, and pet records.
- [ ] **Money and legal papers:** insurance policies, the lease or deed, bank and card contact numbers (not PINs), and recent tax returns.
- [ ] **Medical papers:** insurance cards, the written medicine list, prescriptions, and vaccination records.
- [ ] **Contacts:** family, doctors, the insurance agent, the landlord or lender, and employers.

- **Documents and insurance: copy key papers, record belongings, check cover.** Use FEMA's Emergency Financial First Aid Kit to gather IDs, financial and legal papers, insurance, medical information and contacts, and photograph or video every room and valuable item. Keep paper copies in a waterproof bag and another copy with someone you trust or in secure online storage (FEMA). Check what your insurance covers: standard home and renters policies do not cover flood or earthquake damage, a new flood policy usually takes 30 days to start, and renters can buy their own policy and contents-only flood cover (Ready.gov, FEMA).[29, 30, 38, 41, 42, 82, 120, 121]
- **Fire-resistant, waterproof document pouch.** A fire-resistant, water-resistant pouch or small case for paper copies of key documents, a USB drive and some cash, easy to grab when you leave (FEMA's financial kit suggests fire- and waterproof storage).[38, 107]

### Insurance questions

- You have no renters insurance yet: get a quote and decide. Check what it pays if you have to live somewhere else for a while. (FEMA, Ready.gov)[30, 38]
- Home and renters policies usually don't cover floods. Decide on flood insurance before you need it: a new policy usually starts 30 days after you buy it. It pays up to $250,000 for the building and $100,000 for belongings, and renters can buy cover for belongings only. (FEMA, FloodSmart, Ready.gov)[30, 42, 120]

### Cash

- Cash in small bills, kept with your documents, because ATMs and cards may not work in an outage: about $100 to start, or enough for about 3 days of basics (food, fuel, medicine) at your own daily spending. No agency gives a dollar amount. (Ready.gov, FEMA; some amounts are estimates)[2, 18, 30, 38, 39, 48, 102]

### Savings

No one in the household earns wages, so the plan sets no income-gap goal. Keep a small cushion for emergencies if you can.

- **Money: start an emergency fund and a bare-bones budget.** Set up an automatic transfer, however small, into a savings account you keep for emergencies; the CFPB says the right amount depends on your situation, and planners often suggest working toward three to six months of expenses (FINRA). Write down your essential monthly costs and what you would cut first, so you know how far savings would stretch. If anyone earns, find your state's unemployment website now, so you could file right away if a job ends (Department of Labor).[122, 123, 124, 125, 126, 127]

## Special needs

### Antibiotics

- No antibiotics are included in this plan. If you have a specific, foreseeable need, such as remote travel, talk to your own clinician about a standby prescription with written instructions. Never use fish or pet antibiotics, never share prescriptions, and don't use expired ones. (CDC, FDA)[16, 17, 128, 129]

Nobody in the household listed medical or access needs. Keep a written medicine list anyway, and update this plan if that changes.

### Stress and mental health

#### Stress, mental health and the 988 line

**What helps.** Save 988 in every phone. It offers free, judgment-free support 24/7 for mental health, substance use and more, by call, text or chat.[130] After a disaster, the Disaster Distress Helpline offers crisis counseling 24/7 to anyone in emotional distress. Call or text 1-800-985-5990.[131] Write a short calm plan: what you might feel, what helps you, and who you will call.[114] Oregon's 2 Weeks Ready program even includes practicing mindfulness as a step in getting prepared.[132]

**What to avoid.** Do not keep the news on all day. No one, adult or child, benefits from graphic details or disturbing images.[133] Reach out before things feel unbearable. The 988 line supports people in emotional distress as well as in crisis.[130]
