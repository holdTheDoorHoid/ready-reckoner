## How to use this binder

This binder is your household's plan on paper, for when phones, power or the internet are down. Keep it where everyone can find it, and keep the contact page with you too.[2]

**In an emergency.** Read Quick start first. Then find what is happening under "Which checklist?" and turn to that page. Each checklist starts with the steps to do first, then what to do next, and when to leave or stay.

**The tabs.** Tab 1 has the numbers to call. Tabs 2 to 5 hold your people, places, animals, papers and supplies. Tabs 6 to 8 are the checklists: happening now, it is coming, and it goes on. Tab 9 is for the days after, with logs to fill in. Tab 10 lists the sources.

**Blanks.** A line means an answer is still missing. Fill it in by hand, together. Use pencil for things that change.

**Practice and review.** Go over the plan with your household and practice it.[3] Practice leaving your home twice a year.[2] Look at your needs again every year, print a new binder, and do it sooner when something changes.[1]

## Quick start: the first five minutes of any emergency

Learn these steps by heart. Do them in order, then turn to the checklist for what is happening.

1. **Get away from danger. If there is fire or smoke, get everyone out and stay out.[4]**
2. **Call 911 if anyone is hurt or in danger. Where you cannot talk, you may be able to text 911.[5, 6]**
3. **Meet and count heads. Go to your meeting place right outside the home, or the one outside the neighborhood if you cannot get home.[2]**
4. **Get official news. Watch your phone for emergency alerts, and listen to a battery radio.[7, 8]**
5. **Do what local officials say. If they tell you to leave, leave.[8]**
6. **Text your out-of-area contact. Keep calls short and text when you can, so lines stay open.[9]**

**Then:** find what is happening in (Tab 1, Which checklist?). Numbers to call: (Tab 1, Contacts at a glance).

#### How to reach each other

Emergency planners call this a PACE plan: a primary way, an alternate, a contingency and an emergency way. Each one takes over when the one before it fails.[13] For a household it can be:

1. Primary: call.
2. Alternate: text. A text may get through when a call does not, and it saves battery.[14]
3. Contingency: everyone texts one out-of-state contact, who passes the news along.[14]
4. Emergency: go to the place you agreed to meet if you get separated.[14]

#### Stress, mental health and the 988 line

**What helps.** Save 988 in every phone. It offers free, judgment-free support 24/7 for mental health, substance use and more, by call, text or chat.[10] After a disaster, the Disaster Distress Helpline offers crisis counseling 24/7 to anyone in emotional distress. Call or text 1-800-985-5990.[12] Write a short calm plan: what you might feel, what helps you, and who you will call.[19] Oregon's 2 Weeks Ready program even includes practicing mindfulness as a step in getting prepared.[20]

**What to avoid.** Do not keep the news on all day. No one, adult or child, benefits from graphic details or disturbing images.[21] Reach out before things feel unbearable. The 988 line supports people in emotional distress as well as in crisis.[10]

Pick a spot for each danger below, at home and at work, and practice going there.[3]

**Strong wind.** In a high wind or severe thunderstorm warning, go inside a sturdy building, to an inside room.[22] Stay away from windows while damaging wind is near.[23]

**Tornado.** A safe room or storm shelter is best; next is a small inside room with no windows on the lowest floor.[24] Even a tied-down mobile home is not safe.[25]

**Hurricane.** If officials tell your evacuation zone to leave, go right away. If you stay, use an inside room away from windows.[26]

**Chemical release.** If told to stay inside, go to an above-ground room with few openings. Close the windows, doors and vents, turn off fans and air conditioning, and seal the room with plastic sheeting and duct tape.[27]

**Smoke.** Make one room a clean room: close its windows and doors, and run an air cleaner that does not make ozone.[28]

**Radiation.** If officials warn of fallout, get inside, stay inside, stay tuned. Go to a basement or the middle of a brick or concrete building.[29]

## Medical emergency

**Here:** almost all households like yours in the next 10 years · How bad: Serious[55]

### Use this when

Someone collapses, stops breathing normally, is choking or bleeding hard, or shows stroke or heart attack signs.

### Do first

1. **Call 911. Give the address and do what the call-taker says.[96]**
2. **Not breathing normally? Push hard and fast in the center of the chest, 100 to 120 pushes a minute.[101]**
3. **Send someone for an AED. Turn it on and follow its voice.[101]**
4. **Choking? If they cannot cough, speak or breathe, give 5 back blows, then 5 abdominal thrusts. Repeat.[102]**
5. **Bleeding hard? Press firmly on the wound with a cloth or your hands until help takes over.[103, 104]**
6. **Stroke signs? A face droops, an arm drifts down or speech is slurred. Note the time.[105]**

2. **Keep pushing** until help arrives or they breathe normally.[101]
3. **Choking person goes limp?** Lay them down and start CPR.[102]
4. **Breathing but not awake?** Roll them onto their side.[104]
5. **Keep them warm.** Cover them, and put something under them.[104]
6. **Talk to them.** Keep them calm.[104]
7. **Give responders** their medicine list and allergies.[30] (Tab 2, Who is in this binder)
8. **Cannot call?** Text 911.[96]

**Leave or stay?**

- **Stay if** it is safe there. Stay with them until help arrives.[103]
- **Leave if** it is not safe there. Move them out of danger first, such as out of traffic.[104]
- **Call** 911 for an ambulance for stroke signs. Do not drive them yourself.[105]

## Boil-water notice

### Use this when

### Do first

2. **Boil it. Bring clear water to a rolling boil for 1 minute, or 3 minutes above 5,000 feet. Let it cool.[149]**

3. **Wash hands** with soap and tap water, unless local officials say not to.[114]
4. **Boil filtered water too.** A home or pitcher filter does not make it safe.[114]
5. **Cannot boil?** Add 8 drops of plain, unscented 6% bleach per gallon. Stir, and wait 30 minutes.[149]
6. **Do laundry** as usual.[114]

# Tab 9: After

## After a disaster: the first 30 days

**Your county.** Cook County, Illinois had 3 federal major-disaster declarations in the last five full years.[210]

Recovery takes weeks. These steps come in rough order.

**Going home.** Go back only when local officials say it is safe. Do not go inside if you smell gas or the building looks damaged.[112] Run a generator only outside, more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents.[92]

**Insurance and records.** Before you clean up, take photos and videos and make a list of the damage.[112] Keep every receipt.[208] Call your insurer as soon as you can, and an adjuster will come to inspect. Check the adjuster's estimate, and talk to them before you sign a repair contract.[175]

**Help from FEMA.** If a federal disaster declaration covers your county, apply at DisasterAssistance.gov or call 1-800-621-3362.[209] FEMA help does not replace insurance.[211]

**Scams.** FEMA never charges to apply or to inspect.[212] Its inspectors carry an official photo ID and never ask for your bank details; a FEMA shirt is not an ID.[208] Never sign your insurance check over to a contractor, and walk away from anyone who wants cash up front.[213]

**Medicine.** In a declared disaster, Medicare drug plans can help you find a pharmacy and replace lost medicine.[181] Many states let a pharmacist give an emergency supply when your prescriber cannot be reached, and the rules differ by state.[31] Healthcare Ready's Rx Open map shows which pharmacies are open in a disaster area.[214]

**Papers.** To replace lost IDs, start with your birth certificate, from the vital records office of the state where you were born.[126]

## Documents and money: decisions and savings

### Decisions

- [ ] **Decide: ID for every person (passport book or card, or a state ID).** A passport book or card for each person, children included, proves who you are and your citizenship if other papers are lost, and the card shows no home address; get them before you need them. The federal fee schedule: a first adult book $165 and card $65 ($130 and $30 plus a $35 acceptance fee), for children under 16 a book $135 and card $50 (22 CFR 22.1).[17, 18, 19]
- [ ] **Decide: homeowners, condo or renters insurance.** You have no renters insurance yet: get a quote and decide. Check what it pays if you have to live somewhere else for a while.[19, 20]
- [ ] **Decide: flood insurance, even outside a flood zone.** Home and renters policies usually don't cover floods. Decide on flood insurance before you need it: Most homes in your county are outside the mapped high-risk flood zone, and that is where many uninsured floods happen: about 3 in 10 flood insurance claims come from outside high-risk flood areas. A quote costs nothing, and a new policy usually starts 30 days after you buy it. It pays up to $250,000 for the building and $100,000 for belongings, and renters can buy cover for belongings only.[20, 21, 22, 23, 24]

### Savings

No one in the household earns wages, so the plan sets no income-gap goal. Keep a small cushion for emergencies if you can.
