## Your risks

These are the events most likely to reach a household like yours in Cook County, Illinois, over the next 10 years. Common events come first: a lost job, a house fire or a week-long outage is far more likely than a disaster from the movies, and the plan is ordered the same way.

### The ones most likely to reach you

#### 1. Cold wave

Nearly every household like yours will go through a spell of dangerous cold in the next ten years (about 3.7 times a year).[2] Winter storms raise the risk of car crashes, hypothermia, frostbite, carbon monoxide poisoning, and heart attacks from overexertion.[3] Ice can bring down power lines, and a hard freeze can break water systems. In Texas, boil-water notices after winter storms lasted about a week in the typical case.[4]

**What helps.** Keep extra blankets, sleeping bags and warm coats, and pick one room to keep warm.[5] Learn how to keep pipes from freezing, and test smoke and carbon monoxide alarms before winter.[3] Keep a car kit with jumper cables, sand, a flashlight, warm clothes, blankets, water and snacks, and keep the gas tank full.[3] Check on older neighbours often.[5] Know the signs of hypothermia, such as shivering, confusion and slurred speech. A body temperature below 95°F is an emergency. Warm the center of the body first.[3] In avalanche country, get training, sign up for alerts from a Forest Service avalanche center, and carry a beacon, a probe and a small shovel.[6]

**What to avoid.** Never heat your home with a gas stove or oven. Use generators and grills only outdoors, away from windows.[3] Choose electric space heaters with automatic shut-off, and keep them 3 feet from anything that can burn.[5, 7] Do not overdo it when you shovel snow.[3]

- **What it can do to you:** no power, no tap water and dangerous heat or cold at home.
- **How bad one can be:** Minor. **How sure we are:** Based on data.

#### 2. Heat wave

Nearly every household like yours will go through a heat wave in the next ten years (about 2.1 times a year).[2] Heat is most dangerous for older adults, babies and young children, pregnant women, outdoor workers, and people with heart disease or asthma.[8] Heat and a power cut together are far worse than either alone. One study estimated that a five-day blackout during a heat wave would send about half of Phoenix's residents to emergency care.[9]

**What helps.** Know where you can go to cool off. Dial 2-1-1 to find air conditioning near you.[8] Cover windows with drapes or shades, and weather-strip doors and windows.[10] Take cool showers, wear loose, light-colored clothing, and drink plenty of fluids. Check on family members, older adults and neighbours.[10] Heat can cause power outages, so plan for refrigerated medicines and powered medical devices.[8] In a drought, follow local limits on water use, and make saving water a daily habit.[11]

**What to avoid.** Do not rely on a fan when it is over 90°F indoors. It can make you hotter.[8] Never leave people or pets in a closed car on a warm day. Heat stroke is an emergency. Signs include a body temperature above 103°F, hot, red, dry skin, a fast pulse and confusion. Call 911.[10]

- **What it can do to you:** no power and dangerous heat or cold at home.
- **How bad one can be:** Minor. **How sure we are:** Based on data.

#### 3. Medical emergency

Of 100 households like yours, about 99 will have someone need emergency care in the next ten years.[12] Injuries and sudden illness are common. In 2024, accidental injuries caused about 58 deaths for every 100,000 people.[13] Emergency departments see about 47 visits for every 100 people each year.[12] In rural areas, ambulances take longer to arrive.[14]

**What helps.** Take a first-aid and bleeding-control class. You may be able to save a life by acting right after an injury, before help arrives.[15] Keep a stocked first-aid kit. Check it regularly and replace anything used or out of date.[16] Keep a list of each person's medicines, doses and allergies with your emergency supplies.[17] Know how to reach 911. Call if you can, and text where that service works.[18] Set up telehealth with your own clinician before you need it.[19]

**What to avoid.** Do not start antibiotics on your own or take someone else's. The wrong medicine can delay the right treatment and cause serious side effects.[20] Do not use expired medicines.[21]

- **What it can do to you:** a medical emergency.
- **How bad one can be:** Serious. **How sure we are:** Based on data.

#### 4. Strong wind

Of 100 households like yours, about 95 (59 to nearly all) will lose power or have damage in a windstorm in the next ten years.[2, 22] Severe weather can happen anytime, in any part of the country. Thunderstorms bring damaging winds, tornadoes, large hail, flash floods and lightning.[23] Tornado winds can top 200 miles an hour.[24]

**What helps.** Pick your shelter spot now. A safe room or storm shelter is best. The next best is a small, windowless room or basement on the lowest floor of a sturdy building.[24] Sign up for your community's warnings, and learn what your sirens sound like.[24] Know the signs of a tornado: a rotating funnel cloud, a cloud of debris, or a roar like a freight train. In your shelter, protect your head and neck with your arms, and pile blankets or cushions around you.[24] When thunder roars, go indoors. Get into a building or a car with a roof.[25]

**What to avoid.** Stay away from windows, doors and outside walls.[24] There is no safe place in a car during a tornado, only less dangerous ones. If you are caught, stay buckled in and keep your head below the windows.[24] During a thunderstorm, avoid running water and electronics plugged into the wall.[25]

- **What it can do to you:** no power, no phone, internet or card payments and damage to the home.
- **How bad one can be:** Minor. **How sure we are:** Rough data.

#### 5. Supply chain disruption

Of 100 households like yours, about 86 (63–98) will find store shelves empty of things they need in the next ten years.[22] Sometimes you cannot get to a store, or the shelves are empty. Winter storms, runs on stores before a storm, curfews and disease outbreaks can all do this. In spring 2020, statewide stay-at-home orders lasted about six weeks in the middle state.[26, 27]

**What helps.** Keep extra food that needs no refrigeration, cooking or water, and choose foods your family will eat.[28] Use the oldest cans first and replace them as you go.[29] Buy a little at a time. Ready.gov asks people to buy supplies slowly, so everyone can get what they need.[30] Keep a manual can opener.[28] The plan counts food in calories, not servings. Many "30-day" meal kits hold only about 1,300 to 1,700 calories a day.[31] That is less than most adults need.[32] Store food somewhere cool and dry. Below 85°F is best.[29]

**What to avoid.** Do not buy everything at once. It strains your budget and the store. Do not buy or eat from cans that bulge, leak, are rusted or are deeply dented.[29] Do not trust a kit's "days" label. Check the calories on the package instead.

- **What it can do to you:** no way to get to a store and gaps in medicine.
- **How bad one can be:** Minor. **How sure we are:** Expert estimate.

These are shown apart, with how likely and how bad as separate columns. A tiny chance times a huge loss would otherwise crowd out everything else, and the plan never lets them take over the budget.

The first advice for a nuclear emergency is to get inside, stay inside and stay tuned.[45] Your three-day supplies already cover that first stretch of sheltering.

These events are very rare. In a 2024 survey, experts and forecasters put the chance of a nuclear catastrophe anywhere in the world before 2045 at 1 to 5 in 100.[44] The right response is simple, and your three-day supplies already cover it.

**What helps.** Get inside, stay inside, stay tuned.[45] Go to a basement or the middle of a large brick or concrete building. Stay there for 24 hours unless there is a fire or another immediate danger, or officials say it is safe to leave. Radiation levels drop fast during the first 24 hours.[45] Turn off fans and air conditioning that pull in outside air.[46] If you were outside, take off your outer layer of clothing. That can remove up to 90% of radioactive material. Then wash with soap and water.[46] If you live near a nuclear power plant, learn whether you are in its planning zone.[43]

**What to avoid.** Do not take potassium iodide unless public health or emergency officials tell you to. It protects only the thyroid.[47] Near a plant, states can give out tablets to people within 10 miles, on official instruction.[48] Do not spend on special gear before your basic supplies are in place.[27]

### No grid power at home: about 3 days (up to 5 days)

Of 100 households like yours, about 30 (25–60) will lose grid power for a day or more in the next 10 years.[2, 9, 22, 37, 38, 49, 50, 51, 52] Most outages last a few hours. In 2024 the average home lost power for about 11 hours in total, mostly because of hurricanes.[53] After recent hurricanes, getting power back to 95 of every 100 customers who lost it took from about 3 days to nearly 3 weeks.[52]

**What helps.** Have a flashlight for each person.[54] Keep a battery or hand-crank radio, and a backup battery for each phone.[55] Keep the fridge and freezer closed. A closed fridge keeps food cold for about four hours, and a full freezer for about two days.[54] If someone relies on a powered medical device or cold medicine, make a plan with their medical provider now.[54] You can also ask your power company to put you on its list for priority restoration.[17] A battery power station burns no fuel, so it makes no carbon monoxide.[56]

**What to avoid.** Never run a generator inside your home or garage, even with the doors and windows open. Keep it outside, more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents.[56] Put a carbon monoxide alarm on every level of your home.[54] Never heat your home with a gas stove or oven. Unplug electronics, because the power can come back with a surge that damages them.[54]

**Your numbers.** Being ready for 3 days leaves about 7 (6–20) of 100 households like yours facing a longer power cut in the next 10 years. This number mostly depends on how often windstorms happen where you live (expert estimate) and how long power cuts from extreme cold straining the grid last (expert estimate).[2, 9, 22, 37, 38, 49, 50, 51, 52]

**What counts toward it:**

- 1 battery light: a headlamp or lantern for each person aged 4 and over. Use lights, not candles. (Ready.gov; some amounts are estimates)[54, 55, 57]
- 1 pack of AA or AAA batteries for the lights and the radio: one pack lasts a household about 7 days. Check which sizes your lights use. (Ready.gov; some amounts are estimates)[2, 9, 22, 37, 38, 49, 50, 51, 52, 55, 57]

**Also worth knowing:**

- 1 battery power station (about 1,070 Wh each) as an optional upgrade for lights, phones and a radio. Your essential load (phones) is about 15 Wh a day, 50 Wh for 3 days; one station gives about 910 Wh after losses, enough for about 60.6 days of that load. A full-size fridge alone would drain one in about 0.9 days. (retail prices, ENERGY STAR; some amounts are estimates)[2, 9, 22, 37, 38, 49, 50, 51, 52, 57, 58, 59]
- Optional: keeping a fridge running takes about 995 Wh a day (a chest freezer about 597) × 3 days = 2,990 Wh. A closed fridge keeps food cold about 4 hours and a full freezer about 48; shelf-stable food avoids the need. (ENERGY STAR, Ready.gov)[2, 9, 22, 28, 37, 38, 49, 50, 51, 52, 54, 59]

### Tap water must be treated: about 7 days (5 days to 2 weeks)

Of 100 households like yours, about 40 (25–65) will have to boil or treat their tap water for a day or more in the next 10 years.[2, 4, 22, 33, 37, 38, 60] A boil-water notice means the taps still run, but germs may be in the water. Most notices come from broken water mains and repairs.[33] In Texas the most common notice lasted three to four days, and a few lasted more than a month.[4]

**What helps.** Use bottled water, or bring clear water to a rolling boil for one minute. Boil for three minutes above 5,000 feet, then let it cool.[61] If you cannot boil water, add plain unscented bleach: 8 drops of 6% bleach or 6 drops of 8.25% bleach per gallon. Stir, and wait 30 minutes.[61] Use boiled or bottled water for drinking, cooking, brushing teeth, baby formula and ice. If the power is out too, a camp stove works, but only outdoors.[56]

**What to avoid.** Do not use water or ice from the fridge dispenser while a notice is on. Boil tap water even if it went through a home filter or a filter pitcher.[62] Boiling does not help if the notice warns of chemicals. That is a do-not-drink notice, and you need stored or bottled water instead.[62, 63]

**Your numbers.** Being ready for a week leaves about 9 (4–20) of 100 households like yours facing a longer boil-water notice in the next 10 years. This number mostly depends on how long boil-water notices from local water problems last (expert estimate) and how long boil-water notices from extreme cold straining the grid last (expert estimate).[2, 4, 22, 33, 37, 38, 60]

**What counts toward it:**

- For a boil-water notice of up to 7 days: make about 0.8 gallons of drinking water safe each day, about 5.3 gallons in all. To make it safe, boil it for 1 minute (3 minutes above 5,000 feet), or add 8 drops of 6 % unscented bleach per gallon (6 drops of 8.25 %) and wait 30 minutes; double the bleach for cloudy or very cold water. A gas stove can boil water while the gas flows; never use a camp stove or grill indoors. (Ready.gov, CDC, EPA)[1, 2, 4, 22, 33, 37, 38, 56, 60, 61, 63]
- 1 bottle of plain unscented bleach (5 to 9 % sodium hypochlorite, not the splashless kind), for making water safe and for cleaning, however long your target is. At about 0.5 mL a gallon, each ounce treats about 60 gallons of clear water (30 if it is cloudy or very cold), so one bottle treats thousands of gallons. Bleach weakens in storage: buy a fresh bottle every 6 months, when you replace the water you store yourself. (CDC, EPA; some amounts are estimates)[57, 61, 63, 64, 65]

**Also worth knowing:**

- If you boil on a camp stove outdoors, about 0.7 lb of propane brings 20 L to a boil (about 25 to 30 L a pound). Never use a camp stove indoors, and keep no more than 2 one-pound cylinders inside. (EIA, local fire code, CDC; some amounts are estimates)[56, 57, 66, 67]

### No tap water at all: about 5 days (3–10)

Of 100 households like yours, about 30 (20–55) will have no usable tap water for a day or more in the next 10 years.[2, 22, 33, 34, 68] Sometimes no water comes out of the tap, or officials say not to use it at all. Earthquakes, hard freezes, hurricanes and chemical spills can do this. After Hurricane Helene in 2024, Asheville, North Carolina, waited about seven weeks for safe tap water.[68] If your well has an electric pump, every power cut is also a water cut.[27]

**What helps.** Store at least one gallon per person per day, for drinking and keeping clean.[1] Fill clean bottles or jugs with tap water and replace it every six months.[64] You can pick a level in the plan. About 3 liters a day covers drinking to survive, one gallon is the basic default, and about 4 gallons (15 liters) is the humanitarian minimum for drinking, cooking and washing.[69] A tank water heater holds 20 to 80 gallons of clean water.[70, 71] For long outages, a filter and a source of raw water go further than more jugs.[27] Filters do not remove viruses, so add a disinfectant such as chlorine dioxide after filtering.[63] A two-bucket toilet keeps waste safe when toilets cannot flush.[72]

**What to avoid.** Do not store water in containers that held chemicals.[64] Milk jugs seal poorly and turn brittle.[73] Never drink pool or spa water. Use it only for washing and cleaning.[71]

**Your numbers.** Being ready for 5 days leaves about 8 (5–15) of 100 households like yours facing a longer water outage in the next 10 years. This number mostly depends on how often chemical spills and releases happen where you live (expert estimate) and how long water outages from chemical releases last (expert estimate).[2, 22, 33, 34, 68]

**What counts toward it:**

- 1 person × 1 gallon a day × 5 days = 5 gallons. Replace water you store yourself every 6 months. (Ready.gov, CDC)[1, 2, 22, 33, 34, 64, 68]
- A two-bucket toilet for when toilets can't flush: 2 sturdy buckets with a seat, one for pee and one for poo. (RDPO, Oregon OEM)[72, 74]
- Toilet bags: about 0.45 heavy garbage bags a person a day (0.3 to 0.6), counting both bags of a double-bagged load: 1 person × 5 days = 3 bags. Fill halfway, tie, double-bag, and store away from food, water, children and pets. Don't put them in curbside trash or bury them unless officials say so. (RDPO, Oregon OEM; some amounts are estimates)[2, 22, 33, 34, 57, 68, 72, 74]
- Cover material for the poo bucket (sawdust, shredded paper, dry leaves or pet bedding): about 1 cup a person a day (0.5 to 1.5) × 1 person × 5 days = 5 cups, about 0.3 gallons. (RDPO; some amounts are estimates)[2, 22, 33, 34, 57, 68, 72]

**Also worth knowing:**

- Free first step: fill clean drink bottles you already have with tap water, about 3 gallons (3 days of your water). Not milk jugs: they leak. Label them and replace the water every 6 months. (Ready.gov, CDC, Church of Jesus Christ)[1, 64, 73]

### Dangerous heat or cold indoors: about 3 days (2–5)

Of 100 households like yours, about 25 (20–50) will face dangerous heat or cold at home with no working cooling or heating for a day or more in the next 10 years.[2, 9, 22, 37, 38, 49, 50, 51, 52] Heat and cold become dangerous indoors when the power fails or the heating or air conditioning stops. Some people need extra care: babies and young children, people over 65, pregnant women, and people with heart disease or asthma.[8] In the 1995 Chicago heat wave, people who lived alone or did not leave home each day were more likely to die. Working air conditioning and social contact protected people.[80]

**What helps.** Agree who checks on whom, especially older neighbours and anyone who lives alone.[5, 8] In heat, go where there is air conditioning. Dial 2-1-1 to find one.[8] Cover windows with drapes or shades.[10] In cold, close off rooms you don't need, stuff towels under doors, and cover windows with blankets at night. Have extra blankets, sleeping bags and warm coats.[5] Babies under one year old should never sleep in a cold room.[5]

**What to avoid.** Fans move air but do not lower body temperature.[10] Above 90°F indoors, a fan can make you hotter.[8] Never heat your home with a gas oven, and never use a grill or camp stove indoors.[56] Keep space heaters 3 feet from anything that can burn.[7] Never leave people or pets in a closed car on a warm day.[10]

**Your numbers.** Being ready for 3 days leaves about 6 (4–15) of 100 households like yours facing a longer spell of dangerous heat or cold in the next 10 years. This number mostly depends on how long power cuts from extreme cold straining the grid last (expert estimate) and how often windstorms happen where you live (expert estimate). Your heating needs electricity to run (a gas furnace needs it for the igniter and fan), so a long winter power cut also cuts your heat. A power cut during a heat wave also stops your air conditioning.[2, 9, 22, 37, 38, 49, 50, 51, 52]

**What counts toward it:**

- 1 battery fan: 1 for the home. Fans help only while it is below 90 °F indoors; above that, go to a cooling centre. (CDC; some amounts are estimates)[8, 57]
- 1 cooling towel, one for each person: wet them and wear them on the neck in a heat wave. (an estimate)[57]
- A heat plan: find your nearest cooling centre (dial 2-1-1), pick the coolest room, cover sunny windows, and agree who checks on whom. Never leave people or pets in a closed car. (CDC, Ready.gov)[8, 10]
- 1 warm blanket, one for each person: most homes already have them. (Ready.gov, Sphere)[55, 69]
- 1 set of warm layers (coat, hat, gloves, dry socks), one for each person. (Ready.gov, CDC)[5, 55]
- A cold plan: pick one room to keep warm, close off the others, put towels under doors and cover windows at night. Never heat with a gas oven, grill, camp stove or generator indoors (carbon monoxide). Gas, oil and propane furnaces usually need electricity for their fans and controls, so plan for cold without power. A body temperature below 95 °F needs medical help at once. (CDC; some amounts are estimates)[5, 56, 57]

**Also worth knowing:**

- An upgrade, not a need: 1 sleeping bag or extra heavy blanket, one for each person. Blankets and warm layers cover a cold target of 3 days. (Ready.gov, Sphere; some amounts are estimates)[2, 9, 22, 37, 38, 49, 50, 51, 52, 55, 57, 69]

### Medication and medical-supply continuity: about 2 weeks (10 days to 1 month)

Of 100 households like yours, about 35 (25–65) will have trouble getting medicine for 3 days or more in the next 10 years.[22, 33, 35, 36, 39] In a disaster, pharmacies and roads can close, and pharmacy computer systems can go down.[27] Anyone who takes a daily medicine needs a way to keep taking it.

**What helps.** Refill early, while you still have a week left. The Red Cross suggests keeping a 7-day supply, and Florida advises at least two weeks.[81, 82] People with diabetes should pack supplies for at least 1 to 2 weeks.[83] Talk to your doctor or pharmacist about building an emergency supply, and keep a written list of each person's medicines, doses and allergies.[17] Many states let pharmacists give an emergency refill, but the rules differ from state to state.[84] After a declared disaster, a federal program can help people without insurance replace prescriptions.[85] If a medicine must stay cold, ask how long it can be kept warm, and plan a cooler.[54]

**What to avoid.** If the power is out for more than a day, throw away medicine that should be refrigerated, unless the label says otherwise.[54] Keep insulin cool but never frozen.[86] Do not use expired medicines. Weak antibiotics can fail to treat an infection.[21] Do not share antibiotics or save them for later.[20]

**Your numbers.** Being ready for 2 weeks leaves about 8 (5–20) of 100 households like yours facing a longer gap in medicine in the next 10 years. This number mostly depends on how often cyberattacks on services happen where you live (expert estimate) and how long gaps in medicine from pharmacy or insurer computer outages last (expert estimate).[22, 33, 35, 36, 39]

**Also worth knowing:**

- No antibiotics are included in this plan. If you have a specific, foreseeable need, such as remote travel, talk to your own clinician about a standby prescription with written instructions. Never use fish or pet antibiotics, never share prescriptions, and don't use expired ones. (CDC, FDA)[20, 21, 87, 88]

### No phone, internet or card payments: about 2 days (1–5)

Of 100 households like yours, about 20 (10–60) will lose phone, internet or card payments for a day or more in the next 10 years.[2, 22, 37, 38] Storms, power cuts and computer failures can knock out phones, internet and card readers. When networks are busy, a text message may get through when a call does not, and it saves battery.[89]

**What helps.** Turn on emergency alerts on every phone. They are free, and you do not need to sign up.[90] Keep a battery or hand-crank radio and a NOAA Weather Radio with a tone alert.[55] NOAA Weather Radio has more than 1,000 transmitters covering all 50 states.[91] Write important phone numbers on paper, not only in your phone.[92] Pick an out-of-state contact everyone can text, and a place to meet if you are separated.[89] Keep some cash in small bills, because ATMs and credit cards may not work.[93] License-free two-way radios (FRS) let family members talk over short distances without a phone network.[94]

**What to avoid.** Do not rely on one source of news. Do not assume you can text 911 everywhere. It is not available in every area, so call 911 if you can.[18]

**Your numbers.** Being ready for 2 days leaves about 9 (4–35) of 100 households like yours facing a longer outage in the next 10 years. This number mostly depends on how often windstorms happen where you live (expert estimate) and how long phone and payment outages from short storm outages last (expert estimate).[2, 22, 37, 38]

**What counts toward it:**

- 1 battery or hand-crank radio that gets NOAA Weather Radio, with a tone alert, for warnings when phones and power are down. (Ready.gov, NOAA)[55, 91]
- Power banks: 1 phone × about 15 Wh a day (10 to 20) × 3 days without power = 50 Wh. Text instead of calling to save battery. (Ready.gov; some amounts are estimates)[2, 9, 22, 37, 38, 49, 50, 51, 52, 55, 57, 89]
- 1 written contact card, one for each person aged 4 and over: an out-of-area contact, meeting places and key numbers. Phones die; paper does not. (Ready.gov)[92, 95]
- 1 paper map of your area, marked with meeting places and ways out. Save offline maps on your phone too. (Ready.gov)[55]
- Cash in small bills, kept with your documents, because ATMs and cards may not work in an outage: about $100 to start, or enough for about 3 days of basics (food, fuel, medicine) at your own daily spending. No agency gives a dollar amount. (Ready.gov, FEMA; some amounts are estimates)[2, 22, 37, 38, 57, 93, 96]

### Must leave home quickly

Of 100 households like yours, about 15 (9–20) will have to leave home quickly at least once in the next 10 years.[22, 33, 34, 37, 38, 39, 40] Wildfires, floods, hurricanes, chemical spills and house fires can force you to leave. Sometimes you get days of warning, sometimes only minutes. In the year before December 2022, about 16 of every 1,000 U.S. households were forced from home by a disaster.[97]

**What helps.** Plan where you would go and more than one way to get there.[98] Keep the gas tank at least half full, and full when an evacuation seems likely.[98] Pack a go-bag for each person. CDC's short list is phone, chargers, medicines, ID, cash and the car emergency kit.[99] Plan for pets, because public shelters may take only service animals.[98] Agree on an if-then rule ahead of time, such as "If our zone gets a warning, we leave within 30 minutes." Plans written this way help people follow through.[100] Leave a note saying when you left and where you are going.[98]

**What to avoid.** Many people wait to check with others before they act on a warning.[101] Always follow the instructions of local officials, and leave early enough to avoid being trapped by bad weather.[98] Never walk or drive through floodwater. Six inches of moving water can knock you down.[102]

**Your numbers.** Warning can be as short as a few minutes or as long as 12 hours. Plan to be away for about 2 days. With no car, plan to leave early when told to evacuate: arrange a ride or know the transit route.[22, 33, 34, 37, 38, 39, 40]

**What counts toward it:**

- 1 go-bag, one for each person: a sturdy bag you already own will do. Pack copies of documents, a phone charger, cash, a flashlight, a whistle, a change of clothes and sturdy shoes, and add water, food and medicines from your home supplies rather than buying them twice. You may get only minutes of warning (a local tsunami or a fast fire), so keep the bags packed by the door, and one at work if you work in a danger zone. Practise grabbing them and leaving. Plan where you would stay for about 2 days. (Washington EMD, Ready.gov, CDC; some amounts are estimates)[22, 33, 34, 37, 38, 39, 40, 55, 57, 99, 103]
- No car: decide now who would drive you, or which bus or train leads out of the area, and leave early, because it takes longer without a car. (Ready.gov)[98]

**Also worth knowing:**

- Water for the go-bags comes out of the water you store at home, not on top of it: the Red Cross three-day supply for leaving home, about 1 gallon a day for the 1 person × 2 days = 2 gallons. Keep it with the bags or take it as you leave. If you leave on foot, carry what you can (about 2.5 L each) and a filter; the rest goes in the car. (Red Cross, Washington EMD, Ready.gov, CDC; some amounts are estimates)[1, 22, 33, 34, 37, 38, 39, 40, 57, 64, 81, 103]
- Food for the go-bags comes out of your home food supply, not on top of it: about 2,262 kcal a day × 2 days = 4,500 kcal of food that needs no cooking or refrigeration. (Red Cross, Washington EMD, Dietary Guidelines)[22, 32, 33, 34, 37, 38, 39, 40, 81, 103]

### Stranded away from home

Of 100 households like yours, about 35 (20–70) will have someone stranded away from home at least once in the next 10 years.[2, 22] An earthquake, a storm or a transit shutdown can leave you far from home with roads or trains closed. You may need to stay where you are, or walk home.

**What helps.** Ready.gov has advised being ready to stay at work for at least 24 hours, with food, water, medicines and comfortable walking shoes.[104] Keep a small get-home bag at work or in the car: water, snacks, a flashlight, a phone battery, a whistle, a dust mask and a paper map.[55] Plan your route home and walk part of it once. Road engineers plan for people walking about 2 to 2.4 miles an hour, so 10 miles can take 4 to 5 hours.[105] In heat, drink about 1 cup of water every 15 to 20 minutes while you walk.[106] Include your children's school in your family plan, and agree who will pick them up.[95]

**What to avoid.** Do not force down water when you are not thirsty. Too much water can be harmful.[107] In heat, do not drink more than 6 cups an hour.[106] If you are stuck in a car in a snowstorm, staying in the car is often safest. Run the engine about 10 minutes an hour with a window cracked, and keep the exhaust pipe clear of snow.[5]

**Your numbers.** The 2-mile (3 km) trip is about 37 minutes on foot; carry about half a litre of water for it in hot weather. The get-home bag is sized by the walk home at about 3 miles an hour, with about half a litre of water per hour in hot weather.[2, 22]

**What counts toward it:**

- Person 1 travels 1.9 miles (3 km) by bike to work or school; walking home at about 3 mph takes about 0.6 hours. Keep a get-home bag at work or in your daily bag: comfortable walking shoes, a light, a paper map, cash in small bills, a rain or warm layer, and water and snacks from home. Be ready to stay at work for 24 hours if you can't leave. (FHWA, Ready.gov; some amounts are estimates)[55, 57, 104, 105]

**Also worth knowing:**

- Water for person 1's walk home: 0.6 hours × about 0.5 L an hour = 0.3 L, in a bottle filled from home rather than bought extra. (FHWA; some amounts are estimates)[57, 105]
- Snacks for person 1's walk home: about 100 kcal for 0.6 hours on foot (about 1,250 kcal every 12 hours), from your food at home rather than bought extra. (FHWA, Dietary Guidelines; some amounts are estimates)[32, 57, 105]

### Medical emergency when help is slow

Of 100 households like yours, about 100 (95–100) will have a medical emergency at least once in the next 10 years.[12, 22] Emergency departments see about 47 visits for every 100 people each year.[12] Ambulances take longer to reach rural homes.[14] After a disaster, emergency medical services are in high demand and help may be delayed.[108]

**What helps.** Learn how to stop serious bleeding. Hospitals, fire departments and health departments often offer free or low-cost classes.[15] Keep a first-aid kit at home and in the car. Check it often and replace what you use.[16] Keep each person's medicine list and allergies where a responder can find them.[17] Know how to reach 911, and call rather than text if you can.[18] CERT training teaches disaster first aid and light search and rescue.[109] Save 988 in every phone for a mental health crisis.[110]

**What to avoid.** Do not save antibiotics for later or take someone else's. The wrong medicine can delay the right treatment.[20] Do not wait for a disaster to learn first aid. People with emergency training before the World Trade Center attack were less likely to develop PTSD afterward.[111]

**What counts toward it:**

- 1 family first-aid kit for 1 person: the Red Cross list for a family of 4 (bandages, gauze, tape, antiseptic wipes, gloves, a breathing barrier, a cold pack, tweezers, an emergency blanket and a thermometer). Add your own medicines and emergency numbers, and replace what you use. (Red Cross)[16]
- 1 package of each of 4 kinds of non-prescription medicine (a pain reliever, an anti-diarrhea medicine, an antacid and a laxative): one package of each lasts about 14 days for 4 people. That is 4 packages for 10 days. Follow the label, and ask a pharmacist about children's versions. (Ready.gov, Red Cross; some amounts are estimates)[2, 16, 22, 26, 35, 36, 55, 57]
- N95 masks for wildfire smoke or an outbreak: 1 a day for each of the 1 person aged 4 and over, for 5 days = 5 masks. A well-fitting N95 protects more than a cloth or surgical mask. (CDC, Ready.gov; some amounts are estimates)[55, 57, 112]
- 1 oral thermometer (non-mercury, non-glass; it is on the Red Cross kit list). (Red Cross)[16]
- Oral rehydration salts for vomiting, diarrhea or heat illness: 3 packets a person for every 2 weeks, at least 2 weeks' worth. For 1 person and 14 days that is 3 packets. Mix each packet into 1 litre of safe water. (CDC; some amounts are estimates)[2, 22, 26, 35, 36, 57, 88, 113]

### House fire

Of 100 households like yours, about 5 (3–8) will have a home fire at least once in the next 10 years.[22, 39] There were about 345,000 home fires in the United States in 2023.[39] Cooking is by far the leading cause of home fires and home fire injuries.[114]

**What helps.** Put smoke alarms inside and outside sleeping areas and on every level of your home. Test them every month.[115] Make an escape plan with two ways out of each room, and practice it twice a year.[116] Cooking left alone is the top cause of kitchen fires that spread beyond the pan, so stay with your cooking. Keep a pan lid or baking sheet nearby to cover a pan that catches fire.[114] Keep anything that can burn at least 3 feet from heaters, fireplaces and candles.[7] A multipurpose extinguisher labeled "A-B-C" works on most home fires.[117]

**What to avoid.** If a person or pet is still inside, get out, call 911, and tell firefighters where they are.[116] Never heat your home with an oven.[7] Turn off space heaters when you leave the room or go to bed.[7] Replace 9-volt alarm batteries at least once a year.[115]

**What counts toward it:**

- A fire escape plan: two ways out of every room, a meeting spot outside, and a practice run with everyone. In a building, know both stairways and never use the elevator in a fire. Test your smoke alarms every month. (Ready.gov)[116]
- No fire extinguisher yet: 1 multipurpose (A-B-C) extinguisher for a home on one floor, kept near the kitchen. Learn how to use it before you need it. (USFA; some amounts are estimates)[57, 117]
- You live on floor 3, above the ground: 1 escape ladder for a bedroom window, in case fire or smoke blocks the way out, unless every bedroom already has a second way out such as a fire escape. Keep it by the window and practise with it. (Ready.gov; some amounts are estimates)[57, 116]

### Home and personal security

Of 100 households like yours, about 35 (20–50) will have a break-in or a nearby curfew at least once in the next 10 years.[22] About 1 in 100 households had a burglary or trespassing in 2023.[118] After disasters, panic and looting are much rarer than news stories suggest. Most people help each other.[119, 120]

**What helps.** Burglars look for homes that are easy targets. Lock up, and light the places where someone could hide.[121] Work with your neighbours to look out for each other.[121] Protect key accounts with a password manager and two-step login.[122] Learn a simple way to handle a tense moment: notice what is happening, say hello, judge the risk, and get help if you need it.[123] If an attack happens in a public place, getting away is the top priority.[124]

**What to avoid.** After a disaster, be careful with people who show up offering fast repairs or help for a fee. Report scams at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.[125] Do not hide a spare key under the doormat or a planter. Leave it with a trusted neighbour.[121]

**What counts toward it:**

- Swap phone numbers with 2 neighbours and agree who checks on whom after a storm or an outage, when phones and roads may be down. (an estimate)[57]

### Home damaged or uninhabitable

Of 100 households like yours, about 4 (3–7) will have to leave home for a while because of damage at least once in the next 10 years.[22, 37, 38, 39, 40, 97, 126] Fires, floods, storms and earthquakes can make a home unsafe to live in. In the year before December 2022, about 16 of every 1,000 U.S. households were forced from home by a disaster.[97] Renters were about half as likely as owners to have insurance, and less likely to have money set aside.[127]

**What helps.** Check your insurance now. A standard homeowner's policy does not cover earthquake damage, and most do not cover flooding.[89, 93] A new flood policy usually takes 30 days to start, so buy it well before storm season.[128] Renters can buy flood coverage for their belongings.[129] Photograph or video your home and belongings. Keep copies of key documents in a fireproof, waterproof box, with a trusted person, or in secure files away from home.[96]

**What to avoid.** Do not keep your only copies of documents at home. Do not wait until a storm is on the way to buy flood insurance.[128] Be careful with anyone who shows up offering quick repairs, and report scams.[125]

**Your numbers.** After a disaster, about 1 in 3 displaced households are back within a week, but about 1 in 4 renters never return, so insurance and copies of documents matter more than supplies here. Renters insurance that pays for somewhere to stay is the main protection if your home becomes unlivable.[22, 37, 38, 39, 40, 97, 126]

**What counts toward it:**

- Copy the 4 parts of FEMA's Emergency Financial First Aid Kit: IDs, financial and legal papers (including insurance), medical information, and household contacts. Keep copies in a waterproof, fire-resistant place or with someone you trust, with photos of your home and belongings, and update them once a year. (FEMA)[96]
- You have no renters insurance yet: get a quote and decide. Check what it pays if you have to live somewhere else for a while. (FEMA, Ready.gov)[93, 96]
- Home and renters policies usually don't cover floods. Decide on flood insurance before you need it: a new policy usually starts 30 days after you buy it. It pays up to $250,000 for the building and $100,000 for belongings, and renters can buy cover for belongings only. (FEMA, FloodSmart, Ready.gov)[93, 128, 129]

The effects of an electromagnetic pulse on home electronics are highly uncertain, so this is optional and never a priority; a battery radio and general supplies are the main protection.[27, 44, 58]
- **Warm sleeping bag or heavy wool blanket for each person** (usually $25–56 per person): A sleeping bag rated for your coldest nights, or a heavy wool blanket, for each person, as Ready.gov lists in its basic kit. With a warm room plan this covers a winter outage when the heating needs electricity.[5, 55, 58]
- **Indoor room thermometer** (usually $2–15 per thermometer): A simple room thermometer, so you know when it is too hot for a fan to help (90°F) or too cold for an infant to sleep, and when to leave for a cooling center or warming shelter (CDC).[5, 8, 58]

### Contacts and meeting places

- **Make a family emergency plan.** Agree how you will reach each other, where you will meet if you are separated, and who picks up the children (Ready.gov). Include each person's medical needs, pets and school or work plans, then practice it.[95, 133]
- **Choose an out-of-area contact everyone can text.** Pick one relative or friend in another state whom everyone in the household will text to say they are safe (Ready.gov). Texts often get through when calls do not, and they use less battery.[89, 95]
- **Written contact card for each person.** Write key phone numbers on a card for each person's wallet or bag: family, the out-of-area contact, work, school, doctor and 988. Ready.gov advises storing important numbers somewhere besides your phone, and offers a fillable card.[92, 96, 133]
- **Turn on emergency alerts on every phone.** Check that Wireless Emergency Alerts are switched on in each phone's settings. They are free and need no sign-up (Ready.gov), but a phone only gets them when it is on, not in airplane mode, and served by a carrier that takes part (FCC).[90, 134]
- **Sign up for your county's emergency alerts.** Sign up for your county or city's emergency text and email alerts, which carry local warnings such as evacuation orders and boil-water notices. Ready.gov's planning questions start with how you will receive alerts and warnings.[90, 95]
- **Know how to reach 911 by call or text.** Always call 911 when you can, because a voice call gives the call center more information; text-to-911 works only in some places (FCC). Find out whether your county accepts texts, which helps when speaking is unsafe or impossible.[18]

### Leaving home: triggers and routes

About 15 of 100 households like yours have to leave home quickly at least once in 10 years. Warning can be 1 minute to 12 hours ahead. Plan to be away for about 2 days.[22, 33, 34, 37, 38, 39, 40]

- **Know your evacuation zone, routes and where you would go.** Many states set evacuation zones and routes; look up yours on your state and county emergency management websites (Ready.gov). Pick where you would go: a friend's or relative's home out of the area, a pet-friendly hotel, or a public shelter.[98, 135, 136, 137]
- **Agree if-then triggers for leaving.** Write short promises such as 'If the county issues an evacuation warning for our zone, we leave within 30 minutes; the bag is by the door; Sam gets the kids.' Plans in if-then form make people much more likely to follow through.[100, 138]
- **Write a go-or-stay card for each likely hazard.** For each hazard your plan names, write one card that says when you leave, when you stay and shelter, and what you do first. People who get complete warnings spend less time checking with others before acting, and a card decided in advance works the same way.[101, 138]
- **Run ten-minute drills twice a year.** Practice a fire escape, grabbing the go-bag against a timer, or Drop, Cover and Hold On, for ten minutes at a time. Ready.gov advises practicing your home fire escape plan twice a year, and people who have drilled act faster and cope better in real emergencies.[111, 116, 139]
- **Never walk or drive through floodwater.** Just six inches of moving water can knock you down and one foot can sweep a vehicle away (Ready.gov). Turn around and find another way, and move to higher ground or a higher floor if water rises.[102, 140]
- **Pick your tornado shelter spot.** Choose where you will go when a tornado warning sounds: a safe room or storm shelter if you have one, otherwise a small, interior, windowless room or basement on the lowest floor of a sturdy building (Ready.gov). If you live in a mobile home, plan which sturdy building you would go to instead.[24]

#### Drills and if-then plans

Supplies help most when you already know what to do with them. Practice and simple rules made in advance turn a plan into action.

**If-then plans.** An if-then plan links a trigger to an action: "If the county issues an evacuation warning for our zone, we leave within 30 minutes." Plans written this way help people follow through.[100] When a warning comes, people often wait to confirm it with others before they act.[101] A trigger you agreed on earlier leaves less to decide in the moment. Write one for each likely hazard, and add who picks up the children and where you will meet.[95]

**Drills.** In New Zealand, people who took part in earthquake drills knew the right actions better, used them more during real shaking, and did more to prepare.[139] Among people who got out of the World Trade Center on 9/11, those with earlier emergency training were less likely to develop PTSD.[111] Practice your fire escape plan twice a year.[116] Other ten-minute drills: Drop, Cover and Hold On, a night without power, grabbing the go-bag against a timer, and sealing a room to shelter in place.[138]

**What to avoid.** Do not skip drills because they feel silly. Each one you finish counts toward your plan.

### School, work and getting home

- **Plan and walk part of your route home.** Map two walking routes from work to home and walk part of one. Plan on about 2 to 2.4 miles an hour with a loaded bag, and about 24 to 32 ounces of water an hour in heat, so a 12-mile walk takes five to six hours.[31, 105, 106]
- **Know your workplace's emergency plan.** Ask how your workplace would shelter people if roads closed; Ready.gov's work-kit advice is to be ready to shelter at work for at least 24 hours. Keep your medicines, walking shoes and a phone charger there.[104]

### Neighbors

- **Get to know two neighbours and swap phone numbers.** Introduce yourself to at least two neighbours and save each other's numbers. After the 2011 Japan tsunami, places with stronger social ties had fewer deaths, especially among older and low-income people. About 7 in 10 people expect help from friends and family first (FEMA's 2024 survey).[127, 141, 142]
- **Agree who checks on whom.** Agree with neighbours or relatives who checks on whom during heat waves, storms and outages, especially older people who live alone. In the 1995 Chicago heat wave, living alone and not leaving home each day made death far more likely, and regular social contact was protective.[8, 80, 143]
- **Make a simple street contact list.** With willing neighbours, list names, phone numbers and anything useful in an emergency: who needs help to evacuate, who has a generator or medical skills, and who has pets. State programs such as Listos California offer templates for exactly this.[143, 144]
- **Join or start a block group, or take CERT training.** Join a neighbourhood preparedness group or take free Community Emergency Response Team (CERT) training, which teaches fire safety, light search and rescue, team organization and disaster first aid (Ready.gov). Trained neighbours can help until responders arrive.[109, 111, 144]

#### Neighbours and mutual aid

Neighbours are a supply, and the plan counts them. After Japan's 2011 tsunami, towns with stronger community ties had lower death rates.[141] In neighbourhoods flooded by the same tsunami, strong ties mattered most for older and lower-income people.[142] In the 1995 Chicago heat wave, living alone raised the risk of dying.[80]

**The gap.** About 7 in 10 people expect help from friends and family in a disaster, but fewer than 2 in 10 had heard how to help their neighbours.[127]

**What helps.** Swap phone numbers with two neighbours. Agree who checks on whom, especially older neighbours and anyone who lives alone. Make a simple contact list for your street. Give a trusted person a spare key, and tell them where your supplies are and how to use any medical equipment.[143] Host a block party, since strong communities start with prepared neighbours.[144] Take CERT training to learn fire safety, light search and rescue, and disaster first aid.[109]

**What to avoid.** Do not wait for a disaster to meet the people next door. A short hello now makes it easier to knock on a door later.

## Documents and money

Keep paper copies in a waterproof pouch and photos you can reach from any phone. FEMA's Emergency Financial First Aid Kit groups them in four parts.[96]

- [ ] **Who you are:** photo IDs, birth certificates, Social Security cards, passports, and pet records.
- [ ] **Money and legal papers:** insurance policies, the lease or deed, bank and card contact numbers (not PINs), and recent tax returns.
- [ ] **Medical papers:** insurance cards, the written medicine list, prescriptions, and vaccination records.
- [ ] **Contacts:** family, doctors, the insurance agent, the landlord or lender, and employers.

- **Copy and protect your key documents.** Use FEMA's Emergency Financial First Aid Kit to gather identification, financial and legal papers, insurance, medical information and contacts. Keep phone photos, paper copies in a waterproof bag, and a copy with someone you trust or in secure online storage.[55, 93, 96]
- **Photograph or video your home and belongings.** Walk through your home recording each room, the contents of closets and drawers, and serial numbers of costly items, as the first step of FEMA's financial kit. It makes insurance and aid claims faster after a fire or flood.[96]
- **Fire-resistant, waterproof document pouch.** A fire-resistant, water-resistant pouch or small case for paper copies of key documents, a USB drive and some cash, easy to grab when you leave (FEMA's financial kit suggests fire- and waterproof storage).[58, 96]

### Insurance questions

- You have no renters insurance yet: get a quote and decide. Check what it pays if you have to live somewhere else for a while. (FEMA, Ready.gov)[93, 96]
- Home and renters policies usually don't cover floods. Decide on flood insurance before you need it: a new policy usually starts 30 days after you buy it. It pays up to $250,000 for the building and $100,000 for belongings, and renters can buy cover for belongings only. (FEMA, FloodSmart, Ready.gov)[93, 128, 129]

- **Review your insurance: home or renters, flood, earthquake.** Check what your policy covers and decide on the gaps: standard home and renters policies do not cover flood or earthquake damage (Ready.gov), and a new federal flood policy usually takes 30 days to start (FEMA). Renters can buy contents-only flood cover.[89, 93, 102, 128, 129]
- **Renters: get a renters-insurance quote.** Renters are about half as likely as owners to have insurance (FEMA's 2024 survey), yet a fire or flood can take everything they own and force a move. Get a quote for renters insurance with additional living expenses, then decide.[93, 96, 127]

#### Preparing when you rent

Renters face disasters with less cushion. In FEMA's 2024 survey, 43 of every 100 renters had insurance, compared with 87 of every 100 owners. Renters were also less likely to have money set aside.[127] After a disaster, renters are more likely to be out of their homes for a long time. Nearly 1 in 4 displaced renter households never went back, compared with 1 in 10 owners.[97]

**What helps.** Get renters insurance if you do not have it, and keep it up to date.[93, 96] Standard renters policies usually do not cover floods, but you can buy flood coverage for your belongings.[129] Photograph your belongings and keep copies of your lease and key documents away from home.[96] Most preparation needs no drilling or permission: water in bottles, a go-bag, a contact card and neighbours' numbers. For power, a battery power station works indoors because it burns no fuel.[56]

**What to avoid.** Do not run a generator on a balcony or near windows. It must be outdoors, more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents.[56] Do not assume the building has a plan. Ask where the exits, shut-offs and meeting points are.

### Cash

- Cash in small bills, kept with your documents, because ATMs and cards may not work in an outage: about $100 to start, or enough for about 3 days of basics (food, fuel, medicine) at your own daily spending. No agency gives a dollar amount. (Ready.gov, FEMA; some amounts are estimates)[2, 22, 37, 38, 57, 93, 96]

### Savings

No one in the household earns wages, so the plan sets no income-gap goal. Keep a small cushion for emergencies if you can.

- **Start an emergency fund, even a small one.** Set up an automatic transfer, however small, into a savings account you keep for emergencies. The CFPB says the right amount depends on your situation; planners often suggest working toward three to six months of expenses (FINRA), and the plan shows a target for your household.[145, 146, 147, 148]

## Special needs

### Antibiotics

- No antibiotics are included in this plan. If you have a specific, foreseeable need, such as remote travel, talk to your own clinician about a standby prescription with written instructions. Never use fish or pet antibiotics, never share prescriptions, and don't use expired ones. (CDC, FDA)[20, 21, 87, 88]

#### Antibiotics and emergency antibiotic kits

Some companies sell "emergency antibiotic kits" for disaster preparedness. Ready Reckoner never sets an antibiotic quantity. Here is why, and what to do instead.

**What the kits are.** By federal law, antibiotics for people need a prescription.[149] The kits are sold through an online questionnaire that a prescriber signs off, and they typically cost $300 to $400. A 2026 medical review found that this model prescribes for an illness no one has diagnosed. That invites the wrong drug, the wrong dose and the wrong length of treatment. It can also cause side effects and resistant infections.[19]

**When a standby prescription can make sense.** CDC notes that travelers often carry some prescribed medicines for specific risks where they are going.[88] The same review suggests telehealth with a clinician who knows you, and keeping prescriptions made in advance for specific high-risk cases.[19] If a specific situation worries you, ask your own clinician.

**What to avoid.** Do not save antibiotics for later, share them, or take someone else's.[20] Do not use expired antibiotics. They can fail to treat an infection.[21] Never use aquarium, bird or other animal antibiotics. The FDA calls these products unapproved and unsafe.[87]

Nobody in the household listed medical or access needs. Keep a written medicine list anyway, and update this plan if that changes.

### Stress and mental health

#### Stress, mental health and the 988 line

Stress before, during and after a disaster is normal. In FEMA's 2024 survey, the most common worries were health and paying bills, well ahead of disasters.[127] Preparation helps with more than supplies. Among people who got out of the World Trade Center on 9/11, those with earlier emergency training were less likely to develop PTSD.[111]

**What helps.** Save 988 in every phone. It offers free, judgment-free support 24/7 for mental health, substance use and more, by call, text or chat.[110] After a disaster, the Disaster Distress Helpline offers crisis counseling 24/7 to anyone in emotional distress. Call or text 1-800-985-5990.[150] Write a short calm plan: what you might feel, what helps you, and who you will call.[138] Oregon's 2 Weeks Ready program even includes practicing mindfulness as a step in getting prepared.[108]

**What to avoid.** Do not keep the news on all day. No one, adult or child, benefits from graphic details or disturbing images.[151] Do not wait until things feel unbearable to reach out. The 988 line supports people in emotional distress as well as in crisis.[110]

#### Rotating supplies through everyday use

The easiest supplies to keep fresh are the ones you use. Store the foods your household already eats, use the oldest first, and replace them as you shop.[29, 152]

**What to check, and when.** Replace water you bottled yourself every six months.[64] Look through your pantry every few weeks and use cans you have had for a while.[29] Dates on most foods are about quality, not safety. Infant formula is an exception, and its date must be followed.[29] Test smoke alarms every month, and replace 9-volt batteries at least once a year.[115] Check first-aid supplies for used or expired items.[16] Update your documents at tax time, when the clocks change, on your birthday or at the new year.[96] Rotate pet water and food in the go-kit every two months.[137]

**Make it a habit.** Tie checks to things you already do, like grocery trips or the clock change. New habits take time. In one study, they took from 18 to 254 days to become automatic, and missing a day now and then did not set people back.[153] One missed month is fine.

**What to avoid.** Do not store food in a hot garage or attic. Heat cuts shelf life sharply.[154]
