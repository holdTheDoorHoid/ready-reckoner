## Your risks

The events most likely to reach a household like yours in Coos County, Oregon, over the next 10 years, most likely first. A lost job or a week-long outage is far more likely than a disaster from the movies, and the plan is ordered the same way.

### The ones most likely to reach you

#### 1. Strong wind

Nearly every household like yours will lose power or have damage in a windstorm in the next ten years (about 1.1 times a year).[2, 3]

**What helps.** Pick your shelter spot now. A safe room or storm shelter is best. The next best is a small, windowless room or basement on the lowest floor of a sturdy building.[4] Sign up for your community's warnings, and learn what your sirens sound like.[4] When thunder roars, go indoors. Get into a building or a car with a roof.[5]

**What to avoid.** Stay away from windows, doors and outside walls.[4] During a thunderstorm, avoid running water and electronics plugged into the wall.[5]

**What it can do:** no power, no phone, internet or card payments and damage to the home. **How bad:** Minor. **How sure:** Rough data.

#### 2. Medical emergency

Nearly every household like yours will have someone need emergency care in the next ten years.[6, 7]

**What helps.** Take a first-aid and bleeding-control class. You may be able to save a life by acting right after an injury, before help arrives.[8] Keep a stocked first-aid kit. Check it regularly and replace anything used or out of date.[9] Keep a list of each person's medicines, doses and allergies with your emergency supplies.[10] Know how to reach 911. Call if you can, and text where that service works.[11] Set up telehealth with your own clinician before you need it.[12]

**What to avoid.** Do not start antibiotics on your own or take someone else's. The wrong medicine can delay the right treatment and cause serious side effects.[13] Do not use expired medicines.[14]

**What it can do:** a medical emergency. **How bad:** Serious. **How sure:** Based on data.

#### 3. Cold wave

Of 100 households like yours, about 96 will go through a spell of dangerous cold in the next ten years.[2]

**What helps.** Keep extra blankets, sleeping bags and warm coats, and pick one room to keep warm.[15] Learn how to keep pipes from freezing, and test smoke and carbon monoxide alarms before winter.[16] Keep a car kit with jumper cables, sand, a flashlight, warm clothes, blankets, water and snacks, and keep the gas tank full.[16] Check on older neighbours often.[15] Know the signs of hypothermia, such as shivering, confusion and slurred speech. A body temperature below 95°F is an emergency. Warm the center of the body first.[16]

**What to avoid.** Never heat your home with a gas stove or oven. Use generators and grills only outdoors, away from windows.[16] Choose electric space heaters with automatic shut-off, and keep them 3 feet from anything that can burn.[15, 17] Do not overdo it when you shovel snow.[16]

**What it can do:** no power, no tap water and dangerous heat or cold at home. **How bad:** Moderate. **How sure:** Based on data.

#### 4. Heat wave

Of 100 households like yours, about 95 will go through a heat wave in the next ten years.[2]

**What helps.** Know where you can go to cool off. Dial 2-1-1 to find air conditioning near you.[18] Cover windows with drapes or shades, and weather-strip doors and windows.[19] Take cool showers, wear loose, light-colored clothing, and drink plenty of fluids. Check on family members, older adults and neighbours.[19] Heat can cause power outages, so plan for refrigerated medicines and powered medical devices.[18] In a drought, follow local limits on water use, and make saving water a daily habit.[20]

**What to avoid.** Do not rely on a fan when it is over 90°F indoors. It can make you hotter.[18] Never leave people or pets in a closed car on a warm day. Heat stroke is an emergency. Signs include a body temperature above 103°F, hot, red, dry skin, a fast pulse and confusion. Call 911.[19]

**What it can do:** no power and dangerous heat or cold at home. **How bad:** Minor. **How sure:** Based on data.

#### 5. Stranded in a vehicle

Of 100 households like yours, about 95 (63 to nearly all) will be stranded away from home by a crash, breakdown or shutdown in the next ten years.[3, 21]

**What helps.** Keep a car kit with jumper cables, sand, a flashlight, warm clothes, blankets, water and snacks.[16] Keep the gas tank at least half full.[22] Check the pressure in all your tires, including the spare, at least once a month.[23] Before a winter trip, tell someone where you are going and when you expect to arrive, and ask them to call for help if you are late.[15] If you drive an electric car, know its range and where you can charge along your route.[24, 25]

**What to avoid.** If you are stuck in a snowstorm, staying in the car is often safest. Tie a bright cloth to the antenna. Run the engine about 10 minutes an hour with a window open a little, and keep the exhaust pipe clear of snow.[15] Never drive into a flooded road or around the barriers.[26]

**What it can do:** being stranded away from home. **How bad:** Minor. **How sure:** Mostly data.

#### 6. Job loss

Of 100 households like yours, about 92 (77–99) will have someone lose a job in the next ten years.[3, 27, 28]

**What helps.** Build an emergency fund a little at a time. The right amount depends on your situation.[29] Planners often suggest three to six months of living expenses, and more if your income varies.[30] Know how to file for unemployment in your state.[31] Keep copies of your financial, legal and medical papers together, with the names of people to call.[32] Write a bare-bones budget you could switch to, and keep a pantry that can stretch a tight month.

**What to avoid.** Do not wait to file. Contact your state's unemployment program as soon as possible after losing work.[31] Do not keep your emergency fund in investments you would have to sell. A savings account lets you take money out at any time without a penalty.[30]

**What it can do:** lost income. **How bad:** Serious. **How sure:** Mostly data.

#### 7. Earthquake

Of 100 households like yours, about 11 will feel an earthquake strong enough to knock things off shelves in the next ten years.[33, 34]

**What helps.** Practice Drop, Cover and Hold On: drop to your hands and knees, cover your head and neck, and hold on until the shaking stops.[35] Secure bookcases, water heaters, TVs and other heavy things, and keep heavy objects on low shelves. A standard homeowner's policy does not cover earthquake damage.[35] Store water in case the taps stop. On the coast, the shaking is your warning. As soon as it stops, walk to high ground or inland without waiting for an alert.[36] A loud roar from the ocean, or water that suddenly drains away, is another sign.[37]

**What to avoid.** Do not run outside during shaking, and stay away from doorways.[35] Do not go back to the shore until officials say it is safe. Tsunami waves can keep coming for hours.[36]

**What it can do:** no power, no tap water, no way to get to a store, gaps in medicine, no phone, internet or card payments, a medical emergency and damage to the home. **How bad:** Very severe. **How sure:** Mostly data.

#### 8. Tsunami

Of 100 households like yours, about 9 (3–18) will have to leave home or work for a tsunami warning in the next ten years.[3, 34, 38]

**What it can do:** having to leave home and damage to the home. **How bad:** Severe. **How sure:** Rough data.

### Rare but severe

Shown apart, because a tiny chance times a huge loss would otherwise crowd out everything else. The plan never lets them take over the budget.

| What | How likely | How bad |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nuclear attack | Forecasters asked in 2024 put the chance of a nuclear catastrophe (10 million or more deaths worldwide) before 2045 at 1 to 5 in 100. Spread over those years, that is about 1 in 2,000 to about 1 in 400 a year. No reliable estimate exists for effects where you live. The first 24 hours of sheltering inside are covered by your basic supplies.[47, 48] | Very severe |
| Terrorist attack | An attack that disrupts daily life where you live is rare: expert estimates range from about 1 in 50,000 to about 1 in 5,000 a year.[3] | Very severe |

**What helps.** Get inside, stay inside, stay tuned.[48] Go to a basement or the middle of a large brick or concrete building. Stay there for 24 hours unless there is a fire or another immediate danger, or officials say it is safe to leave. Radiation levels drop fast during the first 24 hours.[48] Turn off fans and air conditioning that pull in outside air.[49] If you were outside, take off your outer layer of clothing. That can remove up to 90% of radioactive material. Then wash with soap and water.[49] If you live near a nuclear power plant, learn whether you are in its planning zone.[50]

**What to avoid.** Do not take potassium iodide unless public health or emergency officials tell you to. It protects only the thyroid.[51] Near a plant, states can give out tablets to people within 10 miles, on official instruction.[52] Do not spend on special gear before your basic supplies are in place.[53]

### No grid power at home: about 6 months (2 months to 1 year)

**What helps.** Have a flashlight for each person.[54] Keep a battery or hand-crank radio, and a backup battery for each phone.[55] Keep the fridge and freezer closed. A closed fridge keeps food cold for about four hours, and a full freezer for about two days.[54] If someone relies on a powered medical device or cold medicine, make a plan with their medical provider now.[54] You can also ask your power company to put you on its list for priority restoration.[10] A battery power station burns no fuel, so it makes no carbon monoxide.[56]

**What to avoid.** Never run a generator inside your home or garage, even with the doors and windows open. Keep it outside, more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents.[56] Put a carbon monoxide alarm on every level of your home.[54] Never heat your home with a gas stove or oven. Unplug electronics, because the power can come back with a surge that damages them.[54]

### No tap water at all: about 1 year (6 months to 1 year)

**What helps.** Store at least one gallon per person per day, for drinking and keeping clean.[1] Fill clean bottles or jugs with tap water and replace it every six months.[57] You can pick a level in the plan. About 3 liters a day covers drinking to survive, one gallon is the basic default, and about 4 gallons (15 liters) is the humanitarian minimum for drinking, cooking and washing.[58] A tank water heater holds 20 to 80 gallons of clean water.[59, 60] For long outages, a filter and a source of raw water go further than more jugs.[53] Filters do not remove viruses, so add a disinfectant such as chlorine dioxide after filtering.[61] A two-bucket toilet keeps waste safe when toilets cannot flush.[62]

**What to avoid.** Do not store water in containers that held chemicals.[57] Milk jugs seal poorly and turn brittle.[63] Never drink pool or spa water. Use it only for washing and cleaning.[60]

### Can't get to a store: about 2 months (1½–6)

**What helps.** Keep extra food that needs no refrigeration, cooking or water, and choose foods your family will eat.[64] Use the oldest cans first and replace them as you go.[65] Buy a little at a time. Ready.gov asks people to buy supplies slowly, so everyone can get what they need.[66] Keep a manual can opener.[64] The plan counts food in calories, not servings. Many "30-day" meal kits hold only about 1,300 to 1,700 calories a day.[67] That is less than most adults need.[68] Store food somewhere cool and dry. Below 85°F is best.[65]

**What to avoid.** Do not buy everything at once. It strains your budget and the store. Do not buy or eat from cans that bulge, leak, are rusted or are deeply dented.[65] Do not trust a kit's "days" label. Check the calories on the package instead.

### Dangerous heat or cold indoors: about 2 weeks (10 days to 2 weeks)

**What helps.** Agree who checks on whom, especially older neighbours and anyone who lives alone.[15, 18] In heat, go where there is air conditioning. Dial 2-1-1 to find one.[18] Cover windows with drapes or shades.[19] In cold, close off rooms you don't need, stuff towels under doors, and cover windows with blankets at night. Have extra blankets, sleeping bags and warm coats.[15] Babies under one year old should never sleep in a cold room.[15]

**What to avoid.** Fans move air but do not lower body temperature.[19] Above 90°F indoors, a fan can make you hotter.[18] Never heat your home with a gas oven, and never use a grill or camp stove indoors.[56] Keep space heaters 3 feet from anything that can burn.[17] Never leave people or pets in a closed car on a warm day.[19]

### Medication and medical-supply continuity: about 3 months (1½–6)

**What helps.** Refill early, while you still have a week left. The Red Cross suggests keeping a 7-day supply, and Florida advises at least two weeks.[69, 70] People with diabetes should pack supplies for at least 1 to 2 weeks.[71] Talk to your doctor or pharmacist about building an emergency supply, and keep a written list of each person's medicines, doses and allergies.[10] Many states let pharmacists give an emergency refill, but the rules differ from state to state.[72] After a declared disaster, a federal program can help people without insurance replace prescriptions.[73] If a medicine must stay cold, ask how long it can be kept warm, and plan a cooler.[54]

**What to avoid.** If the power is out for more than a day, throw away medicine that should be refrigerated, unless the label says otherwise.[54] Keep insulin cool but never frozen.[74] Do not use expired medicines. Weak antibiotics can fail to treat an infection.[14] Do not share antibiotics or save them for later.[13]

### No phone, internet or card payments: about 1½ months (3 weeks to 3 months)

**What helps.** Turn on emergency alerts on every phone. They are free, and you do not need to sign up.[75] Keep a battery or hand-crank radio and a NOAA Weather Radio with a tone alert.[55] NOAA Weather Radio has more than 1,000 transmitters covering all 50 states.[76] Write important phone numbers on paper, not only in your phone.[77] Pick an out-of-state contact everyone can text, and a place to meet if you are separated.[35] Keep some cash in small bills, because ATMs and credit cards may not work.[78] License-free two-way radios (FRS) let family members talk over short distances without a phone network.[79]

**What to avoid.** Do not rely on one source of news. Do not assume you can text 911 everywhere. It is not available in every area, so call 911 if you can.[11]

### House fire

**What helps.** Put smoke alarms inside and outside sleeping areas and on every level of your home. Test them every month.[82] Make an escape plan with two ways out of each room, and practice it twice a year.[83] Cooking left alone is the top cause of kitchen fires that spread beyond the pan, so stay with your cooking. Keep a pan lid or baking sheet nearby to cover a pan that catches fire.[84] Keep anything that can burn at least 3 feet from heaters, fireplaces and candles.[17] A multipurpose extinguisher labeled "A-B-C" works on most home fires.[85]

**What to avoid.** If a person or pet is still inside, get out, call 911, and tell firefighters where they are.[83] Never heat your home with an oven.[17] Turn off space heaters when you leave the room or go to bed.[17] Replace 9-volt alarm batteries at least once a year.[82]

### Home and personal security

**What helps.** Burglars look for homes that are easy targets. Lock up, and light the places where someone could hide.[86] Work with your neighbours to look out for each other.[86] Protect key accounts with a password manager and two-step login.[87] Learn a simple way to handle a tense moment: notice what is happening, say hello, judge the risk, and get help if you need it.[88] If an attack happens in a public place, getting away is the top priority.[89]

**What to avoid.** After a disaster, be careful with people who show up offering fast repairs or help for a fee. Report scams at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.[90] Do not hide a spare key under the doormat or a planter. Leave it with a trusted neighbour.[86]

### Contacts and meeting places

- **Make a household plan and a contact card for each person.** Agree how you will reach each other, where you will meet if you are separated, and who picks up the children (Ready.gov). Pick one out-of-state contact everyone texts to say they are safe, since texts often get through when calls do not, and write key numbers on a card for each wallet or bag (Ready.gov). Add school, daycare and work plans, and go over the plan together once a year.[32, 35, 77, 97, 101, 102]
- **Set up emergency alerts and know how to reach 911.** Make sure every phone can get warnings: Wireless Emergency Alerts are free and need no sign-up (Ready.gov), but a phone only gets them when it is on, not in airplane mode, and served by a carrier that takes part (FCC). Add your county's text and email alerts and your power company's outage texts for local news. Call 911 when you can, because texting 911 works only in some places (FCC).[11, 54, 75, 101, 103]

### Leaving home: triggers and routes

About 20 of 100 households like yours have to leave home quickly at least once in 10 years. Warning can be 1 minute to 12 hours ahead. Plan to be away for about 2 days.[3, 34, 36, 38, 39, 44, 45, 46]

- **Plan how you would leave: zone, routes, destination and triggers.** Look up your evacuation zone and routes on your state and county emergency management websites, mark two routes on a paper map, and pick where you would go, pet-friendly if you have pets (Ready.gov). For each likely hazard, write a go-or-stay card and an if-then trigger, such as 'If our zone gets an evacuation warning, we leave within 30 minutes.' Plans written in if-then form make people more likely to follow through.[22, 55, 80, 104, 105, 106, 107, 108]
- **Keep your vehicle ready: half a tank, a charged battery, a good spare.** Keep at least half a tank of gas at all times and a full tank when an evacuation looks likely (Ready.gov); if you drive an electric car, keep it charged through storm season and pick destinations within its range (Hillsborough County, Florida). Check the spare tire with the others each month, and keep a phone charging cable in the car (NHTSA). Run the engine only outdoors, never in a garage (CDC).[16, 22, 23, 24, 25, 56, 95, 109]

- **Practice: ten-minute drills and the first things to do.**

#### Drills and if-then plans

**If-then plans.** An if-then plan links a trigger to an action: "If the county issues an evacuation warning for our zone, we leave within 30 minutes." Plans written this way help people follow through.[107] When a warning comes, people often wait to confirm it with others before they act.[80] A trigger you agreed on earlier leaves less to decide in the moment. Write one for each likely hazard, and add who picks up the children and where you will meet.[101]

**Drills.** In New Zealand, people who took part in earthquake drills knew the right actions better, used them more during real shaking, and did more to prepare.[110] Among people who got out of the World Trade Center on 9/11, those with earlier emergency training were less likely to develop PTSD.[111] Practice your fire escape plan twice a year.[83] Other ten-minute drills: Drop, Cover and Hold On, a night without power, grabbing the go-bag against a timer, and sealing a room to shelter in place.[108]

**What to avoid.** Do not skip drills because they feel silly. Each one you finish counts toward your plan.

### Neighbors

- **Neighbours and skills: swap numbers, check on each other, learn first aid.**

#### Neighbours and mutual aid

**The gap.** About 7 in 10 people expect help from friends and family in a disaster, but fewer than 2 in 10 had heard how to help their neighbours.[112]

**What helps.** Swap phone numbers with two neighbours. Agree who checks on whom, especially older neighbours and anyone who lives alone. Make a simple contact list for your street. Give a trusted person a spare key, and tell them where your supplies are and how to use any medical equipment.[113] Host a block party, since strong communities start with prepared neighbours.[114] Take CERT training to learn fire safety, light search and rescue, and disaster first aid.[115]

**What to avoid.** Do not wait for a disaster to meet the people next door. A short hello now makes it easier to knock on a door later.

- [ ] **Money and legal papers:** insurance policies, the lease or deed, bank and card contact numbers (not PINs), and recent tax returns.
- [ ] **Medical papers:** insurance cards, the written medicine list, prescriptions, and vaccination records.
- [ ] **Contacts:** family, doctors, the insurance agent, the landlord or lender, and employers.

### Insurance questions

- Home and renters policies usually don't cover floods. Decide on flood insurance before you need it: a new policy usually starts 30 days after you buy it. It pays up to $250,000 for the building and $100,000 for belongings. (FEMA, FloodSmart, Ready.gov)[78, 116, 117]
- A standard home policy does not cover earthquake damage. Decide whether earthquake insurance is worth its deductible where you live. (Ready.gov)[35]

### Medicine

- 1 person takes prescription medicine every day: keep 30 days of it on hand. Your target is 90 days; beyond 30 days, ask your prescriber or insurer about a longer fill. ### Stress and mental health

#### Stress, mental health and the 988 line

**What helps.** Save 988 in every phone. It offers free, judgment-free support 24/7 for mental health, substance use and more, by call, text or chat.[128] After a disaster, the Disaster Distress Helpline offers crisis counseling 24/7 to anyone in emotional distress. Call or text 1-800-985-5990.[129] Write a short calm plan: what you might feel, what helps you, and who you will call.[108] Oregon's 2 Weeks Ready program even includes practicing mindfulness as a step in getting prepared.[91]

**What to avoid.** Do not keep the news on all day. No one, adult or child, benefits from graphic details or disturbing images.[130] Do not wait until things feel unbearable to reach out. The 988 line supports people in emotional distress as well as in crisis.[128]
