### Leaving home

Warning can be as short as a few minutes (a home fire) or as long as 12 hours. For chemical spills and releases, plan for as little as a few minutes of warning; for wildfires, 15 minutes. ### Staying in touch

**Four ways to reach each other.** Emergency planners call this a PACE plan: a primary way, an alternate, a contingency and an emergency way. Each one takes over when the one before it fails.[15] For a household it can be:

1. Primary: call.
2. Alternate: text. A text may get through when a call does not, and it saves battery.[16]
3. Contingency: everyone texts one out-of-state contact, who passes the news along.[16]
4. Emergency: go to the place you agreed to meet if you get separated.[16]

## Your risks

What could reach a household like yours in Sacramento County, California over the next 10 years: the likeliest events, and the rarer ones that can kill, most likely first.

### What to know and do

#### 1. Wildfire smoke

Nearly every household like yours will go through days of unhealthy wildfire smoke in the next ten years (about 2 times a year).[10, 17, 18]

**What helps.** If anyone has heart or lung disease, ask their health care provider now what to do on smoky days. If someone has asthma, make sure they have an asthma action plan.[19] Keep at least five days of food and medicine at home, so you can stay in.[20] When smoke is forecast, check the air quality on AirNow or your local news, and set up your clean room.[19] Staying indoors is the best protection. Keep two-strap respirators for anyone who has to go out.[21]

**What to avoid.** Avoid hard exercise while it is smoky.[19] Do not stay in a home that is too hot or still smoky inside: go to a friend's home out of the smoke, or a public cleaner-air shelter.[20]

**How bad:** Serious (mostly data).

#### 2. Heat wave

Nearly every household like yours will go through a heat wave in the next ten years (about 1.5 times a year).[22]

**Includes:** a blackout during a heat wave.

**What helps.** Know where you can go to cool off. Dial 2-1-1 to find air conditioning near you.[23] Cover windows with drapes or shades, and weather-strip doors and windows.[24] Take cool showers, wear loose, light-colored clothing, and drink plenty of fluids. Check on family members, older adults and neighbours.[24] Heat can cause power outages, so plan for refrigerated medicines and powered medical devices.[23] In a drought, follow local limits on water use, and make saving water a daily habit.[25]

**What to avoid.** Do not rely on a fan when it is over 90°F indoors. It can make you hotter.[23] Never leave people or pets in a closed car on a warm day.[24] Heat stroke is an emergency. Signs include a body temperature of 103°F or more, hot, red skin that may be dry or damp, a fast, strong pulse, confusion or passing out. Call 911, move the person somewhere cooler, cool them with cool cloths or a cool bath, and give them nothing to drink.[26]

**How bad:** Serious (based on data).

#### 3. Medical emergency

Nearly every household like yours will have someone need emergency care in the next ten years.[27]

**Includes:** far from emergency care; falls.

**What helps.** Take a first-aid and bleeding-control class. You may be able to save a life by acting right after an injury, before help arrives.[28] If a teen or adult collapses and their heart stops, call 911 and push hard and fast in the center of the chest. This hands-only CPR needs no training. Note where the nearest heart defibrillator (AED) is; it talks you through each step.[29] Keep a stocked first-aid kit. Check it regularly and replace anything used or out of date.[30] Keep a list of each person's medicines, doses and allergies with your emergency supplies.[31] Know how to reach 911. Call if you can, and text where that service works.[32]

**What to avoid.** Do not start antibiotics on your own or take someone else's. The wrong medicine can delay the right treatment and cause serious side effects.[33] Do not use expired medicines.[34]

**How bad:** Serious (based on data).

#### 4. Burst pipe or water leak

Of 100 households like yours, about 14 will have a burst pipe or leak flood part of the home in the next ten years.[35]

**Includes:** sewer backup.

**What helps.** Find your main water shut-off valve now, and show everyone how to turn it off. A burst pipe can flood a room quickly.[36, 37] Once a year, check the hoses to the water heater, washing machine, dishwasher and fridge, and replace any that crack or leak. A leak alarm can warn you early.[36] In freezing weather, let a faucet drip at night, open the cabinet doors under sinks on outside walls, and keep the heat at 55°F or higher.[37] Home and renters policies usually cover burst pipes. Many do not cover a sewer backup unless you add that cover, and floods need their own policy.[36]

**What to avoid.** Never thaw a frozen pipe with an open flame; use a hair dryer. Watch for electric shock around standing water.[37] Do not leave home while the washing machine or dishwasher is running.[36]

**How bad:** Severe (mostly data).

#### 5. Flooding from rivers or heavy rain

Of 100 households like yours, about 4 (1–9) will have flood water reach their home in the next ten years.[9, 10]

**Includes:** flash flood; basement or street flooding in heavy rain.

**What helps.** Look up your address on FEMA's flood maps, and sign up for your community's warnings.[38] Homeowner's insurance does not cover flooding, and a flood policy typically takes up to 30 days to start.[38] Renters can buy flood coverage for their belongings.[39] Know how you would get to higher ground or a higher floor.[38]

**What to avoid.** Six inches of moving water can knock you down, and one foot can sweep your car away.[38] Never drive around barriers on a flooded road. The road may have collapsed under the water.[40] Stay off bridges over fast-moving water.[38] Leave if you are told to.[41]

**How bad:** Very severe (rough data).

#### 6. Earthquake

Of 100 households like yours, about 4 will feel an earthquake strong enough to knock things off shelves in the next ten years.[42]

**Includes:** liquefaction; fire after an earthquake; named earthquake scenarios.

**What helps.** Practice Drop, Cover and Hold On: drop to your hands and knees, cover your head and neck, and hold on until the shaking stops.[16] Secure bookcases, water heaters, TVs and other heavy things, and keep heavy objects on low shelves. A standard homeowner's policy does not cover earthquake damage.[16] Store water in case the taps stop.

**What to avoid.** Do not run outside during shaking, and stay away from doorways.[16]

**How bad:** Severe (mostly data).

#### 7. House fire

Of 100 households like yours, about 3 will have a house fire in the next ten years.[13]

**Includes:** carbon monoxide poisoning; lithium battery fire.

**What helps.** Put smoke alarms inside and outside every bedroom and on every level, and test them each month.[43] Practice your escape plan twice a year, including finding your way out in the dark.[44] Sleep with bedroom doors closed. Teach children not to hide from firefighters.[44] If there is smoke, crawl low under it to your exit.[44] If you cannot get out, close the door, cover vents and cracks with cloth or tape, call 911, and signal from the window with a light-colored cloth or a flashlight.[44] Insurance and copies of your documents help you recover afterward.[45]

**What to avoid.** Before you open a door, feel the door and the knob. If either is hot, or smoke is coming around it, keep it closed and use your second way out.[44] If a person or pet is still inside, get out and tell firefighters right away.[44] If your clothes catch fire, stop, drop and roll.[44]

**How bad:** Severe (based on data).

#### 8. Dam or levee failure

Between 1 in 230 and 1 in 12 households like yours would be told to leave because a dam or levee fails or threatens to in the next ten years.[4, 10, 11, 12]

**Includes:** levee failure or overtopping; mine tailings or coal slurry dam failure.

**What helps.** Ask your county emergency management office whether a high-hazard dam lies upstream, and whether it has an emergency action plan.[46] Ask your local government whether you live near a levee; the national levee database maps every known levee.[12, 47] Know your evacuation route and how you would be warned.[46] Behind a levee, FEMA urges flood insurance even in a moderate-risk zone, because a levee can be overtopped or fail.[48]

**What to avoid.** If a dam or levee is failing, or a flash flood is possible, get to higher ground right away.[46] It is never safe to walk or drive into floodwater.[40]

**How bad:** Severe (rough data).

### No grid power at home: about 3 days (up to 5 days)

**What helps.** A light for each person, a radio and phone power are in your plan; keeping food cold and charging up are on the forecast list; registries are under Local help.

**What to avoid.** Never run a generator inside your home or garage, even with the doors and windows open. Keep it outside, more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents.[89] Put a carbon monoxide alarm on every level of your home.[90] Headache, dizziness, nausea or confusion can mean carbon monoxide poisoning. Get everyone outside to fresh air, then call 911.[91] Never heat your home with a gas stove or oven. Unplug electronics, because the power can come back with a surge that damages them.[90] Treat every downed power line as live: stay at least 30 feet from it and anything it touches, and call 911.[92] If one falls on your car, stay inside unless the car is on fire.[93]

The worst power cut in your region's records (where it was worst, about 85 miles (137 km) away) was November 2018 Camp Fire; being ready for 3 days would have left some homes that lost power still waiting (about 25 in 100 of all customers there were still out).[4, 8, 10, 11, 12, 22, 42, 50, 51, 94, 95, 96]

### Tap water must be treated: about 3 days (0–10)

**What helps.** Use bottled water, or bring clear water to a rolling boil for one minute. Boil for three minutes above 5,000 feet, then let it cool.[97] If you cannot boil water, add plain unscented bleach: 8 drops of 6% bleach or 6 drops of 8.25% bleach per gallon. Stir, and wait 30 minutes.[97] Use boiled or bottled water for drinking, cooking, brushing teeth, baby formula and ice. If the power is out too, a camp stove works, but only outdoors.[89]

**What to avoid.** Do not use water or ice from the fridge dispenser while a notice is on. Boil tap water even if it went through a home filter or a filter pitcher.[98] Boiling does not help if the notice warns of chemicals. That is a do-not-drink notice, and you need stored or bottled water instead.[98, 99]

### No tap water at all: about 3 days (2–7)

**What helps.** Store at least one gallon per person per day, for drinking and keeping clean.[1] Fill clean bottles or jugs with tap water and replace it every six months.[100] You can pick a level in the plan. About 3 liters a day covers drinking to survive, one gallon is the basic default, and about 4 gallons (15 liters) is the humanitarian minimum for drinking, cooking and washing.[101] A tank water heater holds 20 to 80 gallons of clean water.[102, 103] For long outages, a filter and a source of raw water go further than more jugs.[88] Filters do not remove viruses, so add a disinfectant such as chlorine dioxide after filtering.[99] A two-bucket toilet keeps waste safe when toilets cannot flush.[104]

**What to avoid.** Do not store water in containers that held chemicals.[100] Milk jugs seal poorly and turn brittle.[105] Never drink pool or spa water. Use it only for washing and cleaning.[103] After a flood, do not drink from a private well until it has been disinfected and tested. Your health department can tell you how.[106]

Water-system failures are counted about half as often as the national average here (an expert estimate), because about 15 of 100 public-water customers in your county are served by a system with a health-based violation in the last five years (EPA) and you said your water system has had no problems you remember.[4, 5, 6, 9, 10, 11, 12, 22, 35, 36, 42, 50, 51, 94, 107, 108]

## Your shelter plan

Pick a spot for each danger below, at home and at work, and practice going there.[14]

**Strong wind.** In a high wind or severe thunderstorm warning, go inside a sturdy building, to an inside room or basement.[123] Stay away from windows while damaging wind is near.[124]

**Earthquake.** Drop where you are, cover your head and neck, and hold on. Crawl under a sturdy table, or next to an inside wall away from windows. Do not run outside.[16]

**Chemical release.** If told to stay inside, go to an above-ground room with few openings. Close the windows, doors and vents, turn off fans and air conditioning, and seal the room with plastic sheeting and duct tape.[125]

**Smoke.** Make one room a clean room: close its windows and doors, and run an air cleaner that does not make ozone.[20]

**Radiation.** If officials warn of fallout, get inside, stay inside, stay tuned. Go to a basement or the middle of a brick or concrete building.[83]

**Before any storm**

- Charge phones, power banks and other batteries.[126] 
- Fill the car's tank and get some cash, since card readers and cash machines need power.[126] If you would leave in an electric car, charge it fully.[127]
- Refill prescriptions if you can.[126]
- Fill the bathtub and other large containers with water for flushing and cleaning.[126]
- Turn the fridge to its coldest setting and keep the doors shut.[126] A full freezer keeps food cold for about 48 hours, a fridge for about four.[90]
- Bring in or tie down outdoor furniture and grills.[3]
- Check your evacuation zone and your plan to leave.[3]
- Check with neighbors, older adults and anyone who may need help.[3]

**Before a hard freeze.** Let hot and cold water drip from a faucet at night, open the cabinet doors under sinks on outside walls, and keep the heat at 55°F or higher. Make sure everyone knows how to shut off the water in case a pipe bursts.[37]

**Before a heat wave.** Pick a place to cool down, such as a library, or ask your local health department about cooling options. Plan to check on family, older adults and neighbors. Fans do not lower body temperature.[24]

## Documents and money

Keep paper copies in a waterproof pouch and photos you can reach from any phone. FEMA's Emergency Financial First Aid Kit groups them in four parts.[45]

- [ ] **Money and legal papers:** insurance policies, the lease or deed, a will and powers of attorney, bank and card contact numbers (not PINs), and recent tax returns.
- [ ] **Medical papers:** insurance cards, the written medicine list, prescriptions, and vaccination records.
- [ ] **Contacts:** family, doctors, the insurance agent, the landlord or lender, and employers.

### Decisions

- [ ] **Decide: ID for every person (passport book or card, or a state ID).** A passport book or card for each person, children included, proves who you are and your citizenship if other papers are lost, and the card shows no home address; get them before you need them. The federal fee schedule: a first adult book $165 and card $65 ($130 and $30 plus a $35 acceptance fee), for children under 16 a book $135 and card $50 (22 CFR 22.1).[45, 139, 140]
- [ ] **Decide: flood insurance, even outside a flood zone.** Home and renters policies usually don't cover floods. Decide on flood insurance before you need it: Most homes in your county are outside the mapped high-risk flood zone, and that is where many uninsured floods happen: about 3 in 10 flood insurance claims come from outside high-risk flood areas. A quote costs nothing, and a new policy usually starts 30 days after you buy it. It pays up to $250,000 for the building and $100,000 for belongings. (FEMA, FloodSmart, Ready.gov)[9, 39, 141, 142, 143]
- [ ] **Decide: earthquake insurance.** A standard home policy does not cover earthquake damage. Decide whether earthquake insurance is worth its deductible where you live. (Ready.gov)[16]
- [ ] **Decide: disability and life insurance for the people who earn.** Someone in your household earns money the others rely on: check whether your job offers disability insurance, and whether life insurance would carry the others through the years they would need. About 1 in 4 workers who start at 20 become disabled before retirement age. NAIC's buyer's guide helps you work out how much cover is enough. (SSA, NAIC)[61, 144]
- [ ] **Home repairs to weigh when the time comes:** a stronger roof when you re-roof (FORTIFIED); bolt the house to its foundation (earthquake retrofit); harden the home against embers and wildfire. Some come with grants or insurance discounts: ask your state emergency management office and your insurer.[3, 145, 146, 147, 148, 149, 150]

## After a disaster: the first 30 days

**Your county.** Sacramento County, California had 2 federal major-disaster declarations in the last five full years.[152]

Recovery takes weeks. These steps come in rough order.

**Going home.** Go back only when local officials say it is safe. Do not go inside if you smell gas or the building looks damaged.[153] Run a generator only outside, more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents.[89]

**Insurance and records.** Before you clean up, take photos and videos and make a list of the damage.[153] Keep every receipt.[154] Call your insurer as soon as you can, and an adjuster will come to inspect. Check the adjuster's estimate, and talk to them before you sign a repair contract.[155]

**Help from FEMA.** If a federal disaster declaration covers your county, apply at DisasterAssistance.gov or call 1-800-621-3362.[156] FEMA help does not replace insurance.[157]

**Scams.** FEMA never charges to apply or to inspect.[158] Its inspectors carry an official photo ID and never ask for your bank details; a FEMA shirt is not an ID.[154] Never sign your insurance check over to a contractor, and walk away from anyone who wants cash up front.[159]

**Medicine.** In a declared disaster, Medicare drug plans can help you find a pharmacy and replace lost medicine.[160] Many states let a pharmacist give an emergency supply when your prescriber cannot be reached, and the rules differ by state.[138] Healthcare Ready's Rx Open map shows which pharmacies are open in a disaster area.[161]

**Papers.** To replace lost IDs, start with your birth certificate, from the vital records office of the state where you were born.[162]

## Special needs

### Medicine

- 1 person takes prescription medicine every day: keep 21 days of it on hand. Keep a written list of each medicine, what it is for and the dose. After a disaster declaration some states let pharmacies give an emergency refill of up to 30 days, and many allow only a few days; ask your pharmacist what yours allows. ### Antibiotics

- No antibiotics are included in this plan. If you have a specific, foreseeable need, such as remote travel, talk to your own clinician about a standby prescription with written instructions. Never use fish or pet antibiotics, never share prescriptions, and don't use expired ones. (CDC, FDA)[33, 34, 171, 172]

### Older adults

- **Plan for older adults in the household.** Plan transport if help is needed to evacuate, copy Medicare and Medicaid cards, give someone in your support network a key and show them your equipment, and ask any clinic you rely on about its emergency plans (Ready.gov). Heat and cold are harder on people over 65, so set up daily check-ins in extreme weather.[23, 113, 173]

### Pets and animals

- Dry food for the cat for 14 days (at least a week): about 0.15 lb a day for each cat = 2.1 lb. Feed what the label says for yours, and keep it in an airtight, waterproof container. (ASPCA, Ready.gov; some amounts are estimates)[10, 17, 18, 22, 59, 60, 128, 174, 175, 176, 177]
- 1 pet carrier, one for each pet, with a pet go-kit: water and food from your home supplies, any medicine it takes, records and a photo. Public shelters may take only service animals, so find pet-friendly places to stay ahead of time. (ASPCA, Ready.gov)[2, 174]

### Stress and mental health

#### Stress, mental health and the 988 line

**What helps.** Save 988 in every phone. It offers free, judgment-free support 24/7 for mental health, substance use and more, by call, text or chat.[178] After a disaster, the Disaster Distress Helpline offers crisis counseling 24/7 to anyone in emotional distress. Call or text 1-800-985-5990.[179] Write a short calm plan: what you might feel, what helps you, and who you will call.[180] Oregon's 2 Weeks Ready program even includes practicing mindfulness as a step in getting prepared.[168]

**What to avoid.** Do not keep the news on all day. No one, adult or child, benefits from graphic details or disturbing images.[181] Reach out before things feel unbearable. The 988 line supports people in emotional distress as well as in crisis.[178]
