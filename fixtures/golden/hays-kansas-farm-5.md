### Staying in touch

**Four ways to reach each other.** Emergency planners call this a PACE plan: a primary way, an alternate, a contingency and an emergency way. Each one takes over when the one before it fails.[12] For a household it can be:

1. Primary: call.
2. Alternate: text. A text may get through when a call does not, and it saves battery.[13]
3. Contingency: everyone texts one out-of-state contact, who passes the news along.[13]
4. Emergency: go to the place you agreed to meet if you get separated.[13]

**School and child care.** Every place your child spends time should have an emergency plan. Keep your contact details, the list of adults allowed to pick up your child, custody papers and medical needs up to date there. After an emergency, follow official directions about where to reunite.[14]

## Your risks

What could reach a household like yours in Ellis County, Kansas over the next 10 years: the likeliest events, and the rarer ones that can kill, most likely first.

### What to know and do

#### 1. Medical emergency

Nearly every household like yours will have someone need emergency care in the next ten years (about 2.4 times a year).[15, 16]

**Includes:** far from emergency care; falls; going into labour during a disaster.

**What helps.** Take a first-aid and bleeding-control class. You may be able to save a life by acting right after an injury, before help arrives.[17] If a teen or adult collapses and their heart stops, call 911 and push hard and fast in the center of the chest. This hands-only CPR needs no training. Note where the nearest heart defibrillator (AED) is; it talks you through each step.[18] Keep a stocked first-aid kit. Check it regularly and replace anything used or out of date.[19] Keep a list of each person's medicines, doses and allergies with your emergency supplies.[20] Know how to reach 911. Call if you can, and text where that service works.[21]

**What to avoid.** Do not start antibiotics on your own or take someone else's. The wrong medicine can delay the right treatment and cause serious side effects.[22] Do not use expired medicines.[23]

**How bad:** Serious (based on data).

#### 2. Heat wave

Nearly every household like yours will go through a heat wave in the next ten years (about 1.6 times a year).[6, 8]

**Includes:** a blackout during a heat wave.

**What helps.** Know where you can go to cool off. Dial 2-1-1 to find air conditioning near you.[24] Cover windows with drapes or shades, and weather-strip doors and windows.[25] Take cool showers, wear loose, light-colored clothing, and drink plenty of fluids. Check on family members, older adults and neighbours.[25] Heat can cause power outages, so plan for refrigerated medicines and powered medical devices.[24] In a drought, follow local limits on water use, and make saving water a daily habit.[26]

**What to avoid.** Do not rely on a fan when it is over 90°F indoors. It can make you hotter.[24] Never leave people or pets in a closed car on a warm day.[25] Heat stroke is an emergency. Signs include a body temperature of 103°F or more, hot, red skin that may be dry or damp, a fast, strong pulse, confusion or passing out. Call 911, move the person somewhere cooler, cool them with cool cloths or a cool bath, and give them nothing to drink.[27]

**How bad:** Serious (mostly data).

#### 3. Burst pipe or water leak

Of 100 households like yours, about 21 (14–30) will have a burst pipe or leak flood part of the home in the next ten years.[8, 28, 29]

**Includes:** sewer backup.

**What helps.** Find your main water shut-off valve now, and show everyone how to turn it off. A burst pipe can flood a room quickly.[30, 31] Once a year, check the hoses to the water heater, washing machine, dishwasher and fridge, and replace any that crack or leak. A leak alarm can warn you early.[30] In freezing weather, let a faucet drip at night, open the cabinet doors under sinks on outside walls, and keep the heat at 55°F or higher.[31] Home and renters policies usually cover burst pipes. Many do not cover a sewer backup unless you add that cover, and floods need their own policy.[30]

**What to avoid.** Never thaw a frozen pipe with an open flame; use a hair dryer. Watch for electric shock around standing water.[31] Do not leave home while the washing machine or dishwasher is running.[30]

**How bad:** Severe (mostly data).

#### 4. Hail

Of 100 households like yours, about 8 (3–16) will have hail damage their home or car in the next ten years.[6, 8]

**What helps.** Pick your shelter spot now. A safe room or storm shelter is best.[32] The next best is a small, windowless room or basement on the lowest floor of a sturdy building.[32, 33] Sign up for your community's warnings, and learn what your sirens sound like.[32] Know the signs of a tornado: a rotating funnel cloud, a cloud of debris, or a roar like a freight train. In your shelter, protect your head and neck with your arms, and pile blankets or cushions around you.[32] When thunder roars, go indoors. Get into a building or a car with a roof.[34]

**What to avoid.** Stay away from windows, doors and outside walls.[32] There is no safe place in a car during a tornado, only less dangerous ones. If you are caught, stay buckled in and keep your head below the windows.[32] During a thunderstorm, avoid running water and electronics plugged into the wall.[34]

**How bad:** Severe (mostly data).

#### 5. Flooding from rivers or heavy rain

Of 100 households like yours, about 4 (1–11) will have flood water reach their home in the next ten years.[7, 8]

**Includes:** flash flood; basement or street flooding in heavy rain.

**What helps.** Look up your address on FEMA's flood maps, and sign up for your community's warnings.[35] Homeowner's insurance does not cover flooding, and a flood policy typically takes up to 30 days to start.[35] Renters can buy flood coverage for their belongings.[36] Know how you would get to higher ground or a higher floor.[35]

**What to avoid.** Six inches of moving water can knock you down, and one foot can sweep your car away.[35] Never drive around barriers on a flooded road. The road may have collapsed under the water.[37] Stay off bridges over fast-moving water.[35] Leave if you are told to.[38]

**How bad:** Very severe (rough data).

#### 6. Tornado

Of 100 households like yours, about 3 (1–10) will have a tornado damage or cut off their neighborhood in the next ten years.[6, 8]

**What helps.** The steps under "Hail" above apply here too.

**How bad:** Severe (rough data).

#### 7. House fire

Of 100 households like yours, about 3 will have a house fire in the next ten years.[11]

**Includes:** carbon monoxide poisoning; lithium battery fire.

**What helps.** Put smoke alarms inside and outside every bedroom and on every level, and test them each month.[39] Practice your escape plan twice a year, including finding your way out in the dark.[40] Sleep with bedroom doors closed. Teach children not to hide from firefighters.[40] If there is smoke, crawl low under it to your exit.[40] If you cannot get out, close the door, cover vents and cracks with cloth or tape, call 911, and signal from the window with a light-colored cloth or a flashlight.[40] Insurance and copies of your documents help you recover afterward.[41]

**What to avoid.** Before you open a door, feel the door and the knob. If either is hot, or smoke is coming around it, keep it closed and use your second way out.[40] If a person or pet is still inside, get out and tell firefighters right away.[40] If your clothes catch fire, stop, drop and roll.[40]

**How bad:** Severe (based on data).

#### 8. Wildfire

Of 100 households like yours, about 3 (1–7) will have to leave home or lose power because of a wildfire in the next ten years.[6, 8]

**What helps.** Keep a zone at least 30 feet around your home clear of leaves, debris and anything that burns.[42] Know your evacuation zone and routes, and practice with your pets.[42] Store N95 masks for smoke. Pick a room you can close off from outside air, and run a portable air cleaner there.[42] N95 masks are not made to fit children, so for them the clean room is the protection.[43] A box fan with a MERV 13 filter makes a low-cost air cleaner.[44] If your heating and cooling system brings in fresh air, set it to recirculate.[42] Keep phones charged and have backup chargers.[42]

**What to avoid.** Leave as soon as authorities tell you to, even if you cannot see flames.[42] Do not use an air cleaner that makes ozone.[45] Avoid hard exercise when it is smoky, so you breathe in less smoke.[45]

**How bad:** Serious (rough data).

### Notes on these numbers

- These chances are for Ellis County as a whole. A home near a river, the coast or a steep slope can face more; the county's average is what the plan uses.
- Power-outage records (2015-2025) show homes in Ellis County caught in an outage about once every 2 years. Matched by date to the storms behind them, some outages come from winter storms more often than the county's storm records explain, so the difference is counted under each cause. Outages from other causes, or matched to no storm, are counted in the power-cut estimates, not under a storm.
- Medicine shortages are left out: no one takes a daily prescription.
- Ambulances take longer to reach rural homes, so first-aid skills and supplies count for more here.
- Heat waves are marked Serious for this household because someone in it is pregnant or nursing; they are most dangerous for households like yours.
- Wildfire smoke is marked Serious for this household because there is a child; smoke harms them most.

### No grid power at home: about 3 days (up to 7 days)

**What helps.** A light for each person, a radio and phone power are in your plan; keeping food cold and charging up are on the forecast list; registries are under Local help.

**What to avoid.** Never run a generator inside your home or garage, even with the doors and windows open. Keep it outside, more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents.[89] Put a carbon monoxide alarm on every level of your home.[90] Headache, dizziness, nausea or confusion can mean carbon monoxide poisoning. Get everyone outside to fresh air, then call 911.[91] Never heat your home with a gas stove or oven. Unplug electronics, because the power can come back with a surge that damages them.[90] Treat every downed power line as live: stay at least 30 feet from it and anything it touches, and call 911.[92] If one falls on your car, stay inside unless the car is on fire.[93]

The worst power cut in your region's records (where it was worst, about 99 miles (159 km) away) was Winter storm, April 2017; being ready for 3 days would have outlasted it for at least 9 in 10 of the homes that lost power.[6, 8, 46, 53, 54, 94, 95, 96]

### No tap water at all: about 2 months (3 weeks to 6 months)

**What helps.** Store at least one gallon per person per day, for drinking and keeping clean.[1] Fill clean bottles or jugs with tap water and replace it every six months.[97] You can pick a level in the plan. About 3 liters a day covers drinking to survive, one gallon is the basic default, and about 4 gallons (15 liters) is the humanitarian minimum for drinking, cooking and washing.[98] A tank water heater holds 20 to 80 gallons of clean water.[99, 100] For long outages, a filter and a source of raw water go further than more jugs.[87] Filters do not remove viruses, so add a disinfectant such as chlorine dioxide after filtering.[101] A two-bucket toilet keeps waste safe when toilets cannot flush.[102]

**What to avoid.** Do not store water in containers that held chemicals.[97] Milk jugs seal poorly and turn brittle.[103] Never drink pool or spa water. Use it only for washing and cleaning.[100] After a flood, do not drink from a private well until it has been disinfected and tested. Your health department can tell you how.[104]

This rounds up to 2 months: before rounding it is about 7 weeks, just past 6 weeks.[6, 8]

**What to avoid.** Do not buy everything at once. It strains your budget and the store. Do not buy or eat from cans that bulge, leak, are rusted or are deeply dented.[105] Do not trust a kit's "days" label. Check the calories on the package instead.

## Your shelter plan

Pick a spot for each danger below, at home and at work, and practice going there.[2]

**Strong wind.** In a high wind or severe thunderstorm warning, go inside a sturdy building, to an inside room or basement.[122] Stay away from windows while damaging wind is near.[123]

**Tornado.** A safe room or storm shelter is best; next is a small inside room with no windows on the lowest floor.[32] Even a tied-down mobile home is not safe.[33]

**Chemical release.** If told to stay inside, go to an above-ground room with few openings. Close the windows, doors and vents, turn off fans and air conditioning, and seal the room with plastic sheeting and duct tape.[124]

**Smoke.** Make one room a clean room: close its windows and doors, and run an air cleaner that does not make ozone.[116]

**Radiation.** If officials warn of fallout, get inside, stay inside, stay tuned. Go to a basement or the middle of a brick or concrete building.[82]

## When a storm, freeze or heat wave is forecast

Hurricanes, winter storms and heat waves usually come with warning. Keep your phone charged when one is forecast, and use the time the same way each time.[125]

**Before any storm**

- Charge phones, power banks and other batteries.[126] 
- Fill the car's tank and get some cash, since card readers and cash machines need power.[126] If you would leave in an electric car, charge it fully.[127]
- Refill prescriptions if you can.[126]
- Fill the bathtub and other large containers with water for flushing and cleaning.[126]
- Turn the fridge to its coldest setting and keep the doors shut.[126] A full freezer keeps food cold for about 48 hours, a fridge for about four.[90]
- Bring in or tie down outdoor furniture and grills.[125]
- Check your evacuation zone and your plan to leave.[125]
- Check with neighbors, older adults and anyone who may need help.[125]

**Before a hard freeze.** Let hot and cold water drip from a faucet at night, open the cabinet doors under sinks on outside walls, and keep the heat at 55°F or higher. Make sure everyone knows how to shut off the water in case a pipe bursts.[31]

**Before a heat wave.** Pick a place to cool down, such as a library, or ask your local health department about cooling options. Plan to check on family, older adults and neighbors. Fans do not lower body temperature.[25]

## After a disaster: the first 30 days

**Your county.** Ellis County, Kansas had 3 federal major-disaster declarations in the last five full years.[152]

Recovery takes weeks. These steps come in rough order.

**Going home.** Go back only when local officials say it is safe. Do not go inside if you smell gas or the building looks damaged.[153] Run a generator only outside, more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents.[89]

**Insurance and records.** Before you clean up, take photos and videos and make a list of the damage.[153] Keep every receipt.[154] Call your insurer as soon as you can, and an adjuster will come to inspect. Check the adjuster's estimate, and talk to them before you sign a repair contract.[155]

**Help from FEMA.** If a federal disaster declaration covers your county, apply at DisasterAssistance.gov or call 1-800-621-3362.[156] FEMA help does not replace insurance.[157]

**Scams.** FEMA never charges to apply or to inspect.[158] Its inspectors carry an official photo ID and never ask for your bank details; a FEMA shirt is not an ID.[154] Never sign your insurance check over to a contractor, and walk away from anyone who wants cash up front.[159]

**Medicine.** In a declared disaster, Medicare drug plans can help you find a pharmacy and replace lost medicine.[160] Many states let a pharmacist give an emergency supply when your prescriber cannot be reached, and the rules differ by state.[138] Healthcare Ready's Rx Open map shows which pharmacies are open in a disaster area.[161]

**Papers.** To replace lost IDs, start with your birth certificate, from the vital records office of the state where you were born.[162]

## If it lasts for months

- [ ] Going further: food for months (month 1)
- [ ] Household kit: paper plates, cups, utensils, trash bags, waterproof matches: 3 kits (month 15)
- [ ] Water carriers for hauling, about 5 gallons each: 4 carriers (month 39)

**How likely here.** From your own power curve, power out for two months or more: between 1 in 31,000 and 1 in 1,900 households like yours in the next 10 years; for three months or more: between 1 in 130,000 and 1 in 8,000.[6, 8, 46, 53, 54, 94, 95, 96]

These pointers are for the rare event that lasts for months. They are places to start, not a manual.

**Rain.** Each state sets its own rules on collecting rain, and the rules vary widely, so check yours.[163] Rainwater is not safe to drink until germs and chemicals are removed, but it can water plants you do not eat.[164]

**Wells.** A well pump stops when the power goes out. Have a licensed electrician connect any generator.[165] A hand pump fitted to the well can still draw water in a long outage; test and treat that water before drinking it.[166]

**Toilets.** After a strong earthquake, toilets may not flush for weeks or months. A twin-bucket toilet keeps pee and poo apart, and handling poo safely stops diseases like cholera.[102]

**Food.** Badly home-canned food can cause botulism. Vegetables, meat and fish need a pressure canner.[167] Use tested recipes, such as those from the National Center for Home Food Preservation.[168] Your state or county Extension service can help with growing and preserving food.[167]

**Spirits.** Keep regular sleep and wake times, take breaks from the news, and talk with people you trust.[169] Keep books, games and puzzles for children.[131]

### Stress, mental health and the 988 line

**What helps.** Save 988 in every phone. It offers free, judgment-free support 24/7 for mental health, substance use and more, by call, text or chat.[184] After a disaster, the Disaster Distress Helpline offers crisis counseling 24/7 to anyone in emotional distress. Call or text 1-800-985-5990.[185] Write a short calm plan: what you might feel, what helps you, and who you will call.[186] Oregon's 2 Weeks Ready program even includes practicing mindfulness as a step in getting prepared.[187]

**What to avoid.** Do not keep the news on all day. No one, adult or child, benefits from graphic details or disturbing images.[188] Reach out before things feel unbearable. The 988 line supports people in emotional distress as well as in crisis.[184]
