## Your risks

These are the events most likely to reach a household like yours in Ellis County, Kansas, over the next 10 years. Common events come first: a lost job, a house fire or a week-long outage is far more likely than a disaster from the movies, and the plan is ordered the same way.

### The ones most likely to reach you

#### 1. Medical emergency

Nearly every household like yours will have someone need emergency care in the next ten years (about 2.4 times a year).[2, 3] Injuries and sudden illness are common. In 2024, accidental injuries caused about 58 deaths for every 100,000 people.[4] Emergency departments see about 47 visits for every 100 people each year.[2] In rural areas, ambulances take longer to arrive.[3]

**What helps.** Take a first-aid and bleeding-control class. You may be able to save a life by acting right after an injury, before help arrives.[5] Keep a stocked first-aid kit. Check it regularly and replace anything used or out of date.[6] Keep a list of each person's medicines, doses and allergies with your emergency supplies.[7] Know how to reach 911. Call if you can, and text where that service works.[8] Set up telehealth with your own clinician before you need it.[9]

**What to avoid.** Do not start antibiotics on your own or take someone else's. The wrong medicine can delay the right treatment and cause serious side effects.[10] Do not use expired medicines.[11]

- **What it can do to you:** a medical emergency.
- **How bad one can be:** Serious. **How sure we are:** Based on data.

#### 2. Heat wave

Nearly every household like yours will go through a heat wave in the next ten years (about 1.6 times a year).[12, 13] Heat is most dangerous for older adults, babies and young children, pregnant women, outdoor workers, and people with heart disease or asthma.[14] Heat and a power cut together are far worse than either alone. One study estimated that a five-day blackout during a heat wave would send about half of Phoenix's residents to emergency care.[15]

**What helps.** Know where you can go to cool off. Dial 2-1-1 to find air conditioning near you.[14] Cover windows with drapes or shades, and weather-strip doors and windows.[16] Take cool showers, wear loose, light-colored clothing, and drink plenty of fluids. Check on family members, older adults and neighbours.[16] Heat can cause power outages, so plan for refrigerated medicines and powered medical devices.[14] In a drought, follow local limits on water use, and make saving water a daily habit.[17]

**What to avoid.** Do not rely on a fan when it is over 90°F indoors. It can make you hotter.[14] Never leave people or pets in a closed car on a warm day. Heat stroke is an emergency. Signs include a body temperature above 103°F, hot, red, dry skin, a fast pulse and confusion. Call 911.[16]

- **What it can do to you:** no power and dangerous heat or cold at home.
- **How bad one can be:** Minor. **How sure we are:** Mostly data.

#### 3. Strong wind

Nearly every household like yours will lose power or have damage in a windstorm in the next ten years.[13, 18] Severe weather can happen anytime, in any part of the country. Thunderstorms bring damaging winds, tornadoes, large hail, flash floods and lightning.[19] Tornado winds can top 200 miles an hour.[20]

**What helps.** Pick your shelter spot now. A safe room or storm shelter is best. The next best is a small, windowless room or basement on the lowest floor of a sturdy building.[20] Sign up for your community's warnings, and learn what your sirens sound like.[20] Know the signs of a tornado: a rotating funnel cloud, a cloud of debris, or a roar like a freight train. In your shelter, protect your head and neck with your arms, and pile blankets or cushions around you.[20] When thunder roars, go indoors. Get into a building or a car with a roof.[21]

**What to avoid.** Stay away from windows, doors and outside walls.[20] There is no safe place in a car during a tornado, only less dangerous ones. If you are caught, stay buckled in and keep your head below the windows.[20] During a thunderstorm, avoid running water and electronics plugged into the wall.[21]

- **What it can do to you:** no power, no phone, internet or card payments and damage to the home.
- **How bad one can be:** Moderate. **How sure we are:** Rough data.

#### 4. Stranded in a vehicle

Of 100 households like yours, about 95 (63 to nearly all) will be stranded away from home by a crash, breakdown or shutdown in the next ten years.[13, 22] Police reported about 6.1 million crashes in 2023.[22] Breakdowns, closed roads and storms can also leave you stuck for hours.

**What helps.** Keep a car kit with jumper cables, sand, a flashlight, warm clothes, blankets, water and snacks.[23] Keep the gas tank at least half full.[24] Check the pressure in all your tires, including the spare, at least once a month.[25] Before a winter trip, tell someone where you are going and when you expect to arrive, and ask them to call for help if you are late.[26] If you drive an electric car, know its range and where you can charge along your route.[27, 28]

**What to avoid.** If you are stuck in a snowstorm, staying in the car is often safest. Tie a bright cloth to the antenna. Run the engine about 10 minutes an hour with a window open a little, and keep the exhaust pipe clear of snow.[26] Never drive into a flooded road or around the barriers.[29]

- **What it can do to you:** being stranded away from home.
- **How bad one can be:** Minor. **How sure we are:** Mostly data.

#### 5. Job loss

Of 100 households like yours, about 95 (87–99) will have someone lose a job in the next ten years.[13, 30, 31] Losing income is one of the most likely disruptions a household faces. In 2024, about 8 of every 100 people who worked or looked for work were unemployed at some point.[30] Of long-time workers who lost a job in 2023 to 2025, about 2 in 3 were working again by January 2026.[32] A 20-year-old worker has a 1-in-4 chance of becoming disabled before retirement age.[33]

**What helps.** Build an emergency fund a little at a time. The right amount depends on your situation.[34] Planners often suggest three to six months of living expenses, and more if your income varies.[35] Know how to file for unemployment in your state.[36] Keep copies of your financial, legal and medical papers together, with the names of people to call.[37] Write a bare-bones budget you could switch to, and keep a pantry that can stretch a tight month.

**What to avoid.** Do not wait to file. Contact your state's unemployment program as soon as possible after losing work.[36] Do not keep your emergency fund in investments you would have to sell. A savings account lets you take money out at any time without a penalty.[35]

- **What it can do to you:** lost income.
- **How bad one can be:** Serious. **How sure we are:** Mostly data.

### Rare but severe

These are shown apart, with how likely and how bad as separate columns. A tiny chance times a huge loss would otherwise crowd out everything else, and the plan never lets them take over the budget.

| What | How likely | How bad |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nuclear attack | Experts' estimates of a worldwide nuclear catastrophe range from about 1 in 2,000 to about 1 in 400 a year. No reliable estimate exists for effects where you live. The first 24 hours of sheltering inside are covered by your basic supplies.[44, 45] | Very severe |
| Terrorist attack | An attack that disrupts daily life where you live is rare: expert estimates range from about 1 in 50,000 to about 1 in 5,000 a year.[13] | Very severe |

The first advice for a nuclear emergency is to get inside, stay inside and stay tuned.[45] Your three-day supplies already cover that first stretch of sheltering.

These events are very rare. In a 2024 survey, experts and forecasters put the chance of a nuclear catastrophe anywhere in the world before 2045 at 1 to 5 in 100.[44] The right response is simple, and your three-day supplies already cover it.

**What helps.** Get inside, stay inside, stay tuned.[45] Go to a basement or the middle of a large brick or concrete building. Stay there for 24 hours unless there is a fire or another immediate danger, or officials say it is safe to leave. Radiation levels drop fast during the first 24 hours.[45] Turn off fans and air conditioning that pull in outside air.[46] If you were outside, take off your outer layer of clothing. That can remove up to 90% of radioactive material. Then wash with soap and water.[46] If you live near a nuclear power plant, learn whether you are in its planning zone.[47]

**What to avoid.** Do not take potassium iodide unless public health or emergency officials tell you to. It protects only the thyroid.[48] Near a plant, states can give out tablets to people within 10 miles, on official instruction.[49] Do not spend on special gear before your basic supplies are in place.[50]

### No grid power at home: about 3 days (up to 7 days)

Of 100 households like yours, about 35 (25–75) will lose grid power for a day or more in the next 10 years.[12, 13, 15, 18, 51, 52, 53] Most outages last a few hours. In 2024 the average home lost power for about 11 hours in total, mostly because of hurricanes.[54] After recent hurricanes, getting power back to 95 of every 100 customers who lost it took from about 3 days to nearly 3 weeks.[55]

**What helps.** Have a flashlight for each person.[56] Keep a battery or hand-crank radio, and a backup battery for each phone.[57] Keep the fridge and freezer closed. A closed fridge keeps food cold for about four hours, and a full freezer for about two days.[56] If someone relies on a powered medical device or cold medicine, make a plan with their medical provider now.[56] You can also ask your power company to put you on its list for priority restoration.[7] A battery power station burns no fuel, so it makes no carbon monoxide.[58]

**What to avoid.** Never run a generator inside your home or garage, even with the doors and windows open. Keep it outside, more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents.[58] Put a carbon monoxide alarm on every level of your home.[56] Never heat your home with a gas stove or oven. Unplug electronics, because the power can come back with a surge that damages them.[56]

**Your numbers.** Being ready for 3 days leaves about 8 (6–25) of 100 households like yours facing a longer power cut in the next 10 years. This number mostly depends on how often windstorms happen where you live (expert estimate) and how long power cuts from a windstorm bigger than any in the recent records last (expert estimate).[12, 13, 15, 18, 51, 52, 53]

**What counts toward it:**

- 4 battery lights: a headlamp or lantern for each person aged 4 and over. Use lights, not candles. (Ready.gov; some amounts are estimates)[56, 57, 59]
- 1 pack of AA or AAA batteries for the lights and the radio: one pack lasts a household about 7 days. Check which sizes your lights use. (Ready.gov; some amounts are estimates)[12, 13, 15, 18, 51, 52, 53, 57, 59]

**Also worth knowing:**

- 1 battery power station (about 1,070 Wh each) as an optional upgrade for lights, phones and a radio. Your essential load (phones) is about 45 Wh a day, 140 Wh for 3 days; one station gives about 910 Wh after losses, enough for about 20.2 days of that load. A full-size fridge alone would drain one in about 0.9 days. (retail prices, ENERGY STAR; some amounts are estimates)[12, 13, 15, 18, 51, 52, 53, 59, 60, 61]
- Optional: a portable generator can run the fridge, lights and the well pump through a 3-day outage. It must run outdoors, at least 20 feet from windows, doors and vents, never in a garage, and it needs fuel (see the fuel line). (CDC, Ready.gov, Ready Reckoner research; some amounts are estimates)[12, 13, 15, 18, 51, 52, 53, 56, 58, 59, 62]
- Optional: keeping a fridge running takes about 995 Wh a day (a chest freezer about 597) × 3 days = 2,990 Wh. A closed fridge keeps food cold about 4 hours and a full freezer about 48; shelf-stable food avoids the need. (ENERGY STAR, Ready.gov)[12, 13, 15, 18, 51, 52, 53, 56, 61, 63]
- Optional: your well pump needs about 1,250 W to run (1,000 to 1,500) and 2 to 3 times that to start; check its nameplate. At 1 hour a day that is 3,750 Wh for 3 days. A battery power station usually cannot start it; a generator or a hand pump can. Stored water is the cheaper way to cover this. (an estimate)[12, 13, 15, 18, 51, 52, 53, 59]

### No tap water at all: about 2 months (3 weeks to 6 months)

Of 100 households like yours, about 75 (65–95) will have no usable tap water for a day or more in the next 10 years.[12, 13] Sometimes no water comes out of the tap, or officials say not to use it at all. Earthquakes, hard freezes, hurricanes and chemical spills can do this. After Hurricane Helene in 2024, Asheville, North Carolina, waited about seven weeks for safe tap water.[64] If your well has an electric pump, every power cut is also a water cut.[50]

**What helps.** Store at least one gallon per person per day, for drinking and keeping clean.[1] Fill clean bottles or jugs with tap water and replace it every six months.[65] You can pick a level in the plan. About 3 liters a day covers drinking to survive, one gallon is the basic default, and about 4 gallons (15 liters) is the humanitarian minimum for drinking, cooking and washing.[66] A tank water heater holds 20 to 80 gallons of clean water.[67, 68] For long outages, a filter and a source of raw water go further than more jugs.[50] Filters do not remove viruses, so add a disinfectant such as chlorine dioxide after filtering.[69] A two-bucket toilet keeps waste safe when toilets cannot flush.[70]

**What to avoid.** Do not store water in containers that held chemicals.[65] Milk jugs seal poorly and turn brittle.[71] Never drink pool or spa water. Use it only for washing and cleaning.[68]

**Your numbers.** Being ready for 2 months leaves about 8 (2–25) of 100 households like yours facing a longer water outage in the next 10 years. This number mostly depends on how often droughts happen where you live (expert estimate) and how long water outages from drought lowering a well last (expert estimate). Your well pump runs on electricity, so every power cut is also a water cut. The pump itself also fails now and then (about once in 14 years).[12, 13]

**What counts toward it:**

- 5 people × 1 gallon a day × 14 days = 70 gallons, plus 4.1 gallons for pregnancy or breastfeeding, plus 11.4 gallons for the 2 dogs and the 3 cats: about 85.4 gallons. Replace water you store yourself every 6 months. Your target is 60 days. Storing all of it would take about 366.2 gallons, so the plan stores the first 14 days and makes the rest safe with a filter and a water source (see the next line). (Ready.gov, CDC, National Academies, PetMD; some amounts are estimates)[1, 12, 13, 59, 65, 72, 73, 74, 75, 76, 77, 78]
- After day 14, for the other 46 days: make about 6.1 gallons of water safe each day, about 280.7 gallons in all, with a water filter and a source such as rain barrels, a stream or a pond, instead of storing more. Filter it, then boil it for 1 minute (3 minutes above 5,000 feet), or add 8 drops of 6 % unscented bleach per gallon (6 drops of 8.25 %) and wait 30 minutes; double the bleach for cloudy or very cold water. If there is no water source near you, store more water instead. (Ready.gov, CDC, National Academies, PetMD; some amounts are estimates)[1, 12, 13, 59, 65, 69, 72, 73, 74, 75, 76, 77, 78, 79]
- 1 bottle of plain unscented bleach (5 to 9 % sodium hypochlorite, not the splashless kind), for making water safe and for cleaning, however long your target is. At about 0.5 mL a gallon, each ounce treats about 60 gallons of clear water (30 if it is cloudy or very cold), so one bottle treats thousands of gallons. Bleach weakens in storage: buy a fresh bottle every 6 months, when you replace the water you store yourself. (CDC, EPA; some amounts are estimates)[59, 65, 69, 79, 80]
- 12 large animals × 25 L a day (20 to 30) × 60 days = 18,000 L, about 4,755.1 gallons. Fill tubs or stock tanks before a storm or an outage. (Sphere, ASPCA)[12, 13, 66, 81]
- A two-bucket toilet for when toilets can't flush: 2 sturdy buckets with a seat, one for pee and one for poo. (RDPO, Oregon OEM)[70, 77]
- Toilet bags: about 0.45 heavy garbage bags a person a day (0.3 to 0.6), counting both bags of a double-bagged load: 5 people × 60 days = 135 bags. Fill halfway, tie, double-bag, and store away from food, water, children and pets. Don't put them in curbside trash or bury them unless officials say so. (RDPO, Oregon OEM; some amounts are estimates)[12, 13, 59, 70, 77]
- Cover material for the poo bucket (sawdust, shredded paper, dry leaves or pet bedding): about 1 cup a person a day (0.5 to 1.5) × 5 people × 60 days = 300 cups, about 18.8 gallons. (RDPO; some amounts are estimates)[12, 13, 59, 70]

Label them and replace the water every 6 months. (Ready Reckoner research, CDC, Ready.gov, Church of Jesus Christ; some amounts are estimates)[1, 50, 59, 65, 71]

### Can't get to a store: about 10 days (7 days to 3 weeks)

Of 100 households like yours, about 50 (25–85) will be unable to shop for food and supplies for 3 days or more in the next 10 years.[13, 18, 39, 40, 82] Sometimes you cannot get to a store, or the shelves are empty. Winter storms, runs on stores before a storm, curfews and disease outbreaks can all do this. In spring 2020, statewide stay-at-home orders lasted about six weeks in the middle state.[50, 82]

**What helps.** Keep extra food that needs no refrigeration, cooking or water, and choose foods your family will eat.[63] Use the oldest cans first and replace them as you go.[83] Buy a little at a time. Ready.gov asks people to buy supplies slowly, so everyone can get what they need.[84] Keep a manual can opener.[63] The plan counts food in calories, not servings. Many "30-day" meal kits hold only about 1,300 to 1,700 calories a day.[62] That is less than most adults need.[85] Store food somewhere cool and dry. Below 85°F is best.[83]

**What to avoid.** Do not buy everything at once. It strains your budget and the store. Do not buy or eat from cans that bulge, leak, are rusted or are deeply dented.[83] Do not trust a kit's "days" label. Check the calories on the package instead.

**Your numbers.** Being ready for 10 days leaves about 9 (4–30) of 100 households like yours facing a longer stretch without shopping in the next 10 years. This number mostly depends on how long times without shopping from store shortages and runs on stores last (expert estimate) and how often pandemics happen where you live (expert estimate).[13, 18, 39, 40, 82]

### Dangerous heat or cold indoors: about 3 days (2–7)

Of 100 households like yours, about 25 (20–60) will face dangerous heat or cold at home with no working cooling or heating for a day or more in the next 10 years.[12, 13, 15, 18, 51, 52, 53] Heat and cold become dangerous indoors when the power fails or the heating or air conditioning stops. Some people need extra care: babies and young children, people over 65, pregnant women, and people with heart disease or asthma.[14] In the 1995 Chicago heat wave, people who lived alone or did not leave home each day were more likely to die. Working air conditioning and social contact protected people.[92]

**What helps.** Agree who checks on whom, especially older neighbours and anyone who lives alone.[14, 26] In heat, go where there is air conditioning. Dial 2-1-1 to find one.[14] Cover windows with drapes or shades.[16] In cold, close off rooms you don't need, stuff towels under doors, and cover windows with blankets at night. Have extra blankets, sleeping bags and warm coats.[26] Babies under one year old should never sleep in a cold room.[26]

**What to avoid.** Fans move air but do not lower body temperature.[16] Above 90°F indoors, a fan can make you hotter.[14] Never heat your home with a gas oven, and never use a grill or camp stove indoors.[58] Keep space heaters 3 feet from anything that can burn.[93] Never leave people or pets in a closed car on a warm day.[16]

**Your numbers.** Being ready for 3 days leaves about 6 (4–20) of 100 households like yours facing a longer spell of dangerous heat or cold in the next 10 years. This number mostly depends on how often windstorms happen where you live (expert estimate) and how long power cuts from a windstorm bigger than any in the recent records last (expert estimate). Your heating needs electricity to run (a gas furnace needs it for the igniter and fan), so a long winter power cut also cuts your heat. A power cut during a heat wave also stops your air conditioning.[12, 13, 15, 18, 51, 52, 53]

**What counts toward it:**

- 2 battery fans: 1 for the home plus 1 for the 1 person most at risk from heat (65 and over, babies, pregnancy). Fans help only while it is below 90 °F indoors; above that, go to a cooling centre. (CDC; some amounts are estimates)[14, 59]

### Medical emergency when help is slow

Households like yours average about 2 emergency-room visits a year (national figures).[2, 3, 13] Emergency departments see about 47 visits for every 100 people each year.[2] Ambulances take longer to reach rural homes.[3] After a disaster, emergency medical services are in high demand and help may be delayed.[120]

### House fire

Of 100 households like yours, about 3 (2–3) will have a home fire at least once in the next 10 years.[42] There were about 345,000 home fires in the United States in 2023.[42] Cooking is by far the leading cause of home fires and home fire injuries.[126]

**What helps.** Put smoke alarms inside and outside sleeping areas and on every level of your home. **What to avoid.** If a person or pet is still inside, get out, call 911, and tell firefighters where they are.[128] Never heat your home with an oven.[93] Turn off space heaters when you leave the room or go to bed.[93] Replace 9-volt alarm batteries at least once a year.[127]

**What counts toward it:**

- A fire escape plan: two ways out of every room, a meeting spot outside, and a practice run with everyone. If anyone sleeps upstairs, check that each bedroom has a second way out, such as a window onto a porch roof or an escape ladder. Test your smoke alarms every month. (Ready.gov)[128]

### Extras for the hazards you face

These help with one hazard rather than a whole need, so they sit outside the budget. Consider them once the steps above are done.

- **Plastic sheeting and duct tape to shelter in place** (usually $43–70 per kit): Thick plastic sheeting and duct tape to seal a room's windows, doors and vents if officials tell you to shelter in place from a chemical release (Ready.gov). Cut the sheets several inches wider than each opening ahead of time and label them.[57, 60, 149, 150]
- **Personal radiation dosimeter card** (usually $25 per dosimeter): A card that changes color to show how much radiation it has absorbed. It is optional: in a radiation emergency, official instructions on when to shelter and when to leave matter far more than your own reading.[46, 60]
- **Shielded (Faraday) bag for spare electronics** (usually $38–98 per bag): A metal-lined bag for a spare radio, phone or power bank. The effects of an electromagnetic pulse on home electronics are highly uncertain, so this is optional and never a priority; a battery radio and general supplies are the main protection.[44, 50, 60]
- **Warm sleeping bag or heavy wool blanket for each person** (usually $25–56 per person): A sleeping bag rated for your coldest nights, or a heavy wool blanket, for each person, as Ready.gov lists in its basic kit. With a warm room plan this covers a winter outage when the heating needs electricity.[26, 57, 60]
- **Indoor room thermometer** (usually $2–15 per thermometer): A simple room thermometer, so you know when it is too hot for a fan to help (90°F) or too cold for an infant to sleep, and when to leave for a cooling center or warming shelter (CDC).[14, 26, 60]

### Contacts and meeting places

- **Make a family emergency plan.** Agree how you will reach each other, where you will meet if you are separated, and who picks up the children (Ready.gov). Include each person's medical needs, pets and school or work plans, then practice it.[110, 151]
- **Choose an out-of-area contact everyone can text.** Pick one relative or friend in another state whom everyone in the household will text to say they are safe (Ready.gov). Texts often get through when calls do not, and they use less battery.[102, 110]
- **Written contact card for each person.** Write key phone numbers on a card for each person's wallet or bag: family, the out-of-area contact, work, school, doctor and 988. Ready.gov advises storing important numbers somewhere besides your phone, and offers a fillable card.[37, 105, 151]
- **Turn on emergency alerts on every phone.** Check that Wireless Emergency Alerts are switched on in each phone's settings. They are free and need no sign-up (Ready.gov), but a phone only gets them when it is on, not in airplane mode, and served by a carrier that takes part (FCC).[103, 152]
- **Sign up for your county's emergency alerts.** Sign up for your county or city's emergency text and email alerts, which carry local warnings such as evacuation orders and boil-water notices. Ready.gov's planning questions start with how you will receive alerts and warnings.[103, 110]
- **Know how to reach 911 by call or text.** Always call 911 when you can, because a voice call gives the call center more information; text-to-911 works only in some places (FCC). Find out whether your county accepts texts, which helps when speaking is unsafe or impossible.[8]

### Leaving home: triggers and routes

About 6 of 100 households like yours have to leave home quickly at least once in 10 years. Warning can be 1 minute to 12 hours ahead. Plan to be away for about 2 days.[12, 13, 38, 41, 42]

- **Know your evacuation zone, routes and where you would go.** Many states set evacuation zones and routes; look up yours on your state and county emergency management websites (Ready.gov). Pick where you would go: a friend's or relative's home out of the area, a pet-friendly hotel, or a public shelter.[24, 81, 86, 153]
- **Agree if-then triggers for leaving.** Write short promises such as 'If the county issues an evacuation warning for our zone, we leave within 30 minutes; the bag is by the door; Sam gets the kids.' Plans in if-then form make people much more likely to follow through.[113, 154]
- **Write a go-or-stay card for each likely hazard.** For each hazard your plan names, write one card that says when you leave, when you stay and shelter, and what you do first. People who get complete warnings spend less time checking with others before acting, and a card decided in advance works the same way.[114, 154]
- **Run ten-minute drills twice a year.** Practice a fire escape, grabbing the go-bag against a timer, or Drop, Cover and Hold On, for ten minutes at a time. Ready.gov advises practicing your home fire escape plan twice a year, and people who have drilled act faster and cope better in real emergencies.[123, 128, 155]
- **Keep the gas tank at least half full.** Ready.gov advises keeping at least half a tank of gas at all times in case you have to leave suddenly, and a full tank when an evacuation looks likely. Gas stations close or run dry when the power is out.[23, 24]
- **Never walk or drive through floodwater.** Just six inches of moving water can knock you down and one foot can sweep a vehicle away (Ready.gov). Turn around and find another way, and move to higher ground or a higher floor if water rises.[29, 115]
- **Pick your tornado shelter spot.** Choose where you will go when a tornado warning sounds: a safe room or storm shelter if you have one, otherwise a small, interior, windowless room or basement on the lowest floor of a sturdy building (Ready.gov). If you live in a mobile home, plan which sturdy building you would go to instead.[20]

#### Drills and if-then plans

Supplies help most when you already know what to do with them. Practice and simple rules made in advance turn a plan into action.

**If-then plans.** An if-then plan links a trigger to an action: "If the county issues an evacuation warning for our zone, we leave within 30 minutes." Plans written this way help people follow through.[113] When a warning comes, people often wait to confirm it with others before they act.[114] A trigger you agreed on earlier leaves less to decide in the moment. Write one for each likely hazard, and add who picks up the children and where you will meet.[110]

**Drills.** In New Zealand, people who took part in earthquake drills knew the right actions better, used them more during real shaking, and did more to prepare.[155] Among people who got out of the World Trade Center on 9/11, those with earlier emergency training were less likely to develop PTSD.[123] Practice your fire escape plan twice a year.[128] Other ten-minute drills: Drop, Cover and Hold On, a night without power, grabbing the go-bag against a timer, and sealing a room to shelter in place.[154]

**What to avoid.** Do not skip drills because they feel silly. Each one you finish counts toward your plan.

### School, work and getting home

- **Plan and walk part of your route home.** Map two walking routes from work to home and walk part of one. Plan on about 2 to 2.4 miles an hour with a loaded bag, and about 24 to 32 ounces of water an hour in heat, so a 12-mile walk takes five to six hours.[62, 117, 118]
- **Know your workplace's emergency plan.** Ask how your workplace would shelter people if roads closed; Ready.gov's work-kit advice is to be ready to shelter at work for at least 24 hours. Keep your medicines, walking shoes and a phone charger there.[116]
- **Know your children's school or daycare emergency plan.** Find out how the school or daycare would shelter or move children and how it contacts parents, and list who else may pick your children up. Ready.gov asks families to include school and daycare in their plans.[110, 156]
- **Check the spare tire and jack.** Find the spare tire, jack and wrench, and check the spare's pressure when you check the others. A flat tire is a common way to be stranded, and it is easier to learn the steps on a dry day.[25, 50]

#### Talking with children about emergencies

Children cope better when they understand what is happening and what they can do to help keep themselves and their family safe.[157] Everyone can help prepare, including young children and teens.[158]

**Before anything happens.** Let children help build the kit and practice the plan.[158] Make sure each child knows your family meeting place and has a contact card.[110] Practice the fire escape plan together twice a year.[128]

**When something happens.** Start by asking what they already know and what questions they have. Give simple, concrete facts without scary details. For a young child, that might be: "A tree branch fell on the power lines, so the lights are off." Tell them about the many people working to help, and the steps being taken to keep them safe.[157]

**What to avoid.** Limit news coverage. If children watch, watch with them and answer their questions.[157] Reassure them when you honestly can, but do not give false reassurance. Let them know it is all right to be upset. If you feel overwhelmed, get support from another adult first. Helping others in the community can ease a child's sense of helplessness.[157]

### Neighbors

- **Get to know two neighbours and swap phone numbers.** Introduce yourself to at least two neighbours and save each other's numbers. After the 2011 Japan tsunami, places with stronger social ties had fewer deaths, especially among older and low-income people. About 7 in 10 people expect help from friends and family first (FEMA's 2024 survey).[142, 159, 160]
- **Agree who checks on whom.** Agree with neighbours or relatives who checks on whom during heat waves, storms and outages, especially older people who live alone. In the 1995 Chicago heat wave, living alone and not leaving home each day made death far more likely, and regular social contact was protective.[14, 92, 161]
- **Make a simple street contact list.** With willing neighbours, list names, phone numbers and anything useful in an emergency: who needs help to evacuate, who has a generator or medical skills, and who has pets. State programs such as Listos California offer templates for exactly this.[161, 162]
- **Join or start a block group, or take CERT training.** Join a neighbourhood preparedness group or take free Community Emergency Response Team (CERT) training, which teaches fire safety, light search and rescue, team organization and disaster first aid (Ready.gov). Trained neighbours can help until responders arrive.[121, 123, 162]

#### Neighbours and mutual aid

Neighbours are a supply, and the plan counts them. After Japan's 2011 tsunami, towns with stronger community ties had lower death rates.[159] In neighbourhoods flooded by the same tsunami, strong ties mattered most for older and lower-income people.[160] In the 1995 Chicago heat wave, living alone raised the risk of dying.[92]

**The gap.** About 7 in 10 people expect help from friends and family in a disaster, but fewer than 2 in 10 had heard how to help their neighbours.[142]

**What helps.** Swap phone numbers with two neighbours. Agree who checks on whom, especially older neighbours and anyone who lives alone. Make a simple contact list for your street. Give a trusted person a spare key, and tell them where your supplies are and how to use any medical equipment.[161] Host a block party, since strong communities start with prepared neighbours.[162] Take CERT training to learn fire safety, light search and rescue, and disaster first aid.[121]

**What to avoid.** Do not wait for a disaster to meet the people next door. A short hello now makes it easier to knock on a door later.

### Pets

- **Pet plan: microchip, records, a photo and a buddy.** Keep your pet's ID tag and microchip details current, put vaccination records and a recent photo with your documents, and arrange a buddy who can care for or evacuate your pets if you cannot (Ready.gov). If officials ask you to evacuate, your pets should go too.[81, 86]
- **Plan for horses and livestock.** Plan to evacuate large animals early, set up a buddy system with nearby farms to share trailers and pasture, and keep vet records handy (ASPCA). Large animals need about 20 to 30 liters of water a day each, so fill tubs before the power goes out (Sphere).[66, 81]

## Documents and money

Keep paper copies in a waterproof pouch and photos you can reach from any phone. FEMA's Emergency Financial First Aid Kit groups them in four parts.[37]

- [ ] **Who you are:** photo IDs, birth certificates, Social Security cards, passports, and pet records.
- [ ] **Money and legal papers:** insurance policies, the lease or deed, bank and card contact numbers (not PINs), and recent tax returns.
- [ ] **Medical papers:** insurance cards, the written medicine list, prescriptions, and vaccination records.
- [ ] **Contacts:** family, doctors, the insurance agent, the landlord or lender, and employers.

- **Copy and protect your key documents.** Use FEMA's Emergency Financial First Aid Kit to gather identification, financial and legal papers, insurance, medical information and contacts. Keep phone photos, paper copies in a waterproof bag, and a copy with someone you trust or in secure online storage.[37, 57, 106]
- **Photograph or video your home and belongings.** Walk through your home recording each room, the contents of closets and drawers, and serial numbers of costly items, as the first step of FEMA's financial kit. It makes insurance and aid claims faster after a fire or flood.[37]
- **Fire-resistant, waterproof document pouch.** A fire-resistant, water-resistant pouch or small case for paper copies of key documents, a USB drive and some cash, easy to grab when you leave (FEMA's financial kit suggests fire- and waterproof storage).[37, 60]

### Insurance questions

- Home and renters policies usually don't cover floods. Decide on flood insurance before you need it: a new policy usually starts 30 days after you buy it. It pays up to $250,000 for the building and $100,000 for belongings. (FEMA, FloodSmart, Ready.gov)[106, 143, 144]

- **Review your insurance: home or renters, flood, earthquake.** Check what your policy covers and decide on the gaps: standard home and renters policies do not cover flood or earthquake damage (Ready.gov), and a new federal flood policy usually takes 30 days to start (FEMA). Renters can buy contents-only flood cover.[102, 106, 115, 143, 144]

### Cash

- Cash in small bills, kept with your documents, because ATMs and cards may not work in an outage: about $100 to start, or enough for about 3 days of basics (food, fuel, medicine) at your own daily spending. No agency gives a dollar amount. (Ready.gov, FEMA; some amounts are estimates)[13, 18, 37, 59, 106]

### Savings

Of 100 households like yours, about 20 (10–45) will have an income gap of more than 3 months in the next 10 years. The goal is about 6 months of expenses (about $31,200); you have 1 month saved. Your supplies plan is done by month 38. After that, your $80 a month for supplies could go here, reaching the goal in about 27 years. This is a savings goal, kept separate from the supplies budget.

- **Start an emergency fund, even a small one.** Set up an automatic transfer, however small, into a savings account you keep for emergencies. The CFPB says the right amount depends on your situation; planners often suggest working toward three to six months of expenses (FINRA), and the plan shows a target for your household.[34, 35, 139, 140]
- **Know how to file for unemployment in your state.** Find your state's unemployment insurance website and what it needs from you, so you can file on the first day if a job ends (Department of Labor). Keep pay stubs and employer details with your documents.[30, 36]
- **Write a bare-bones budget you could switch to.** List the spending you could cut first if income stopped, and what your essential costs are each month. Knowing that number tells you how far your savings would stretch and sets your emergency-fund goal.[34, 50]

## Special needs

### Medicine

- 1 person carries an epinephrine auto-injector: check the expiry date, and ask the prescriber how many to keep at home, in the go-bag and on the person. (Ready.gov)[7]

- **Written medicine list for each person.** A paper list of each person's medicines with the dose their prescriber set, what each one is for, allergies, and the prescriber's and pharmacy's phone numbers. Keep one copy in the go-bag and one with your documents (Ready.gov).[7, 37]
- **Set up telehealth and pharmacy accounts now.** Create your logins for your insurer's or clinic's telehealth service and your pharmacy's app or website while it is easy. In an emergency you can then reach your own clinician and move a prescription without traveling.[9, 62]

### Antibiotics

- No antibiotics are included in this plan. If you have a specific, foreseeable need, such as remote travel, talk to your own clinician about a standby prescription with written instructions. Never use fish or pet antibiotics, never share prescriptions, and don't use expired ones. (CDC, FDA)[10, 11, 100, 101]

#### Antibiotics and emergency antibiotic kits

Some companies sell "emergency antibiotic kits" for disaster preparedness. Ready Reckoner never sets an antibiotic quantity. Here is why, and what to do instead.

**What the kits are.** By federal law, antibiotics for people need a prescription.[163] The kits are sold through an online questionnaire that a prescriber signs off, and they typically cost $300 to $400. A 2026 medical review found that this model prescribes for an illness no one has diagnosed. That invites the wrong drug, the wrong dose and the wrong length of treatment. It can also cause side effects and resistant infections.[9]

**When a standby prescription can make sense.** CDC notes that travelers often carry some prescribed medicines for specific risks where they are going.[101] The same review suggests telehealth with a clinician who knows you, and keeping prescriptions made in advance for specific high-risk cases.[9] If a specific situation worries you, ask your own clinician.

**What to avoid.** Do not save antibiotics for later, share them, or take someone else's.[10] Do not use expired antibiotics. They can fail to treat an infection.[11] Never use aquarium, bird or other animal antibiotics. The FDA calls these products unapproved and unsafe.[100]

- Baby wipes: at least 2 packs for each child in diapers for up to 2 weeks: 2 packs. (CDC)[13, 18, 39, 40, 82, 88]
- 1 oral thermometer (non-mercury, non-glass; it is on the Red Cross kit list). (Red Cross)[6]

### Pregnancy and nursing

- **Plan for pregnancy and nursing.** CDC advises people who are pregnant or nursing to keep at least 7 to 10 days of prescriptions and ask about a 30-day emergency refill, keep copies of prenatal records, and avoid water treated with iodine. ### Pets and animals

- 12 large animals × 25 L a day (20 to 30) × 60 days = 18,000 L, about 4,755.1 gallons. Fill tubs or stock tanks before a storm or an outage. (Sphere, ASPCA)[12, 13, 66, 81]
- Dry food for the 2 dogs and the 3 cats for 10 days (at least a week): about 0.7 lb a day for each dog and 0.15 lb a day for each cat = 18.5 lb. - 5 pet carriers, one for each pet, with a pet go-kit: water and food from your home supplies, any medicine each takes, records and a photo. Public shelters may take only service animals, so find pet-friendly places to stay ahead of time. (ASPCA, Ready.gov)[24, 81]

#### Pets and service animals

If local officials ask you to evacuate, your pets should evacuate too.[86] Most public shelters take only service animals, so plan where your pets would stay.[24]

**What helps.** Find pet-friendly places to stay ahead of time, such as a friend's home or a pet-friendly motel.[24, 81] Pack a pet go-kit: food in an airtight, waterproof container, a water bowl and several days of water, extra medicine, and litter or bags.[86] The ASPCA suggests 7 to 10 days of food, at least 7 days of water, a two-week supply of medicine, a carrier for each pet, records and recent photos.[81] A dog drinks about 1 ounce of water per pound of body weight each day.[73] The plan sizes cat water from a standard formula for animals' daily fluid needs.[74] Put a rescue-alert sticker on your home so rescuers know pets are inside.[81] The ASPCA also recommends microchipping your pets.[81]

**What to avoid.** Do not leave pets behind, even for what seems like a short evacuation. Plan as if you might be away for weeks.[81] Never leave a pet in a closed car on a warm day.[16]

### Stress and mental health

#### Stress, mental health and the 988 line

Stress before, during and after a disaster is normal. In FEMA's 2024 survey, the most common worries were health and paying bills, well ahead of disasters.[142] Preparation helps with more than supplies. Among people who got out of the World Trade Center on 9/11, those with earlier emergency training were less likely to develop PTSD.[123]

**What helps.** Save 988 in every phone. It offers free, judgment-free support 24/7 for mental health, substance use and more, by call, text or chat.[122] After a disaster, the Disaster Distress Helpline offers crisis counseling 24/7 to anyone in emotional distress. Call or text 1-800-985-5990.[165] Write a short calm plan: what you might feel, what helps you, and who you will call.[154] Oregon's 2 Weeks Ready program even includes practicing mindfulness as a step in getting prepared.[120]

**What to avoid.** Do not keep the news on all day. No one, adult or child, benefits from graphic details or disturbing images.[157] Do not wait until things feel unbearable to reach out. The 988 line supports people in emotional distress as well as in crisis.[122]

#### Rotating supplies through everyday use

The easiest supplies to keep fresh are the ones you use. Store the foods your household already eats, use the oldest first, and replace them as you shop.[83, 148]

**What to check, and when.** Replace water you bottled yourself every six months.[65] Look through your pantry every few weeks and use cans you have had for a while.[83] Dates on most foods are about quality, not safety. Infant formula is an exception, and its date must be followed.[83] Test smoke alarms every month, and replace 9-volt batteries at least once a year.[127] Check first-aid supplies for used or expired items.[6] Update your documents at tax time, when the clocks change, on your birthday or at the new year.[37] Rotate pet water and food in the go-kit every two months.[81]

**Make it a habit.** Tie checks to things you already do, like grocery trips or the clock change. New habits take time. In one study, they took from 18 to 254 days to become automatic, and missing a day now and then did not set people back.[167] One missed month is fine.

**What to avoid.** Do not store food in a hot garage or attic. Heat cuts shelf life sharply.[168]
